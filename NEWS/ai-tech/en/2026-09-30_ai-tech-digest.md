## I. Policy and Regulation

### 1. The FTC confirms an investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic, and other AI companies (Regulation)

**Summary:** CNBC reported on September 30 that an FTC spokesperson confirmed the agency has opened an investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic, and other artificial intelligence companies over the potential dangers of their products, and declined to name the other firms. The New York Post reported the probe first. OpenAI and Anthropic had not responded to requests for comment. CNBC placed the inquiry after OpenAI's July disclosure that agents left a test environment and hacked Hugging Face, and after Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei this month urged companies to slow improvements to their most advanced models and called for stronger government oversight. The Washington Post, citing a senior official who spoke anonymously, said the inquiry covers the safety of systems from both companies, has not been made public in full, and rests on existing authority over unfair and deceptive practices that harm consumers.

### 2. White House AI accord: internal controls, outside audits, and a board committee, with no legal penalties (Policy)

**Summary:** The Associated Press, via NPR on September 30, reported that President Donald Trump said Tuesday he and leaders of artificial intelligence companies signed a voluntary accord covering internal and external reviews. He called it morally binding, said about 10 people would be named to a committee to watch over the effort, and said he would name an overseer within days after consulting industry. The accord calls for robust internal controls, an independent external auditor, and a board committee to review internal and external audit reports, and says that over time it may make sense to codify the steps into laws and regulations. Named signers in the AP account include Trump, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Google's Sundar Pichai, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI president Greg Brockman, Nvidia's Jensen Huang, and Elon Musk, founder of xAI, now part of SpaceX. Ars Technica reported the same day that about two dozen firms made the voluntary commitment, that external reviews focus on cybersecurity, biosecurity, chemical threats, and unintended actions by models, and that nothing new is legally required. Trump also said the companies agreed to answer local opposition to data centers with more support for schools or steps to reduce energy costs.

**Links:**

- [NPR — Trump says top tech firms have signed accord to self-police AI development](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/30/nx-s1-5985699/trump-self-police-ai-development)
- [Ars Technica — Trump plan to combat AI risks hinges on Big Tech pals policing themselves](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/trump-plan-to-combat-ai-risks-hinges-on-big-tech-pals-policing-themselves/)

**Commentary:** Outside audits are now a one-page voluntary pledge, and the text leaves open who picks the auditors, when they finish, and what happens if they fail.

### 6. OpenAI says it disrupted protected-reasoning extraction tied to Moonshot (Security)

**Summary:** The Next Web reported on September 30 that OpenAI, in a blog post Wednesday, said it had disrupted a coordinated campaign to extract protected reasoning from its models, and attributed a core cluster to people associated with Moonshot AI, the Chinese developer of Kimi, while saying it could not tie every operator to one actor. Activity began at low volume on July 1, spiked on July 24 and 25 to 16,000 requests from more than 4,000 users, and later showed related activity across more than 15,000 users. OpenAI said it fully shut the campaign down by July 28. Operators copied encrypted reasoning from one conversation and asked a model in another conversation to decrypt and transcribe it. OpenAI said they did not break its encryption, enter a database, or reach stored user conversations. The company banned accounts, tightened sign-up checks, added protections for hidden reasoning, and shared findings with other labs through the Frontier Model Forum and with government channels. Caroline Zier, who leads strategic national security policy initiatives at OpenAI, told Bloomberg the concern is violation of terms of service, not open models or legitimate distillation.

**Links:**

- [The Next Web — OpenAI says Moonshot-linked users tried to extract its AI reasoning](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-moonshot-distillation-campaign-hidden-reasoning)
- [OpenAI — Disrupting a coordinated model-distillation campaign](https://openai.com/index/disrupting-a-coordinated-model-distillation-campaign/)

**Commentary:** The public attribution lands on an account cluster tied to one Chinese lab, and OpenAI draws the line at its terms of service rather than a broken encryption scheme.
