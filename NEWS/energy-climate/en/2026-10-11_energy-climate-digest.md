### 1. Hurricane Simon remains Category 4 at noon, tracking north along Mexico's west coast (Hurricane)
**Summary:** The National Hurricane Center's intermediate advisory at noon Central Time on Sunday placed Simon's center near 19.5N, 105.5W, about 85 miles (140 km) west-northwest of Manzanillo and 65 miles (100 km) south-southeast of Cabo Corrientes, moving north-northwest at 7 mph. Additional rainfall of 8 to 16 inches was expected, with isolated storm totals up to 30 inches in parts of Michoacan, Colima, and Jalisco, and a risk of life-threatening flooding and mudslides.

### 3. A dawn flash flood on Nepal's Budhi Gandaki damages a 341 MW hydropower site (Flood)
**Summary:** A preliminary assessment published Sunday by the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development said the flash flood in Nepal's Budhi Gandaki corridor was likely triggered by the failure of a temporary landslide dam near Chumchet in Gorkha district, with the flood starting around 4 a.m. Nepal time on October 11 and carrying mud and debris downstream. Kamala Duwadi, a resident of Arughat, told the Asian News International that police ordered an evacuation around 4:30 a.m. and that she and workers upstream moved to higher ground. Initial reports said people were missing, houses had been washed away, and bridges and riverside infrastructure were damaged. Damage was also reported at the 341 MW Budhi Gandaki hydropower site at Selleri, with the full extent still being assessed. ICIMOD noted that an April 2024 flood in the same valley, caused by an ice avalanche into Birendra Tal, was a different mechanism, and that both events show why slope, river, and downstream power sites have to be watched together.

## II. Oil, Gas, and Energy Security

### 4. Gulf oil shut-ins fall to about 1.17 million barrels a day, still 57.5 percent of output (Oil and gas)
**Summary:** The U.S. Marine Minerals Administration said on October 11 that, from reports by 31 companies as of 11:00 a.m. Central Time, an estimated 1,173,863 barrels a day of Gulf oil was shut in, 57.54 percent of that day's oil production. Shut-in gas was 1,051 million cubic feet a day, 49.11 percent. Personnel had been evacuated from 32 of 371 manned platforms, 8.63 percent; from 1 of 11 non-dynamically positioned rigs; and from none of 17 dynamically positioned rigs. The agency said facilities would be inspected after the storm, undamaged ones would return immediately after standard checks, and damaged ones would take longer. Compared with the agency's Saturday morning bulletin of about 1.403 million barrels a day, 68.76 percent, and 113 evacuated platforms, crews and output are coming back, and more than half the oil is still off.

### 5. The Energy Department on Saturday authorized an exchange of up to 4 million barrels, to be returned with a premium in 2027 (Petroleum reserve)
**Summary:** On October 10 the U.S. Department of Energy authorized an emergency exchange of up to 4 million barrels of crude from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve to address supply disruptions from severe weather in the Gulf. ExxonMobil and BP America may each take up to 2 million barrels, and participating companies must return the borrowed crude plus additional premium barrels in 2027. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said the exchange is meant to help keep refineries running and limit fuel-supply disruptions, and that the two companies will return more barrels than are withdrawn. The department also said ExxonMobil's exchange will accelerate deliveries of previously awarded reserve crude. AFP reported Sunday that the reserve had already been drawn since the U.S. war with Iran, leaving stocks at their lowest since 1982.

## III. Policy and Climate Accountability

### 7. The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act puts transmission and oil and gas pipelines on one faster permit track (Permitting)
**Summary:** Fortune reported Sunday that the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, drafted by Senators Mike Lee, Sheldon Whitehouse, Martin Heinrich, and Shelley Moore Capito, would shorten permitting timelines for solar farms, power lines, and oil and gas pipelines, make it harder for a president to revoke permits, narrow environmental reviews, and limit litigation to federal courts. Ben Norris, senior vice president of regulatory affairs at the Solar Energy Industries Association, told Fortune that the clean energy and transmission the text would unlock is worth the tradeoffs elsewhere and would put downward pressure on prices. Earthjustice Action president Abigail Dillen said the deal would fast-track gas, close courthouse doors, and dismantle baseline protections for water and endangered species. EQT chief executive Toby Rice pointed to the company's 303-mile Mountain Valley Pipeline, which took a decade and an act of Congress, and argued for a technology-neutral approach. Fortune said votes are unlikely to begin before the November 3 midterm elections.

### 8. AFP: courts keep taking emissions cases as governments scale climate policy back (Litigation)
**Summary:** AFP reported Sunday that Dennis van Berkel of the Climate Litigation Network sees continued momentum for climate cases, and that earlier this week Australia's highest court blocked a coal-mine expansion in New South Wales over its climate impact. Margaret Young, a law professor at the University of Melbourne, said climate litigation is growing at home and abroad. The report said governments from Europe to the United States are scaling back climate policy and that the November COP is not expected to deliver major progress on cutting fossil fuels. Over the past two years the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, and the International Court of Justice have said in advisory opinions that states have legal duties to address climate change, and an opinion from the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights is still expected. A Paris court in June cited the ICJ opinion and ordered TotalEnergies to include emissions from the use of its products in its vigilance plan; a Dutch court in January ordered the Netherlands to strengthen its climate plan. Both rulings are on appeal. Pending cases include Philippine victims of Typhoon Odette suing Shell in the United Kingdom, residents of Indonesia's Pari island suing Holcim in Switzerland, and Pakistani farmers suing RWE and Heidelberg Materials in Germany.

**Links:**

## IV. Green Power and Carbon Markets

### 10. Nigeria wants local solar manufacturing in place before it considers import limits (Solar)
**Summary:** The Voice of Nigeria reported Sunday that Kingsley Udeh, minister of innovation, science and technology, said the government is talking with international manufacturers, including companies in Turkey, to expand domestic solar-panel production and quality control, and that it does not intend to restrict imports until local capacity is sufficient. Michael David, executive director of the Global Initiative for Food Security and Ecosystem Preservation, asked for a time-bound renewable industrialization roadmap, affordable manufacturing finance, and enforceable quality standards.

### 11. The third-quarter CBAM certificate price is €82.32 a tonne, and default power charges in the Western Balkans land near €73 to €94 per MWh (Carbon market)
**Summary:** CTX SEE calculated on Sunday that the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism certificate price for the third quarter is €82.32 per tonne of CO2, up from €75.28 in the second quarter. Using national default emission factors, the gross exposure is about €85.70 per MWh for Serbian electricity, €80.59 for Montenegro, €94.50 for Bosnia and Herzegovina, and €73.01 for North Macedonia. Albania's zero default factor produces no default carbon charge. The article treats the figures as illustrative, not final charges for October deliveries. The fourth-quarter price will be set in January 2027, and carbon prices already paid in the country of origin can be deducted. On October 9 Serbia's day-ahead average rose to €239.96 per MWh and Hungary's to €269.43, a spread of €29.47, still well below Serbia's default carbon exposure. Energy Community Secretariat data show gross commercial exchange between the Western Balkans and neighboring EU markets fell about 19 percent year on year in the first half of 2026. Wind or solar exported into the EU is priced on actual emissions rather than the national default only with a qualifying physical power-purchase agreement, cross-border nominations, hourly matching, and review by an accredited verifier. Guarantees of origin do not replace that evidence. The first certificates for 2026 imports are purchased from February 2027, and the annual declaration and surrender deadline is September 30, 2027.

**Links:**

- [CTX SEE — The CBAM certificate price raises carbon-adjusted electricity costs for Western Balkans exports](https://ctxsee.eu/cbam-certificate-price-raises-carbon-adjusted-electricity-costs-for-western-balkans-exports/)

**Commentary:** The default factor prices a country's whole power mix in one number, so a low-carbon plant needs hour-by-hour physical delivery evidence to enter the EU market on its own emissions.
