### 2. UK tables Energy Independence Bill: ends new North Sea exploration licences, mandates community benefits and advances grid reform (Policy)
**Summary:** On November 4 the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero introduced the Energy Independence Bill. It delivers the manifesto pledge to issue no new licences to explore new North Sea oil and gas fields, while Transitional Energy Certificates support continued production from existing licensed areas and nearby already-explored acreage—officials say that could unlock around 60 million barrels and support hundreds of jobs—and ends new onshore licences in England that could enable shale fracking. On bills, the government plans to scrap household gas standing charges for about 23 million homes, with roughly 60% of gas accounts expected to save about £45 a year on average, and to put the shift of 75% of domestic Renewables Obligation costs onto public spending on a lasting footing through March 2029. Clean-power measures require binding community benefit agreements for new onshore wind and ground-mount solar above 5 MW (minded-to floors of £5,000/MW/year for wind and £700/MW/year for solar), pathways for communities to take up to a 20% stake, faster grid connections, and tougher Ofgem powers against rogue suppliers and brokers.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** Ban new licences while shielding legacy barrels with “transition certificates”—classic British compromise that writes a climate signal into the statute and leaves production politics to existing infrastructure.

### 4. China’s Renewable Energy Law revision draft goes to first reading: unified power market, offtake duties and green certificates enter statute (China)
**Summary:** Xinhua’s November 4 brief reiterated that on November 2 the Renewable Energy Law revision draft was submitted for first review by the 25th session of the 14th NPC Standing Committee—ten chapters and 76 articles. The draft directs the state to build a unified national electricity market, adapt market rules to renewable characteristics, push renewables into trading in an orderly way, and create a sustainable pricing settlement mechanism to protect reasonable returns; it also hardens a mix of mandatory and voluntary consumption promotion, renewable offtake responsibility weights and a green electricity certificate system. Legislative purposes add high-quality development, building an energy powerhouse and carbon peaking/neutrality; ambient heat and hydrogen are brought into scope, with stronger rules on development order, new-type grids and flexibility, equipment recycling and supply-chain security. January–September market-traded power already reached 5,853.6 TWh, up 18.9% year on year, with more than half of new-energy volumes market-priced—the factual backdrop for the rewrite.

## II. Clean Power, Storage & Grids

### 5. Lithuanian parliament passes renewables acceleration package: projects may extend up to 48 months, performance guarantees halved (Clean power)
**Summary:** pv magazine reported on November 4 that Lithuania’s Seimas adopted an Energy Ministry package amending the Laws on Energy, Electricity and Energy from Renewable Sources after a sharp slowdown in new projects. Developers with valid permits get a limited window to extend timelines by up to 48 months and switch among generation, storage and consumption projects, with an extra bank guarantee required; performance guarantees owed to network operators fall from €50/kW to €25/kW. From November 1, 2026, energy communities of up to 100 kW under the prosumer-park model need no permits; large investors may tap operator equipment reserves to speed connections, and TSO/DSOs must stock critical restoration materials after accidents or physical interference. The package also clarifies cross-border electricity cybersecurity roles and adds penalties for methane-rule and Russian-gas import breaches.

**Links:**

- [pv magazine — Lithuania moves to revive renewables growth with sweeping reforms](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/11/04/lithuania-moves-to-revive-renewables-growth-with-sweeping-reforms/)

**Commentary:** When projects stall on permits and collateral, a small Baltic state chooses lower friction plus security hardening—the bottleneck has shifted from willingness to build to execution liquidity.

### 7. Siemens study: European cities face ~€24.7 billion EV distribution-grid bill; smart charging could cut about 40% (Grids)
**Summary:** A Siemens study for ChargeUp Europe, ACEA and EIT Urban Mobility—first reported by Reuters on November 3 and widely cited on November 4—models 64 cities across the EU27 plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway. By 2030, passenger and light-commercial EVs grow about 3.8× on average, implying roughly €24.7 billion in distribution-grid investment; smart meters and load management that shift charging into troughs could save about €10.6 billion (~40%), bringing needs toward the mid-teens of billions of euros. Low-voltage networks serving home and workplace slow charging dominate the bill; Germany has the largest need, followed by Norway, Belgium and the Netherlands, while eastern Europe grows fastest from a lower base. Predictable off-peak tariffs proposed by the European Commission could help consumers shift charging times.

### 9. Five Chinese ministries’ Product Carbon Footprint Standards Action Plan (2026–2028) keeps circulating: 100+ national standards to meet trade rules (Carbon standards)
**Summary:** Industry media on November 4 continued covering the Action Plan on Building a Key Product Carbon Footprint Standards System (2026–2028) jointly issued by SAMR, NDRC, MEE, MIIT and NEA. On a “urgent first” logic it targets energy, raw materials, consumer goods, machinery and transport equipment, aiming to issue 100-plus product carbon-footprint national standards—some developed in parallel with international norms—covering key primary energy and materials plus intermediate products with strong supply-chain pull, plus national carbon-emission standardisation pilots. Energy chapters schedule coal, power (including wind, solar, hydro, nuclear, T&D and storage), and gaseous and liquid fuels; autos, ships, steel, non-ferrous metals, chemicals and building materials also appear on the list. Officials frame the package as helping industries respond to shifting international trade rules.

## IV. Climate Risk & Financial Stability

### 11. ECB’s Lagarde: this summer’s heat was a warning—push EU climate reinsurance plus grids and storage (Climate finance)
**Summary:** In a November 4 ECB blog, President Christine Lagarde wrote that Europe’s heatwaves, droughts and wildfires caused over 30,000 excess deaths, burned about 400,000 hectares in Spain and France, and cut Rhine and Danube levels enough to disrupt shipping and nuclear cooling. ECB estimates put activity in hit regions 1.5% lower two years after a heatwave and 3% lower four years after a drought; historically only about a quarter of European catastrophe losses have been insured. She argued Middle East-driven gas spikes interacted with heat—higher AC demand, weaker hydro, constrained nuclear—while evening prices tightened as solar dropped; solar was the rare major source that outperformed during the heat. Priorities include an EU-level public-private climate reinsurance scheme floated with EIOPA, grid upgrades that save more than €2 in system costs per euro invested, common rules for behind-the-meter firm supply to support the grid, and using next week’s COP31 to turn ambition into action.
