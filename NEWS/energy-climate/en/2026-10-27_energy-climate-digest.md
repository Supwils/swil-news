## I. Policy & Carbon Markets

### 1. EU proposes Climate Resilience Law requiring plans for 2°C/3°C warming (adaptation)
**Summary:** The European Commission on Tuesday unveiled its first law aimed at boosting climate resilience, conceding that cutting emissions alone is no longer enough against heatwaves, supercharged storms, and wildfires. Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said the bloc must keep cutting emissions harder and move full-frontal into clean energy, but that alone “no longer is good enough.” The proposed Climate Resilience Law asks governments to plan for a world 2°C hotter by 2050 and 3°C by century’s end, aligning with UN estimates that current policies track toward about 2.8°C. Member states would run national climate-risk assessments every five years from 2029 and draw up climate-proofing plans by end-2030. The European Environment Agency’s next EU-wide risk assessment is due by June 2028, and the Commission plans an EU strategic plan on climate resilience by end-September 2028. The package carries no new funding; the bloc needs roughly €70 billion a year for adaptation by 2050 versus about €29 billion spent now. Hoekstra stressed that 1.5°C remains the goal while preparing for the current trajectory.

**Commentary:** Writing a 3°C planning duty into law without new money shifts the fight to whether plans alone can protect people.

### 2. China’s NDRC issues Energy Supply Mid- to Long-Term Contract Measures, effective Dec. 1 (security of supply)
**Summary:** Xinhua reported on Tuesday that China’s National Development and Reform Commission has issued the Energy Supply Mid- to Long-Term Contract Management Measures (Trial), effective December 1, to strengthen production–supply–storage–sales systems and reinforce the role of mid- to long-term contracts in energy security. The rules bring thermal coal, electricity, and natural-gas supply contracts under a unified framework covering scope, supply responsibilities, contracting and performance, and supervision. The measures prioritize livelihoods and a secure floor for household energy use, combine effective markets with capable government, and seek to balance development and security. NDRC said the rules should help contracts act as a stabilizer and ballast for supply-chain resilience.

### 3. CETP members cut international fossil finance by up to 80%, redirect under half to clean energy (climate finance)
**Summary:** IISD, Oil Change International, and Friends of the Earth U.S. released an assessment on October 27 showing Clean Energy Transition Partnership members cut international public fossil-fuel finance to USD 3.3 billion in 2025—an up-to-80% drop from the 2019–2021 pre-CETP average, or USD 8.5–13.2 billion less per year. Eleven of 16 high-income signatories now have fully aligned energy-finance policies, up from 10 last year. Only about 42% of funding removed from fossils has shifted into clean energy; members’ clean-energy support reached USD 5.6 billion in 2025 (28% above baseline), but emerging and developing economies received just 24% versus 71% for advanced economies. Italy led remaining fossil finance at USD 6 billion in 2023–2025, followed by Germany (USD 2.5 billion) and Switzerland (USD 1.6 billion); export credit agencies accounted for 64% of members’ 2025 fossil support. After the U.S. left CETP in 2025, Ex-Im financed nearly USD 8 billion in fossil projects that fiscal year versus about USD 117.5 million for clean energy.

### 4. Trump to announce easing of oil and gas methane rules in Texas, targeting marginal wells (oil & gas)
**Summary:** Reuters, The Hill, and others reported on October 27 that President Trump would announce an EPA proposal to roll back Biden-era methane rules for oil and gas producers during a Texas campaign swing. The draft would ease obligations for low-output “marginal wells,” end the Super Emitter Program that used outside reports of large leaks, and relax rules aimed at phasing out routine flaring of associated gas. Marginal wells produce roughly 6–7% of U.S. oil and gas but about half of industry methane emissions. The administration expects about USD 45 billion in sector savings and additional output of roughly 210 million barrels of oil and 1.41 trillion cubic feet of gas from 2027 to 2050. Biden’s 2023 tightening was projected to avoid about 58 million tons of methane from 2024–2038. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin previewed earlier this month that the proposal would address marginal wells, associated gas, and Super Emitter rules.

### 5. DOE offers Constellation up to USD 5 billion conditional loan to uprate eight nuclear plants (nuclear)
**Summary:** The U.S. Department of Energy on October 27 announced a conditional loan commitment of up to USD 5 billion from its Office of Energy Dominance Financing to Constellation Energy for nuclear uprates and modernization at eight plants in Illinois, Maryland, and Pennsylvania. The work is expected to add nearly 1.1 GW—enough for more than 930,000 homes—and support over 10,000 project-related jobs across PJM and MISO. Sites include Braidwood, Byron, Clinton, Dresden, LaSalle, Quad Cities, Calvert Cliffs, and Limerick. Funding still requires due diligence, definitive financing documents, and technical, legal, environmental, and financial conditions; Constellation said the commitment alone is not a final decision to proceed at every facility. The company has already lined up more than USD 7 billion of private investment with Google and Amazon to add about 1,080 MW of nuclear capacity. DOE said about 94% of President Trump’s 5 GW existing-reactor uprate goal has been identified.

## II. Clean Power & Storage

### 6. Portugal opens 1,050 MVA of grid capacity, including 750 MVA for standalone batteries (storage)
**Summary:** Portugal’s Ministry of Environment and Energy on October 27 launched two electronic auctions offering a combined 1,050 MVA of grid-connection capacity—the first competitive procedures of this kind since 2021: 300 MVA for renewables with co-located storage and 750 MVA for standalone electricity storage. The Directorate-General for Energy and Geology will run the tenders after Official Gazette orders published October 22. Standalone storage must match at least 100% of allocated injection and charging capacity and deliver at least four hours at maximum injection power; co-located projects need storage power equal to at least 20% of allocated injection capacity with the same four-hour requirement. The auctions award connection rights, not power-purchase contracts. Municipalities would receive 70% of revenues from the co-located auction and 30% from the standalone auction. The move aligns with a proposed National Energy Storage Strategy aiming for 3 GW of batteries by 2030.

**Links:**

- [Review Energy — Portugal opens 1,050 MVA for renewables and storage, including 750 MVA for standalone batteries](https://www.review-energy.com/otras-fuentes/portugal-opens-1-050-mva-of-grid-capacity-for-renewables-and-storage-including-750-mva-for-standalone-batteries)

**Commentary:** Three-quarters of the capacity is reserved for standalone batteries—flexibility first, more generation second.

### 7. Thai cabinet approves 50-billion-baht rooftop solar scheme plus smart-grid spend (distributed)
**Summary:** The Bangkok Post reported on October 27, citing Reuters and a finance ministry official, that Thailand’s cabinet approved a 50-billion-baht (about USD 1.48 billion) rooftop solar programme as part of the energy transition. The energy minister had said the government aims to help one million households install panels within a year to shield people from Middle East war-driven energy prices, with 50,000 baht in direct support per household and one-stop registration via MEA and PEA. The cabinet also approved more than 20 billion baht for smart-grid investment so households can sell surplus solar power back to the state, and a biofuel tax cut that officials said would help farmers, ease living costs, and reduce import dependence.

**Summary:** ABC News reported on October 27 that Western Australia’s energy and decarbonisation minister warned business power prices could jump 89% by 2032 without a radical increase in renewable generation. The government wants the South West Interconnected System’s green-power share to rise from about 40% to 75% over five years, mainly via wind, batteries, and solar, supported by gas. The same day, the state environmental watchdog sought comment on APA’s proposed 870 MW Keralup Energy Hub—210 MW for a bio-fertiliser facility and 660 MW eventually connecting to SWIS—which would be the state’s largest gas-fired station. A delayed low-interest loan programme for local manufacturers (up to AUD 15 million for solar, batteries, and equipment upgrades) also launched. The Greens welcomed the renewable target but flagged about 2% renewables in the North West and opposed new gas approvals.

### 10. India MNRE: about USD 220 billion needed in 2027–2030 for 500 GW renewables goal (finance)
**Summary:** CNBC-TV18 reported on October 27 that MNRE Secretary Santosh Kumar Sarangi said India will blend domestic funding, international capital, and blended finance to meet its 500 GW renewable-energy target and is “fairly confident” that mix can work. Policy certainty remains a priority as the country seeks to mobilize around USD 220 billion in renewable financing between 2027 and 2030. Risk-mitigation tools include 25-year DISCOM PPAs, a SECI payment-security mechanism, and centre–state tripartite agreements under which states may cover losses if DISCOMs default. The government is also pushing 5 GW of floating-solar incentives, decentralised renewables, agrivoltaics, and expansion of PM-KUSUM and PM Surya Ghar in lower-solar states, while round-the-clock renewable tenders and batteries are expected to help DISCOMs manage all-day and peak demand.

### 12. Record-hot El Niño ocean waters drive Southern California coastal flooding (sea level)
**Summary:** The Los Angeles Times reported on October 27 that maximum high-tide sea levels along the Southern California coast were nearly two feet above what astronomical forces alone would produce, as El Niño-driven record ocean heat expands seawater. Monday’s high tide flooded business districts in Newport Beach and parts of Pacific Coast Highway in Sunset Beach; San Pedro set an all-time record above 8 feet, with conditions expected to worsen Tuesday. Scientists said even without former-hurricane swells, hot coastal water has lifted peak levels by roughly 1.25 to 1.8 feet. The paper framed unprecedented coastal flooding as quickly becoming a fact of life in some communities.
