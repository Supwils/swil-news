## I. Climate Negotiations & International Cooperation

### 1. COP31 talks in Antalya go into overtime: fossil-fuel language cut from draft; “35 by 35” electrification takes centre stage (Policy)

**Summary:** The 31st UN climate conference was due to close on Nov 20, but talks with nearly 200 countries spilled into overtime amid deadlock on energy, finance and science. The latest draft “cover decision” dropped fossil-fuel language and a roughly USD 120 billion adaptation-finance target, while retaining the presidency’s “35 by 35” goal—raising electricity to 35% of final energy consumption by 2035—and controversial wording on carbon capture and storage. The EU said the pared-back text risked turning the climate COP into a “fossil summit”; France, Panama and others demanded six priorities including an orderly transition away from fossil fuels and clean electrification. China and Australia issued a joint statement the same day welcoming voluntary cooperation on electrification.

### 2. China–Australia joint statement on climate and clean-energy transition echoes “35 by 35” (Policy)

**Summary:** China’s government portal on Nov 20 published a Joint Statement of the People’s Republic of China and Australia on Climate Change and the Clean Energy Transition. Both sides reaffirm the Paris Agreement temperature goals, commit to keeping 1.5°C within reach and minimising the magnitude and duration of any overshoot, welcome international cooperation to triple global renewable capacity by 2030 and double the rate of energy-efficiency improvement, and welcome raising electricity’s share of global final energy consumption to 35% on a voluntary basis by 2035. The statement also stresses that transition pathways differ by national circumstances and that secure, affordable, reliable and resilient energy systems matter.

## II. Policy & Carbon Markets

### 4. EU and UK finalise ETS linking talks, aiming for 1 January 2028 (Carbon markets)

**Summary:** At their bilateral summit on Nov 20, EU and UK leaders announced the conclusion of negotiations to link their emissions trading systems, intending the link to apply from 1 January 2028 subject to ratification and entry into force. Linked allowances would be fungible for compliance, creating a larger, more liquid carbon market; the UK will expand its ETS, including aviation and maritime, bringing over 10 million tonnes of additional CO2 into the system. The deal also lays groundwork for possible mutual CBAM exemptions. The UK government says linking could exempt about GBP 6.5 billion of UK exports from the EU CBAM and add up to GBP 3.8 billion a year to the UK economy by 2040.

### 5. US Senate passes bipartisan energy permitting bill to speed power plants and data centres (Policy)

**Summary:** The US Senate on Nov 20 passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act 62–33; it now goes to the House. The bill aims to accelerate permitting for power plants, transmission, pipelines and data centres, and to make data centres pay for the power and grid connections they use; 14 Democrats and one Independent joined most Republicans in support. Backers say it could speed solar, wind, batteries and transmission amid power prices up more than 6% in the past year and about 39% over five years. The Sierra Club and other green groups, plus many Democrats, oppose it, warning it weakens clean-air, water and species protections and eases oil and gas projects.

### 6. Spain requires data centres to use 80% renewable power and speeds wind, self-consumption and storage rules (Policy)

**Summary:** Spain’s Council of Ministers on Nov 20 approved a royal decree requiring data centres to prove that 80% of their electricity comes from renewables, with hourly matching and additionality criteria to drive new clean capacity; it also allows long-term PPAs, self-consumption and storage, plus a voluntary priority-disconnect option to protect grid stability. The same package strengthens repowering of wind farms over 15 years old (up to 25% extra grid capacity), frees connection capacity for industrial self-consumption and green hydrogen, and eases pumped hydro and CSP storage upgrades. Non-compliance can bring financial penalties and suspension of grid-access rights for up to three years.

## III. Energy Markets & Outlook

### 7. IEA World Energy Outlook 2026: Hormuz shock permanently reshapes priorities; solar and nuclear gain, coal may linger as backup (Outlook)

**Summary:** The IEA’s World Energy Outlook 2026, covered on Nov 20, says the Iran war and Strait of Hormuz disruption amount to one of history’s largest energy supply shocks and will “leave lasting marks”. Governments are prioritising electricity security and affordability, making solar and nuclear the biggest beneficiaries—yet security concerns may also keep some Asian importers leaning on domestic coal as backup. If Hormuz risks persist, oil flows through the strait could fall from about 20 million barrels per day before the crisis to 8.5 million in 2030 and under 2 million by 2035. The IEA also notes that, for the first time, energy demand for cooling overtook heating this year—the start of a multi-decade trend.

## IV. Clean Power & Storage

### 9. China’s first deep-desert wind project connects: first 160 MW of 300 MW Alashan project online (Clean power)

**Summary:** China’s National Energy Administration reported on Nov 20 that the first 16 turbines (160 MW) of the Mengneng “Adian-into-Wuhai” 300 MW wind project—the country’s first wind farm in a desert hinterland—have connected to the grid. Located in the Ulan Buh Desert hinterland of Alxa League, Inner Mongolia, the wind farm is the wind portion of a 2 GW regional mutual-aid renewables scheme that also includes 1.7 GW of solar and 300 MW / 600 MWh of storage. At full capacity the wind project is expected to generate about 690 GWh a year, saving roughly 227,000 tonnes of standard coal and cutting about 590,000 tonnes of CO2, offering a construction template for “desert-gobi-wasteland” mega-bases pushed deeper inland.
