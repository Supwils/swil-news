### 1. Hurricane Simon weakens to 100 mph, with the warning narrowed to Las Islas Marias (Hurricane)
**Summary:** The National Hurricane Center's 2 p.m. MST advisory on Monday placed Simon's center near 20.2N, 106.8W, about 75 miles (115 km) west of Cabo Corrientes and 210 miles (335 km) south of Mazatlan, moving slowly west-northwest at 3 mph, with maximum sustained winds down to 100 mph (155 km/h) and a central pressure of 969 millibars. Rainfall of 5 to 10 inches is expected along the coast, with isolated totals of 15 inches in Sinaloa by Thursday.

### 2. About 146,000 Florida structures still dark after Isaias, with the hardest repairs set for Tuesday (Storm)
**Summary:** USA Today reported Monday that Isaias made landfall near Destin, Florida, on the evening of October 9 as a Category 2 hurricane, and officials still link at least four deaths to the storm. As of Monday morning, about 146,000 structures in Florida had no power, mostly in Escambia and Santa Rosa counties, while about 20,000 homes and businesses in Alabama remained dark. Florida Power & Light said most customers should be restored by the end of October 12, with the hardest-hit areas back on October 13; Alabama Power said 95 percent of affected customers were already restored. Landfall came several hours before high tide, meteorologists estimate the surge could have reached seven feet had it coincided with the tide, schools in Santa Rosa lost roof sections, and several counties remained under boil-water notices.

**Summary:** NBC News reported Monday that oil prices rose after President Donald Trump posted Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate energy ceasefire: Brent jumped about 2 percent to almost $108 a barrel, U.S. crude rose more than 2 percent to about $94, and diesel futures erased last week's small drop. ### 4. Wright says Hormuz crude is back to 80–90 percent, while refined products are at 25–35 percent (Refined products)
**Summary:** Asharq Al-Awsat reported Monday that U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright, speaking by video to the WPC Energy Congress in Riyadh, said oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz had recovered to 80 to 90 percent of pre-conflict levels, averaging 13 million to 14 million barrels a day, with spare capacity and inventories keeping crude broadly manageable but not fully resolved. The larger gap is refined-product exports from the Arabian Gulf, still at 25 to 35 percent of pre-conflict levels, which has worsened shortages of diesel, jet fuel, and gasoline. Wright said Russia had shifted from a major diesel supplier to a limited net importer, China had sharply cut refined-product exports and said it would not export such products in October, and U.S. refineries were processing crude at record levels. Disruptions in the Bab el-Mandeb and longer routes are raising freight costs, with a greater price impact in markets farther from supply.

### 5. Monday morning Gulf oil shut-ins fall to 604,503 barrels a day, 29.63 percent of output (Oil and gas)
**Summary:** The U.S. Marine Minerals Administration said on October 12 that, based on reports from 25 companies as of 11:00 a.m. CDT, shut-in Gulf oil was 604,503 barrels a day, 29.63 percent of current daily oil production, and shut-in gas was 531 million cubic feet a day, 24.81 percent. Personnel remained evacuated from 5 of 371 manned platforms, one of 11 non-dynamically positioned rigs had been evacuated, and none of 17 dynamically positioned rigs had moved. Argus Media reported the same day that the figures were well below the roughly 1.5 million barrels a day of oil and 1.3 billion cubic feet a day of gas offline shortly before landfall, and that companies including BP and Shell began redeploying staff over the weekend.

### 7. The IPCC opens its 65th session with the Seventh Assessment timeline still unresolved (Climate science)

### 9. China's finance ministry seeks comment on climate-disclosure guides that map impacts onto accounts (Disclosure)
**Summary:** China Securities Journal reported on October 12 that the Ministry of Finance recently issued a draft application guide for Climate Disclosure Standard No. 1, plus draft industry guides for power, steel, coal, oil, fertilizer, aluminum, hydrogen, cement, and autos. The guides break climate-related risks and opportunities into climate matter, business effect, and financial effect, and map those effects onto accounting lines; an allowance shortfall, for example, corresponds to buying allowances, higher compliance cost, and entries such as carbon-allowance assets and non-operating expenses. The power guide would cover emissions intensity, the generation mix, and renewable absorption; steel would cover product carbon footprint and energy use; coal would cover methane management, potential carbon dioxide in reserves, and green-mine development. Wu Yanyang of the Industrial Bank carbon-finance institute said power, steel, cement, and aluminum are already in the national carbon market, while the other sectors remain inside energy-consumption controls and low-carbon supply chains.

**Links:**

- [China Securities Journal — How companies should keep a climate ledger, and the guide that may explain it](https://www.cs.com.cn/esg/2026/10/12/detail_2026101210043995.html)

**Commentary:** The standard is moving from principles to ledger lines, so an allowance shortfall has to show up in the accounts and not only in a sustainability report.

### 10. Tsinghua report: 167 net-zero pledges, and only 37 countries have written them into law (Net zero)
**Summary:** China Energy News reported Monday on the 2026 Global Carbon Neutrality Progress Report from Tsinghua University's Institute for Carbon Neutrality. As of May 2026, 167 countries and regions had a net-zero-type target, only two more than the year before; just 37 countries had written it into law, 73 into policy documents, and only 46 had accountability arrangements. The report says 2025 renewable capacity reached 5,149 GW, 49.4 percent of total power capacity, with 692 GW added, and clean-energy investment of about $2.2 trillion was close to twice fossil-fuel investment, while more than 2,500 GW stayed stuck in grid queues. On the current trend, 2030 capacity would be about 10,082 GW, or 91.7 percent of the tripling goal, with a modelled on-time probability of 3.3 percent; even then renewable generation would equal only 74.7 percent of fossil generation, and China accounted for 64 percent of renewable capacity added in 2025.

## IV. Green Power and Storage

### 11. South Korea sets out the auction that will replace renewable quotas in 2027 (Green power)
**Summary:** pv magazine on Monday detailed draft rules from South Korea's Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment for a contract market that replaces the renewable quota system on January 1, 2027; the drafts were presented at a Seoul hearing on September 30. Korea Electric Power Corp. would sign long-term fixed-price contracts, settle the monthly difference against the wholesale price, and recover the cost through tariffs, with two to four tenders a year by energy source. Projects of 500 kW or less would use a separate market, and virtual power plants of at least 1 MW could receive bidding incentives. New projects stop receiving renewable energy certificates after December 31, 2026, the spot market closes at the end of 2029, a detailed plan is due in November, and the first tender is set for the first quarter of 2027; about 210,000 plants totalling 35.4 GW were registered as of September, and storage stays in a separate Korea Power Exchange market.

**Links:**

- [pv magazine — South Korea details an auction market to replace the renewable quota system](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/10/12/south-korea-details-auction-market-to-replace-renewable-quota-system/)

**Commentary:** The certificate spot market is on the way out, revenue shifts to a long contract hedged against the wholesale price, and storage is left in a separate market.
