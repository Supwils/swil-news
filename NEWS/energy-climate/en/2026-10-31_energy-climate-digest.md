## I. Policy & Bill Politics

### 1. UK chancellor: new offshore wind will be vetoed if it will not lower bills (Policy)
**Summary:** The Guardian reported on Oct 31 that UK Chancellor John Healey said ministers will not sign off new offshore-wind projects in the next renewables auction unless they believe the output can be absorbed by Britain’s grid without raising consumer costs. He called clean power the way to “break the grip of international gas prices on our bills,” but said the shift must come “at the right pace, in the right places, with the grid to carry it,” adding there is “no sense building power that can’t connect and then paying to switch it off.” The Autumn Budget cut energy-department spending, including about £741 million from the boiler-upgrade (heat-pump) scheme, feeding concern about 2030 clean-power and clean-heat goals; a September National Audit Office report had warned constraint payments could reach about £7.8 billion a year by 2030.

### 2. EDF: Trump administration has backed nearly half of U.S. coal plants with cash or pollution waivers (United States)
**Summary:** The Guardian on Oct 31 cited an Environmental Defense Fund analysis of 184 grid-connected U.S. coal plants: 90 received Trump administration support via cash, Clean Air Act exemptions, or orders to stay online past planned retirement. About 24 plants received roughly $950 million in taxpayer funds and about 70 received toxic-pollution-limit exemptions; the White House said the administration has “saved” more than 100 coal plants and prevented retirement of more than 17 GW. EDF said companies emailed to seek relief from the 2024 Mercury and Air Toxics Standards update, citing compliance costs; Montana’s Colstrip and Texas’s WA Parish plants featured prominently. Critics say the exemptions prolong polluting units and raise costs; the administration stresses coal’s role in peak reliability.

**Links:**

### 3. Axios: who pays for the AI power boom becomes a U.S. electricity-bill fight (Load)
**Summary:** Axios on Oct 31 noted that data centers already pay for the electricity they use and generally for interconnection, but cost allocation for new plants and broader transmission upgrades remains murky and can spill onto households. Longstanding federal rules typically do not require large users to pay both for their share of the existing grid and for new transmission built to serve them. More than half of states have taken or considered residential protections, including special rates and requirements that data centers fund dedicated infrastructure; Congress is weighing bipartisan legislation that would make large data centers cover more build costs even if projects are canceled. The issue is surfacing in midterm races, with candidates in both parties calling for stronger bill protections.

**Links:**

- [Axios — Who pays for the AI power boom?](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/31/who-pays-ai-power-boom)

**Commentary:** The debate has shifted from “enough megawatts?” to “whose bill?”—cost-sharing rules will sit at the next permitting and campaign crossroads.

### 4. Moldova eases wind and solar permits: 24 months for large projects, 12 in acceleration zones (Permitting)
**Summary:** Moldova1 reported that parliament on Oct 29 adopted renewable-energy law amendments (55 votes in favor) creating a unified digital permitting path via the government’s EVO platform: up to 24 months for large projects, up to 12 months inside designated acceleration areas, and as little as one month for primarily self-consumption PV up to 100 kW. Eligible producers can receive guaranteed purchase or fixed-price support for 15 years after commissioning. Industry leaders warn auction winners with “public utility” status in acceleration zones could pressure neighboring landowners; the Energy Ministry says degraded or unused public land will be prioritized, easements are temporary and compensated, and protected areas are excluded. The rules take effect one month after Official Gazette publication; storage support for smaller producers facing midday price dips is also under study.

**Links:**

- [Moldova1 — Moldova eases permits for wind and solar projects](https://moldova1.md/p/89043/moldova-eases-permits-for-wind-and-solar-projects)

**Commentary:** Digital fast-track is how a small European system catches up on clean power—but land-rights design decides whether acceleration zones favor big developers or local owners.

## II. Clean Power, Storage & Projects

### 5. Neoen secures about AUD 1.8 billion for a 1.2 GW Australian wind–solar–storage portfolio (Finance)
**Summary:** French developer Neoen said on Oct 30 it had raised about AUD 1.8 billion in debt from 13 Australian and international lenders for five operating or under-construction assets totaling about 1.2 GW across South Australia, New South Wales and Western Australia: Hornsdale Power Reserve (150 MW / 193.5 MWh), Goyder South Wind Farm Stage 1A (209 MW), Culcairn Solar Farm (440 MWp), Culcairn Battery (215 MW / 963 MWh) and Narrogin Wind Farm (179 MW). ### 6. U.S. EIA: over 16% of 2026 utility-scale solar additions through September are co-located with batteries (Storage)
**Summary:** The U.S. Energy Information Administration said on Oct 30 that more than 16% of new utility-scale solar capacity added in 2026 through September is co-located with operable battery storage, up from about 2% in 2017. Plans for projects due in the final quarter could lift the 2026 share to about 19%, while plans for 2027–2028 point above 30%. Co-located plants can send output to the grid or the battery depending on wholesale prices. As of September, solar is about 13% of U.S. utility-scale capacity and about 18% of that solar sits with batteries; co-location shares in California, Arizona, New Mexico and Nevada are more than double the national average.

### 7. India’s ASSOCHAM: grids and storage decide whether capacity becomes reliable power; $2.5 trillion flagged before COP31 (India)
**Summary:** ET Energyworld reported on Oct 31 that ASSOCHAM’s COP31 agenda paper From Pledges to Projects says stronger grids, more storage and commercially viable clean power are essential to turn installed renewables into reliable electricity. Battery storage needs are projected to rise from about 37 GWh by 2027 to about 236 GWh by 2031–32, while meeting 2030 climate goals may require about $2.5 trillion. Non-fossil capacity reached about 300.5 GW in July 2026—over 54% of installed power, five years ahead of a related 2030 milestone—after a record about 55.29 GW of renewable additions in FY26 (about 44.6 GW solar). The chamber also flagged CBAM exposure for iron and steel exports to the EU and called for a credible domestic carbon market; the report is being submitted to the PMO and NITI Aayog.

### 8. China’s Liupanshan Unit 1 enters service: first million-kW “dual integration” demonstration (China)
**Summary:** China Power News Network reported on Oct 31 that at 10:56 on Oct 30, Unit 1 at CHN Energy Ningxia Power’s Liupanshan plant completed its 168-hour full-load trial, putting the first unit of the country’s first million-kilowatt-class coal–coal and coal–renewables “dual integration” demonstration into commercial operation. The project plans two 1,000 MW ultra-supercritical indirect air-cooled units, renewables sized at about a 1:2 ratio to the coal units, and the 4 Mt/y Nanwan coal mine with a dedicated rail line. The plant claims a first application of adaptive 20%–100% load control on a million-kW unit for deep peaking and fast ramping to support regional renewables. Zero-liquid-discharge water systems and about 4.1 million cubic meters a year of reclaimed municipal water underpin its southern Ningxia backbone and peaking role.

### 9. Australia debates whether Snowy 2.0 still makes sense as batteries scale (Long-duration storage)
**Summary:** ABC News on Oct 31 reviewed Snowy 2.0’s roughly AUD 25 billion cost—about 12 times early estimates—with delivery targeted around late 2029. The scheme is rated about 2.2 GW with about 350 GWh of reservoir energy, or roughly 160 hours at full output in promotional framing. AEMO has approved about 3.6 GW of batteries due in 2028–29; Origin is planning about 500 MW / eight-hour storage beside the Eraring coal plant slated to close in 2029. Experts noted national batteries still total on the order of 30 GWh—far below Snowy’s depth. Supporters cast it as insurance within a ~650 GWh 2050 dispatchable-storage roadmap; critics question upper-reservoir water practicality, continuous full-output use cases, and whether extreme price volatility is needed for payback, favoring smaller modular pumped hydro next.

**Links:**

- [ABC News — Does Snowy 2.0 still have a place in a world of batteries?](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-31/snowy-scheme-has-place-in-world-of-batteries/107313340)

**Commentary:** Short-duration batteries clear daily swings; deep pumped hydro hedges week-scale gaps—complements, not substitutes, with an ever-harder cost ledger.

## III. Climate Impacts & COP31 Agenda

### 10. Indonesia’s wildfires and toxic haze persist under a “super” El Niño; respiratory cases top 283,000 in seven provinces (Disaster)
**Summary:** NBC News reported on Oct 31 that fires across Indonesia’s forests and peatlands since July—worsened by heat and drought linked to a “super” El Niño—have produced the country’s worst fire season in more than a decade, choking Southeast Asia and closing schools. As of Oct 13, more than 283,000 respiratory-illness cases linked to the fires had been reported in seven provinces, more than double the about 113,000 as of Sept 9. Students protested in Jakarta over the government’s refusal to declare a national disaster; Singapore and Indonesia discussed a joint task force on illegal land burning, while Indonesia said about 20 companies were under investigation. Katadata, citing MoEF’s SiPongi system, recorded 4,497 satellite hotspots in the prior 24 hours as of 11:50 on Oct 31 (203 high confidence), led by East Kalimantan, Central Kalimantan and Central Sulawesi. A delayed rainy season may keep fires into early November; light rains this week brought brief relief.

### 11. COP31 presidency launches Antalya Compact on building efficiency, tied to the 35-by-35 electrification goal (Summit)
**Summary:** Türkiye’s COP31 Presidency on Oct 29 launched the Antalya Compact for Energy Efficient Buildings, open for Party endorsement, aiming for about a 25% cut in global building operational energy intensity by 2035 versus 2020 and accelerating efficient electrification in support of raising electricity’s share of final energy use to 35% by 2035. Developed with the UNEP-hosted GlobalABC secretariat, the Compact sets six action areas spanning national strategies, new and existing building performance, renovation, electrified heating/cooling/cooking, building flexibility for power systems, and finance and capacity. An ICBC ministerial on buildings and climate on Nov 12 during COP31 will push endorsements. Buildings account for nearly 40% of energy-related carbon emissions; the Compact builds on the 2024 Chaillot Declaration backed by more than 70 countries. COP31 runs Nov 9–20 in Antalya.
