### 1. Midway through COP31, negotiators wrestle with what 1.5°C still means after an overshoot warning (COP · Temperature Goal)
**Summary:** On November 15, COP31 talks in Antalya reached their midpoint—the first summit after the UN said a breach of the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C threshold is imminent and unavoidable. Front-line states such as Palau insist 1.5°C is existential for food, economies and sovereignty. Observers say parts of the Arab Group and Like-Minded Developing Countries see room to weaken the benchmark, while some developing countries fear stronger wording without extra finance.

### 2. Türkiye and Australia push an electrification deal; critics say it skips a clean-power mandate (COP · Electrification)
**Summary:** SBS reported on November 15 that as COP31 entered week two, host Türkiye and negotiation chair Australia are pressing for a global electrification agreement to replace fossil-fuel cars, boilers and industrial furnaces with electricity. Türkiye argues broader electrification will cut emissions faster; critics note the draft does not require new power to come from renewables and could crowd out a clearer fossil-fuel phase-down roadmap. Australian Energy Minister Chris Bowen said he expects a first draft deal text early next week. Thousands marched in Antalya demanding urgent fossil-fuel action after UN Secretary-General António Guterres called for immediate, deep cuts.

### 4. Indonesia and Norway sign an Article 6.2 MOPA for up to 12 MtCO₂e, starting with floating solar plus storage (Carbon Markets · Bilateral)
**Summary:** Reporting around COP31 says Indonesia and Norway signed a Mitigation Outcome Purchase Agreement (MOPA)—Indonesia's first commercial contract to operationalize Paris Agreement Article 6.2. With support from the Global Green Growth Institute and Norway's Article 6 Climate Action Fund, Norway plans to buy up to about 12 million tonnes of CO₂e reductions from PLN solar projects through 2038, priced at roughly $20–40 per tonne. The deal backs Indonesia's Generation-Based Incentive program, initially focused on floating solar and prioritizing battery storage so power can be delivered in high-emission peak hours; verified cuts would transfer as ITMOs.

## II. Clean Power, Storage & Regional Policy

### 6. German coalition specialists agree to keep small-solar feed-in pay; grid curtailment rules stay unresolved (Europe · Policy)
**Summary:** On November 15, ESG Insider, citing dpa, reported that SPD and Union energy specialists reached a technical agreement on reforming Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act that would retain feed-in payments for solar systems up to 25 kW—reversing an Economy Ministry draft that would largely end lasting support for new sub-25 kW systems after a short transitional payment and shift them to direct marketing. Parties stressed that partial deals depend on an overall energy package. Grid connection and curtailment compensation remain the core fight: Economy Minister Reiche wants tighter curtailment pay, while the SPD prefers capping allowed feed-in below peak capacity. Negotiators aim for the Federal Council session on December 18, 2026.

## III. Climate Risk, Carbon Capture & Oil-and-Gas Litigation

### 10. A "super" El Niño hangs over COP31 as WMO calls it a risk multiplier (Climate · Extremes)
**Summary:** The Guardian reported from Antalya on November 15 that what may be the strongest El Niño in at least 1,000 years is amplifying wildfires and crop failures in Indonesia, coastal inundation in the United States, drought in Sudan, and flooding in places including Ecuador and Somalia. WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo said societies are "supercharging" weather and El Niño will be a "risk multiplier." Impacts are expected to peak around December and ripple through 2027; UNHCR warns of as many as about 3 million displacements across Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan and Uganda alone, with Kenyan maize output already down about 40%. The adaptation and loss-and-damage finance gap looks sharper against that backdrop.

### 11. California court voids approval of the state's first operating CCS project, a pillar of climate plans (US · CCS)
**Summary:** The Los Angeles Times reported on November 15 that a Kern County judge ruled approval of California Resources Corp.'s TerraVault 1—the state's first operating carbon capture and storage project—violated the California Environmental Quality Act and must be set aside. The court found the environmental review failed to analyze industries such as hydrogen, cement and steel that could later send CO₂ to the reservoirs, undermining the whole informational basis; the project is designed to store up to about 46 million metric tons. Because the judgment is not yet final, it is unclear when injection must stop; CRC says it will aggressively pursue options to advance the project, while plaintiffs seek a shutdown and fresh review that could take months or years.

**Links:**

### 12. Norway's Supreme Court invalidates three North Sea oil and gas approvals for skipping combustion emissions (Europe · Oil & Gas Law)
**Summary:** Norway's Supreme Court Grand Chamber ruled that approvals of development and operation plans for the Breidablikk, Tyrving and Yggdrasil North Sea fields are invalid because environmental assessments omitted greenhouse gases from burning the extracted fuels and their effect on global warming. The ruling does not automatically halt output: two fields are producing and Yggdrasil is due in 2027; the Energy Ministry must reassess under proper procedure, and oil companies were not parties to the case. November 15 finance-week roundups flagged the decision as a signal that future licenses must price "burned later" climate harm into permitting.
