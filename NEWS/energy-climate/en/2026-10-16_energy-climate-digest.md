## I. Policy and Carbon Markets

### 1. EU summit conclusions land: short-term price relief and a methane delay, without changing the carbon-market direction (Policy)
**Summary:** The European Council met in Brussels on October 15-16. Talks ran into Thursday night, and conclusions were adopted on Friday. Leaders called for targeted, temporary, coordinated measures against high energy prices. They welcomed the Commission's intention to prolong the temporary state-aid framework for the industrial sectors most exposed, to optimise EU refinery capacity and efficiency, and to pool energy demand on a voluntary basis. They also welcomed the Commission's proposal to postpone certain obligations in the Methane Regulation. Euronews reported that the proposal would give oil and gas importers an extra year on methane monitoring and reporting, and that it still needs approval by the European Parliament. On carbon markets, leaders asked the Council and Parliament to speed legislation on the Market Stability Reserve so as to curb excessive price volatility. In the ETS review, they want a predictable market, protection against carbon leakage, and less pass-through of carbon-price swings into electricity prices, while preserving the ETS as a signal for investment and innovation. The Commission was asked to update its assessment of all impacts of ETS2, the system covering road transport and building fuels, especially its design and safeguards. The published text does not delay ETS2 to 2030.

## II. Power, Nuclear, and Carbon Capture

### 4. Shengli Oilfield breaks ground on a million-tonne low-concentration carbon-capture project in Dongying (Carbon capture)
**Summary:** China News Service reported Friday from Dongying, Shandong, that China's first million-tonne, full-chain demonstration of capturing, using, and storing low-concentration carbon dioxide broke ground on October 16 at Sinopec's Shengli Oilfield, with startup targeted for the end of 2028. The existing Qilu-Shengli project captures high-concentration tail gas from coal-to-hydrogen units, about 90% carbon dioxide. Since it started in August 2022 it has stored 1.4 billion standard cubic meters, and daily oil output in the demonstration area has risen from 220 tonnes to 525 tonnes. The new project turns to coal-plant flue gas of only 12% to 15% carbon dioxide, which also contains sulfur and nitrogen oxides. Shengli said its absorbent and energy-saving regeneration process has reached a capture rate above 90%, product purity above 99.5%, and heat use below 2.0 gigajoules per tonne of carbon dioxide. The captured gas will be injected through more than 70 wells into beach-bar sand, turbidite, and shale-oil reservoirs. The project pairs wind, storage, a direct line, and capture: 13 turbines totaling 98.8 megawatts, 217 million kilowatt-hours of green electricity a year, supplied directly to the capture plant.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** What broke ground is the harder low-concentration flue-gas chain; the 1.4 billion cubic meters already stored belong to the high-concentration project that started in 2022.

### 7. AP maps three nuclear restarts: up to $4.4 billion in loans and 2.25 gigawatts (Nuclear)
**Summary:** A Friday Associated Press report, also carried by the Los Angeles Times, said the U.S. Department of Energy is lending as much as $4.4 billion to restart the Palisades plant in Michigan, the Crane Clean Energy Center at Pennsylvania's Three Mile Island, and the Duane Arnold plant in Iowa, restoring a combined 2.25 gigawatts, enough for roughly 2 million homes and businesses. All three closed before their operating licenses expired. Holtec is leading Palisades. Fuel loading began in August and paused after an assembly tilted. Power-purchase agreements require the plant in service by March 2027. A $1.5 billion loan approved in 2024 has continued under the current administration. Constellation said in 2024 it would spend $1.6 billion to restart Crane under a 20-year contract with Microsoft and hopes to generate again in 2027. The Energy Department provided a $1 billion loan. NextEra and Google are working to restart Duane Arnold by early 2029. Edwin Lyman of the Union of Concerned Scientists said the reactors were designed to the disaster standards of the 1970s through the 1990s, and that taxpayer money should first close safety gaps. The report said no commercial reactor is known to have generated again after decommissioning began.

### 9. World Bank president says the bank will still back gas "where appropriate" (Finance)
**Summary:** Climate Home News reported Friday that World Bank President Ajay Banga, speaking at the bank's annual meetings in Bangkok, said the bank will keep supporting gas in developing countries "where appropriate," alongside renewables, hydropower, nuclear, transmission, grids, and storage. He said homes, mines, factories, and growing cities need reliable, affordable baseload power. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who did not attend the Bangkok meetings, said in a Wednesday statement that the bank should formally retire its bar on financing gas and oil exploration and production. The bank ended coal support about ten years ago, funded no oil projects last year for the first time, and has ended support for gas exploration, while still backing some transport and use, including a $140 million project to help Benin, Cote d'Ivoire, and Togo import liquefied natural gas. Clean-energy finance was just under $10 billion a year in 2023, 2024, and 2025, against under $1.5 billion a year for fossil fuels. Banga also said Friday that the bank will support nuclear retrofits with the International Atomic Energy Agency, its first such work in 50 years.

### 11. FERC proposes a simpler review path for gas pipelines and LNG projects (Regulation)
**Summary:** Utility Dive reported Friday that the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission voted 4-1 on Thursday to propose dropping the requirement for air-dispersion modeling when it reviews gas projects under the National Environmental Policy Act. Chairman Laura Swett said the models have been a source of confusion, delay, and litigation risk, and that Clean Air Act permitting would remain with the Environmental Protection Agency and state air agencies. Commissioner Judy Chang dissented, saying the change would make it harder to judge how nearby residents are affected. The commission also proposed a three-tier blanket authorization for LNG projects: some activities could proceed automatically without a new application, some would need a 60-day notice and comment period, and additions such as liquefaction trains, tanks, and marine berths at an existing plant would need 90 days. Swett said the current limit on getting American gas to market is infrastructure bottlenecks.

**Links:**

- [Utility Dive — FERC proposes an easier path for gas pipelines and LNG projects](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-gas-pipeline-lng-blanket-authorization-permitting-reform/833234/)

**Commentary:** Thursday's vote moves air-dispersion modeling and some case-by-case applications aside, and the dissent says that weakens the view of local air impacts.
