## I. Climate and Disasters

### 1. Hurricane Isaias makes landfall in Florida at Category 2, with at least two deaths and outages that neared 900,000 (Hurricane)
**Summary:** National Hurricane Center records show Isaias came ashore near Destin, Florida, on the evening of October 9 as a Category 2 hurricane, with sustained winds of 105 mph (about 169 km/h) and a central pressure of 971 millibars, around 8:30 p.m. local time, after wind shear cut it back from a 120 mph peak. NPR confirmed on October 10 that at least two people died, one when a tree collapsed onto a home in Escambia County, Florida, and one when a tree hit a house in Jefferson County, Alabama, which includes Birmingham. As of 11:50 a.m. Eastern, about 780,000 electricity customers in Florida, Alabama, and Georgia were still without power, down from closer to 900,000 earlier; Santa Rosa, Holmes, Washington, and Walton counties each had more than 80 percent of customers out, and Governor Ron DeSantis said about 16,000 utility workers were on repairs and that the flooding was not over. Yale Climate Connections, citing Climate Central, reported Gulf waters averaging 1.4 degrees Celsius warmer than without human-caused warming and locally above 31 degrees Celsius, winds about 7 mph stronger than in that world, and about 6.5 inches of human-caused sea-level rise since 1900 in the area of the greatest storm surge.

**Commentary:** Landfall winds had already dropped from the Category 3 peak; the climate attribution is that warmer water held the storm a notch higher.

### 2. Flooding still covers 21 Thai provinces and Bangkok, with 1.13 million households and 53 deaths by dawn (Flooding)
**Summary:** The Nation reported on October 10 that Thailand's Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation said persistent heavy rain and water from the north still left flooding across 21 provinces and Bangkok, in 104 districts, 893 subdistricts, and 6,673 villages. As of 6 a.m., 1,135,377 households and 3,158,615 people were affected, with 53 deaths in the department's provincial figures, including 23 in Prachin Buri, nine in Samut Prakan, and four in Bangkok. Director-General Nirat Phongsittithaworn told provincial offices to move personnel and machinery into communities and to speed damage surveys where water had receded.

## II. Oil and Gas Supply

### 3. Gulf oil shut-ins still about 1.40 million barrels a day on Saturday morning, 69 percent of that day's output (Oil and gas)
**Summary:** The U.S. Marine Minerals Administration said on October 10 that, from reports by 31 companies as of 11:00 a.m. Central, an estimated 1,402,692 barrels a day of Gulf oil was shut in, 68.76 percent of current daily oil production there. Natural gas shut-ins were 1,229.2 million cubic feet a day, or 57.44 percent. Personnel had been evacuated from 113 of 371 manned production platforms, 30.46 percent, and from 8 of 11 non-dynamically positioned rigs. One of 17 dynamically positioned rigs had moved off location. The agency said facilities will be inspected after the storm, undamaged ones will return to production immediately once standard checks are done, and damaged ones will take longer.

### 4. Treasury issues a six-month waiver for Russian diesel; Kyiv and analysts say the volumes are too small (Diesel)
**Summary:** NPR reported on October 10 that President Trump said Friday, after a call with President Putin, that Russia would supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel immediately to the U.S. and global markets, with more to follow, and that the Treasury issued a six-month waiver within minutes. Al Jazeera listed the later tranches as 500,000 tonnes in November, 1 million tonnes after that, and another 3 million tonnes depending on refinery conditions, and cited an AAA national average of $6.20 a gallon after a September record of $6.52. ## III. Policy and Climate Accountability

### 5. Ireland cuts the carbon tax on heating oil and gas back to 2023 levels, forgoing about 177 million euros a year (Carbon tax)
**Summary:** The Irish Times reported on October 10 that this week's budget returns the carbon-tax rate on home heating oil and gas to 2023 levels for the rest of the government's term and pauses increases on petrol and diesel. Revenue falls by 177 million euros a year, about 17 percent of the 1.1 billion euros the tax raises annually, close to the 173 million euros a year the Department of Agriculture receives from it. Ministers said spending on transition supports would not drop, and Michelle Murphy of Social Justice Ireland questioned how the gap will be filled. Diarmuid Torney of Dublin City University doubted next year's pledged petrol and diesel increases will return, and Eddie Casey of the Irish Fiscal Advisory Council said short-term relief that replaces action leaves Ireland far behind its EU climate targets, with a miss that could cost the state billions.

### 7. Brazilian federal prosecutors sue Shell for about $108 million over the 2024 Rio Grande do Sul floods (Litigation)
**Summary:** The Straits Times reported on October 10, citing AFP, that Brazil's federal prosecutors sued Shell's local subsidiary on October 9 for $108 million over the April-May 2024 floods in Rio Grande do Sul, arguing liability should match an estimated 2 percent share of historical global greenhouse-gas emissions. Folha de S.Paulo reported the claim totals 541 million reais, split into 270.5 million reais each for environmental and climate-related moral damages and for material damages; the floods killed 183 people and displaced about 500,000, and the NGO Instituto Preservar is a co-plaintiff. Shell Brasil told AFP it had not received formal notice. Maria Antonia Tigre of Columbia University's Sabin Center told AFP this is the first time a national public prosecutor has sued an oil major over a climate disaster.

**Links:**

## IV. Renewables and Storage

### 8. Austria's solar subsidy draws 43,300 applications in ten minutes; the 2027 scheme will pay for storage (Solar)
**Summary:** pv magazine reported on October 10 that Austria's economy and energy ministry said Friday the year's final solar-and-storage funding round opened Thursday afternoon, and that OeMAG issued about 43,300 application tickets in the first 10 minutes, against 24,900 in the summer round. State Secretary Elisabeth Zehetner said a subsidy should not be a race to click fastest. The proposed 2027 scheme would take applications year-round against final invoices, replace investment grants with a storage bonus that requires a smart energy-management system, and require European-made inverters on storage paired with new solar; the legal change needs a two-thirds majority in parliament. Systems with smart storage invoiced or commissioned on or after November 1, 2026, could apply under the new scheme in 2027.

### 9. Australia's regulator loosens the Waratah super battery's shock-absorber contract so it can trade at full capacity (Storage)
**Summary:** RenewEconomy reported on October 10 that the Australian Energy Regulator approved a change to the System Integrity Protection Scheme contract for New South Wales's Waratah Super Battery. The Akaysha Energy battery is rated at 850 megawatts and 1,680 megawatt-hours; it had to reserve 700 megawatts and 1,400 megawatt-hours from 5 a.m. to sunset, and may now provide that service only when needed, with at least 24 hours' notice, while trading its full capacity the rest of the time. The 5.5-year contract signed in 2022, originally estimated at more than $600 million, was meant to cover the August 2025 closure of Eraring, the country's largest coal plant at 2.88 gigawatts, a date now pushed to April 2029. A share of merchant revenue will go to Transgrid and EnergyCo, lowering the contract cost paid by New South Wales customers.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** After the coal closure slipped, a battery that had been held in reserve is allowed to earn in the market and return part of that revenue to customers.
