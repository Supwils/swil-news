## I. Oil Shock and Energy Security

### 1. Hormuz traffic falls to two ships as Iran offers a conditional reopening; Brent slips below $100 (oil and gas)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on September 22 that preliminary shipping data showed only two commodity vessels crossed the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, down from 10 a day earlier. Before the Iran conflict, the waterway typically handled about 125 large commercial vessels a day and about 20% of the world’s daily crude and LNG supply. The same day, a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran could reopen the strait within seven days if the United States eases military pressure and lifts its blockade of Iranian ports, and that a proposal has been passed to Washington through mediators. On hopes of a reopening and the restart of Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, Brent crude fell below $100 a barrel to about $97.69, its first such print since September 9. Transit remains near a standstill, and pressure on refined products and freight has not disappeared.

### 2. Macron warns of a fresh oil shock and urges the EU to temporarily ease refinery and fuel rules (policy)
**Summary:** Euronews and Politico reported on September 22 that French President Emmanuel Macron wrote to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen arguing that prolonged Hormuz disruption and attacks on Middle East energy infrastructure are eroding buffers from stocks, refinery flexibility and trade diversion. France said about 14% of petrol stations were short or near-short on the morning of September 22. Macron urged temporary relaxation of EU fuel-quality specs on density, volatility and desulphurisation, saying plants could raise jet-fuel and diesel output by about 5%–20%, and proposed delaying some methane-reporting obligations for fossil importers by one year to January 1, 2028. He warned that if Hormuz stays effectively closed and Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline does not return, depletion of Saudi stocks could remove at least another 4 million barrels a day from global supply. The Commission said potential shortages are “extremely high on the agenda” and will be discussed soon with industry and member states.

### 3. Brussels seeks powers to order electricity demand cuts and block national measures in a crisis (power grids)
**Summary:** Euronews reported on September 22 that, according to draft plans it has seen, the European Commission wants more hands-on emergency powers in a major electricity crisis: authority to declare a regional or EU-wide electricity alarm, require countries to cut consumption by a specified amount, and block national measures that threaten energy supplies elsewhere in Europe. The rethink follows fresh supply and price shocks from the US-Israeli war against Iran, alongside risks from extreme weather, cyberattacks and physical attacks on infrastructure. The draft also calls for more detailed short-term and seasonal adequacy assessments covering plant outages, transmission failures, demand peaks and renewable swings. As electrification deepens, the power system grows more exposed to weather and import shocks, and Brussels is pushing crisis coordination from soft coordination toward enforceable tools.

**Links:**

- [Euronews — Brussels seeks power to order electricity demand cuts in energy crisis](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/09/22/brussels-seeks-power-to-order-electricity-demand-cuts-in-energy-crisis)

**Commentary:** Once electricity is the economy’s main fuel, the EU crisis toolkit expands from gas pipelines to the demand “kill switch.”

## II. Climate Diplomacy, US Policy and Litigation

### 4. UN chief says Big Oil treats the atmosphere as an “open sewer” (climate diplomacy)
**Summary:** AFP/France 24 reported on September 22 that outgoing UN Secretary-General António Guterres told a UN high-level meeting that major oil companies have for decades treated the atmosphere as an “open sewer” and cashed in on the consequences. He said five major Western oil companies have pocketed nearly half a trillion dollars in profits since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, compared with about $34 billion mobilised to help developing countries adapt, while households pay more for energy, food and insurance. The speech came after record northern-hemisphere summer heat, deadly flooding in Nepal linked to glacial collapse, and UN warnings that the world will at least temporarily breach the Paris 1.5°C goal. Guterres also pointed to the solar and wind boom as grounds for hope and was due to host a climate summit the next day ahead of COP31 in Antalya in November.

### 5. Sierra Club’s Dirty Truth report again gives major US utilities an F on clean transition (policy)
**Summary:** Canary Media reported on September 22 that the Sierra Club’s latest Dirty Truth Report gave 76 of the largest US electric utilities an overall F for the second year running, and said more than four in 10 (31 utilities) have backtracked on climate goals since the start of the second Trump administration. For the first time in the report’s six-year history, the volume of new clean energy planned by utilities fell from the prior year. Planned gas capacity remains large, while clean energy in utility plans is expected to cover only about 25% of load growth by 2035, down from about 32% last year and about 52% in 2024. Grades rest on retiring coal, building no new gas plants, and rapidly expanding clean power to replace fossils and meet rising demand.

### 6. New York leads eight states suing to block about $1.4 billion in offshore-wind lease buyouts (offshore wind)
**Summary:** The New Jersey Globe and related New York attorney-general materials dated September 22 said New York Attorney General Letitia James is leading Connecticut, Delaware, Maine, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Rhode Island and Vermont in lawsuits against the Interior and Justice departments challenging settlements with Bluepoint Wind and Invenergy that would cancel critical offshore-wind leases in exchange for more than $1.4 billion in federal funds redirected toward conventional energy. Bluepoint’s New York Bight lease contemplated about 2.4 GW; Invenergy projects also target Northeast load centres. The states argue the deals violate the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, Administrative Procedure Act, NEPA and Judgment Fund rules, and ask courts to void them and restore the leases. California Attorney General Rob Bonta filed a parallel challenge the same day over an about $111 million Morro Bay buyout.

## III. China: Clean Power, Weather Resilience and Environmental Governance

### 8. After solar tops coal by capacity, extreme weather sharpens China’s “weather-dependent” power anxiety (China)
**Summary:** People’s Daily Online reported on September 22, citing National Energy Administration data, that China’s installed solar capacity reached 1.286 billion kilowatts by the end of July, edging past coal’s 1.285 billion and accounting for 31.5% of total installed generation—making solar the largest source by capacity. Capacity is not generation, and coal remains the near-term backbone for support and balancing. The same day, China Youth Daily covered a meteorology-economy forum at the China International Fair for Trade in Services where Tsinghua’s Luo Yong and others argued that rising wind and solar shares make the energy system more “weather-dependent,” like agriculture, with climate change now threatening generation, grids, demand and storage. CMA scientist Shen Yanbo said extreme heat has already pushed national power load to new records eight times this year, with each 1°C rise in peak temperature adding roughly 40–50 GW of load, so new power-system resilience design must treat climate change as a core input.

## IV. Renewables and Storage

Northwest Europe’s large-scale storage pipeline is moving from paper plans into gigawatt-hour FIDs and groundbreaking.

## V. Extreme Weather and Disasters

### 12. Typhoon Dujuan hits eastern Japan with record rain, deaths and mass evacuations (disaster)
**Summary:** Reuters, Al Jazeera and The Independent reported on September 22 that Typhoon Dujuan brought torrential rain and landslides to Japan’s Pacific coast. Reuters, citing NHK, said at least two people were killed and four missing, with power cut to nearly 50,000 homes; The Independent put the death toll at four with at least six missing. Oshima recorded 832 mm of rain in 48 hours, a local record, while parts of Chiba also saw more than 470 mm over 48 hours. Authorities issued evacuation advisories for more than 1.6 million people; rail lines and some Tokyo–Osaka bullet services were disrupted, and Japan Airlines and ANA cancelled hundreds of flights. The meteorological agency warned that even after the storm moved offshore, saturated ground could still trigger landslides and river flooding.
