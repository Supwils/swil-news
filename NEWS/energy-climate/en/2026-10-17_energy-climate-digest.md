## I. Policy, Climate Finance, and Technology Controls

### 1. Ahead of COP31, more than $5 billion in Pacific climate finance remains unpaid (Policy)
**Summary:** RFI reported on October 17 that COP31 will run from November 9 to 20 in Antalya, with Turkish environment minister Murat Kurum as president and Australian climate and energy minister Chris Bowen leading the negotiations. At the Fiji pre-meeting, the World Meteorological Organization warned that sea temperatures in a key equatorial stretch of the Pacific are forecast to average about 3.7C above normal from October to December, a record for the El Nino index and a December peak, while organizers said more than $5 billion of the more than $16 billion committed to Pacific countries since 2015 remains unpaid. EU finance ministers on Friday reaffirmed mobilizing at least $300 billion a year by 2035 and tripling adaptation finance by then, but a draft strategy from Ireland, the rotating presidency, says there is no scope for new climate-funding commitments at this summit. The hosts prioritize a voluntary rise in electricity's share of global final energy from about 20 percent to 35 percent by 2035 without specifying the source of the extra power; Climate Action Tracker found wind and solar supplied 22 percent of Turkey's electricity in 2025, fossil fuels still 56 percent, and power-sector emissions at a record.

### 2. China opens consultation on export controls covering 40 technologies, including 12 in solar manufacturing (Solar)
**Summary:** China Economic Net on October 17 carried a Xinhua report that the Ministry of Commerce and the Ministry of Science and Technology issued Announcement No. 48 of 2026 on October 16, opening consultation on the export catalogue and imposing interim pre-export registration. The draft covers 40 technologies in artificial intelligence, biomedicine, and photovoltaics, with 14 proposed for prohibition and 26 for restriction; solar accounts for 12, covering polysilicon, wafers, cells, and key equipment that carries the controlled know-how. The spokesperson cited perovskite and tandem-module production as a proposed ban and polysilicon engineering of at least 30,000 tonnes a year on a single line as a proposed restriction. Comments close on November 15 and the catalogue is not yet in force, but listed exports, including deals already under way, must be registered until implementation; pv magazine's reading of the draft says the proposed perovskite-silicon tandem ban covers processes and related equipment for wafers of at least 182 mm and efficiency of at least 30 percent, while finished modules sit outside that scope.

- [China Economic Net — Commerce Ministry explains the proposed export-catalogue revision](http://www.ce.cn/xwzx/gnsz/gdxw/202610/t20261017_3261081.shtml)

### 3. China Electric Power Research Institute completes a full-chain power carbon-footprint platform (Carbon footprint)
**Summary:** People's Daily Online on October 17, citing Science and Technology Daily, reported that the China Electric Power Research Institute has built a carbon-footprint accounting system and platform covering generation, transmission, distribution, and end use, so that the life-cycle emissions of one kilowatt-hour can be quantified. The team built a database across power sources, grids, electrical equipment, and transmission projects, using a three-level method that runs from the system to the project to the device, plus a model for transmission and distribution footprint factors. The tools are already in use in Sichuan and Anhui, where provincial electricity footprint factors have been calculated for local product accounting. The report does not publish the factor values.

**Links:**

- [People's Daily Online — Full-chain power-system carbon-footprint platform completed](http://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1017/c1004-40816512.html)

**Commentary:** Saturday's announcement is that the accounting tool is operating in two provinces, not that a publishable set of provincial factors is now available.

## II. Renewables, Storage, and Projects

### 4. First foundation poured on the Guangxi section of the Tibet-Guangdong HVDC line, aimed at 2029 (Transmission)
**Summary:** 21st Century Business Herald reported on October 17 that on October 16 crews began pouring the first tower foundation of the Guangxi section, in Nanning, of the ±800 kV HVDC line from southeastern Tibet to the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area. Approved dynamic investment is about 53 billion yuan, with converter stations in Qamdo, Nyingchi, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen, a route of more than 2,600 kilometres, and a four-terminal voltage-source design; the Guangxi section is about 678 kilometres through seven cities, including Baise, Nanning, and Wuzhou. Some sections are expected to move to tower erection by year-end, and the full line is planned for 2029. The report says delivered power will be entirely clean, sending more than 43 billion kilowatt-hours a year to the Greater Bay Area, displacing about 12 million tonnes of standard coal and avoiding about 33 million tonnes of carbon dioxide, and CCTV News carried the same milestone the same day.

### 5. France plans to ease the grid-connection cap for solar-plus-storage projects up to 25 MW (Storage)
**Summary:** pv magazine reported on October 17 that the French government plans, by the end of 2026, to let solar-plus-storage projects of up to 25 MW of installed capacity use an exemption from the current 17 MW connection limit, provided grid injection stays at or below 17 MW. The step follows a first package in April. Montel reported that the government stated the connection change on Thursday. The same package would tie heat-pump subsidies to smart controls from 2027, with estimated household savings of 150 to 200 euros a year, and would switch smart control on by default for new chargers, saving an estimated 70 to 100 euros a year. Industry pledges through 2030 include remote control in five million EDF households, 1 GW of flexibility from Neoen, a 1 billion euro investment and 1.5 GW more flexibility from NW Group, and 1.5 million connected devices, about 5 to 6 GW, at Octopus Energy France.

**Links:**

- [pv magazine — France to ease grid connection rules for solar-plus-storage projects up to 25 MW](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/10/17/france-to-ease-grid-connection-rules-for-solar-plus-storage-projects-up-to-25-mw/)
- [Montel — France eases grid connection rules for solar-battery projects](https://montelnews.com/nordic/news/569e47db-4a84-471e-99bf-6946d1378efe/france-eases-grid-connection-rules-for-solar-battery-projects)

### 6. Brazil's new curtailment rules add clarity but are unlikely to restart utility-scale solar (Solar)
**Summary:** pv magazine reported on October 17, citing the Brazilian solar association and regulator data, that developers surrendered 923 solar authorizations in 2026, more than 40.6 GW and 134 billion reais (about $26.3 billion). Aneel figures show authorized potential capacity fell from 110 GW across 2,492 plants in January to 60 GW, while average solar curtailment in 2026 was 1.4 GW, or 25.3 percent of solar generation. July's Ordinance 140 compensates curtailment from September 1, 2023, to November 25, 2025, for 682 solar plants and about 25.5 GW, but excludes oversupply events, which already make up most curtailment. Resolution 1,170 of October 6 sets how future cuts are shared across technologies, and Eduardo Tobias of Watt Capital said that clarity alone is unlikely to restart a large wave of new wind and solar projects.

**Links:**

- [pv magazine — Brazil's new curtailment rules unlikely to revive utility-scale solar investment](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/10/17/brazils-new-curtailment-rules-unlikely-to-revive-utility-scale-solar-investment/)

**Commentary:** The new rules say how the curtailment pie will be divided; they do not say how large that pie will be.

### 7. Rooftop solar accelerates in the Philippines as power bills rise (Solar)
**Summary:** Al Jazeera reported from Manila on October 17 that Ember estimates rooftop panels in the Philippines have nearly doubled since early 2025, and that Chinese panel imports from March to May reached $407 million, the largest single overseas market in that window, as the war on Iran lifted power prices. One household in the report saw its bill rise from about $150 to as much as $220; a system can cost up to about $5,000, with a stated payback of four to five years. Imported coal and gas supply more than 70 percent of the mix, the peso has fallen past 63 per dollar, and President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. declared a national energy emergency in late March, while rooftop loans launched that month cover only public-sector workers. Officials point to a 3,500-hectare solar farm north of Manila, expected at 850 MW for about 2.4 million households when fully operating.

**Links:**

### 10. Australia is on track for its wettest El Nino year on record (Extreme weather)
**Summary:** ABC meteorologist Tom Saunders wrote on October 17 that 2026 remains on track to be Australia's wettest El Nino year on record and the first of 27 El Nino years since 1900 with above-average national winter-spring rainfall; this winter was already the wettest El Nino winter in 35 years. Sydney has had more than 100 millimetres this week, central Queensland has seen severe flash flooding, and storms brought destructive winds around Alice Springs after a high stalled in the southern Tasman Sea on Friday. Over about the next eight days, most of Tasmania, Victoria, South Australia, New South Wales, the Northern Territory, and Queensland can expect at least some rain, with weekly totals above 50 millimetres possible in parts of Queensland and northern New South Wales and more than 100 millimetres, about four times the October average, possible in the Maranoa and Warrego. The Bureau of Meteorology outlook favors a wetter-than-normal November across most of the country, while summer odds are near even for most areas.

### 11. More than a dozen beachfront homes destroyed on California's Long Beach Peninsula (Extreme weather)
**Summary:** NBC News reported on October 17 that more than a dozen beachfront homes on Southern California's Long Beach Peninsula were destroyed by surf between October 9 and 11, with eight structures covering 13 homes judged unsafe to enter after remnants of Tropical Storm Rachel raised waves to about 17 feet (about 5.2 metres). Remnants of Hurricane Marie in early September and Hurricane Polo in late September had already produced waves of about 7 feet, and last weekend swell from Malibu to San Diego flooded parts of Newport Beach and closed a section of the Pacific Coast Highway near Laguna Beach. Timu Gallien of UCLA's Coastal Flood Lab said the expected strong El Nino does not peak until early next year and that three more rounds of seasonal high tides remain this year. Climate scientist Daniel Swain said coastal flooding is continuing and could reach Northern California in November and December, and Long Beach mayor Rex Richardson said last week that the city's infrastructure was not built for this.
