## I. Oil Markets and Permitting

### 1. Brent settles at $102.25 as about 325 million of the March pledge has been released (Oil and Gas)
**Summary:** Euronews reported on October 3 that International Energy Agency Executive Director Fatih Birol told Friday's G7 leaders' video meeting that about 325 million barrels of the 400 million pledged in the March 11 collective action have been released, more than 80 percent, leaving about 75 million. The G7 announced Friday that it would release another 100 million barrels of diesel and crude through the IEA, but it did not say whether that 100 million includes the unfinished 75 million or sits on top of it. The Business Times, citing Reuters, said Brent settled down $0.06 at $102.25 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate fell $1.76 to $91.11. For the week, Brent was up 0.1 percent and WTI was down 1.6 percent. Sources said France had proposed that Europe release 50 million barrels of diesel and that IEA members release 50 million barrels of crude, but the report said it was not clear whether the G7 adopted that split. Barclays raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast by $20 to $115 a barrel and lifted its 2026 forecast to $100.

### 2. A bipartisan U.S. Senate permitting bill is pushed to the post-election lame-duck session (Policy)
**Summary:** NCIJ Network reported on October 3 that the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, advanced by Environment and Public Works Chair Shelley Moore Capito, Republican of West Virginia, ranking member Sheldon Whitehouse, Democrat of Rhode Island, and Energy Committee leaders Mike Lee, Republican of Utah, and Martin Heinrich, Democrat of New Mexico, will not get a vote before the midterms and is headed to the lame-duck session. The bill would require AI data centers to pay in full for grid upgrades built to serve them. Modeling by the Center for Climate and Energy Solutions and Greenline Insights said the reforms could cut carbon dioxide by 9 percent in the four regional grids studied, and nationwide could reduce cumulative grid costs by $125 billion and residential bills by $19 billion between 2026 and 2040. North American Clean Energy reported the same day that the bill would also require grid operators to open wholesale markets to local networks of home batteries and rooftop solar within 18 months, and would create a $20 million-a-year Energy Department program to help local governments approve rooftop solar, home batteries, and EV chargers faster. The Western Environmental Law Center, before the text was released, called the compromise a "five-alarm fire" because it would limit state and tribal use of the Clean Water Act for issues unrelated to direct water pollution.

## II. Green Power, Peaking Plants, and the Grid

### 3. Vietnam's power demand is growing about 10 percent a year, and an electricity-law amendment is headed to the October session (Policy)
**Summary:** VietnamPlus reported on October 3 that Deputy Minister of Industry and Trade Truong Thanh Hoai told that day's regular government press conference that national electricity demand is growing about 10 percent a year, while new plants and lines still take a long time, so the ministry is prioritizing rooftop solar, which is quick to build and supplies power on site, along with batteries. Vietnam Electricity puts national rooftop solar at about 10,000 MWp. The Electricity Law asks provincial people's councils to adopt household rooftop-solar support, and only 13 provinces have done so. The deputy minister said Power Development Plan VIII has been slow, the ministry will review and adjust it under National Assembly Resolution 253 of December 11, 2025, and Government Decree 272 of 2026, and it will submit amendments to the 2024 Electricity Law at the National Assembly session opening in October.

### 4. Four 660 MW units at Ningxia's Zhongwei plant reach full operation for the Tengger desert base (Peaking power)
**Summary:** China Energy said on October 3 that Unit 6 at the Zhongwei plant passed its 168-hour trial run on October 2, bringing all four 660 MW units of the peaking supply for the country's first desert-gobi-wasteland renewable base to full operation. The Ningxia Tengger base, tied to the Ningxia-Hunan ±800 kV ultra-high-voltage line, is planned with 13 GW of renewables, including 4 GW of wind and 9 GW of solar, plus 4.64 GW of peaking coal. The four units are designed to generate 11.8 TWh a year and to support stable export of 22 TWh of clean electricity, equivalent to about 7.1 million tonnes of standard coal saved and 18.46 million tonnes of carbon dioxide avoided. Their automatic generation control ramp rate is 2.27 percent of rated power per minute, above the 2.2 percent required of new coal units. Construction started on April 26, 2024, and all four units were commissioned within nine months of the first unit in January.

**Commentary:** Power leaving the desert bases still leans on coal for balance; these four units are there to follow wind and solar, not to replace them.

### 5. Ordos "photovoltaic great wall" has 12.84 GW installed and 800,000 mu under sand control (Solar)
**Summary:** People's Daily reported on October 3 that the photovoltaic great wall on the northern edge of the Kubuqi Desert in Dalad Banner, Ordos, Inner Mongolia, runs about 400 kilometres and averages about 5 kilometres wide. Nearly 200,000 panels form a horse-shaped array certified by Guinness World Records as the world's largest photovoltaic graphic power station. Li Kai, director of the energy security center at the Dalad Banner energy bureau, said that at full build-out the project could generate 40 TWh of green electricity a year, save 13 million tonnes of standard coal, cut 32 million tonnes of carbon dioxide and 9 million tonnes of dust, and control sand across 840,000 mu with panels plus 1.28 million mu of ecological treatment. So far, 12.84 GW is installed and 800,000 mu of photovoltaic sand control is in place. The project is listed among the ecology ministry's green and low-carbon cases. Ordos forest cover has risen from 3 percent to 27.42 percent, and grassland vegetation cover is steady at about 50 percent.

## III. Climate Litigation and Extreme Weather

### 6. The U.S. Supreme Court hears Boulder v. ExxonMobil and Suncor on Monday (Climate litigation)
**Summary:** The Associated Press reported on October 3 that on Monday, the first day of the new term, the Supreme Court will hear the case brought by the city and county of Boulder, Colorado, against Suncor Energy and ExxonMobil. The governments sued in 2018, arguing that the companies violated state law by deceiving the public about their contribution to climate change, and they seek unspecified damages to help pay for worsening disasters. The 2021 Marshall Fire caused about $2 billion in damage, the costliest wildfire in state history, and climate change was considered a factor. The companies and the Trump administration say state-court suits are a back door around the Clean Air Act. Justice Samuel Alito recused himself a week before arguments because he owns oil-company stock, so eight justices will sit and a tie is possible. The Court is also considering whether it has jurisdiction at this stage.

**Links:**

- [The Seattle Times / AP — Supreme Court will hear a major climate case tied to a Colorado wildfire](https://www.seattletimes.com/business/the-supreme-court-will-hear-a-major-climate-change-case-involving-a-devastating-colorado-wildfire/)

**Commentary:** Eight justices will decide whether a state court can make oil companies pay for local climate losses, and a tie would leave the question where it is.

### 7. Bangkok declares a flood disaster in all 50 districts, with about 40 dead (Extreme weather)
**Summary:** The Guardian reported on October 3 that parts of the Thai capital received about 300 millimetres of rain in roughly two days, about half a year of average rainfall in London, and streets turned into stagnant lakes. The government said about 40 people died in flood-related incidents, including drowning and electrocution. A disaster has been declared in all 50 districts, and thousands of people stayed in more than 230 shelters. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said the metropolitan area was still at risk from water flowing in from the north, a war room had been set up, and the weather agency forecast more heavy rain next week. Consultant Naim Laeni, who has studied Bangkok's flood resilience, said drainage canals can handle about 60 millimetres an hour, and about 80 millimetres in upgraded sections, but this rain lasted for days. At Suvarnabhumi airport, about half of Thai Airways' workforce could not report at one point, luggage piled up, and flights were cancelled. The same weather system triggered a landslide in southern Myanmar that killed nine people in one village, with eight more missing in another.

### 9. Nepal plans to lower four high-risk glacial lakes after a disaster that killed more than 1,400 (Glaciers)
**Summary:** ABC published a Reuters report on October 3: Nepal will begin design work and detailed studies this fiscal year, which runs until mid-July 2027, on four central and eastern glacial lakes — Thulagi, Lower Barun, Lumding Tsho, and Hongu II — to reduce the risk of an outburst. Sanjeeb Baral, executive director of the Centre for Hydrology and Water Resources Research, said drainage through gated structures would start after the study and could take up to six years. The work would also install sensors, flood forecasting and warning, and downstream community preparation. Centre expert Subash Tuladhar said the $50 million project would be funded by the Green Climate Fund, the UN Development Programme, and the government. The lakes sit between 4,050 and 5,483 metres. A disaster set off by a glacier collapse in August killed more than 1,400 people, and rescuers are still searching for more than 5,000 people missing in the border region with China. Officials said early recovery would need nearly $5 billion. Glaciologist Samjwal Ratna Bajracharya said lowering the lakes by 3 metres would significantly reduce the threat.
