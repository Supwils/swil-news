### 1. Volkswagen recalls 22,524 ID.4s and advises an 80% charge limit until a fix (Battery safety / United States)
**Summary:** Electrek and Kelley Blue Book reported on October 8 that Volkswagen is recalling 22,524 ID.4s from model years 2023 through 2026. The NHTSA campaign is 26V630 and Volkswagen's internal number is 93EX. Both reports say the high-voltage battery may catch fire and that a remedy is still under development; until the repair is done, owners should keep the charge limit at 80% or below. Owner letters are expected on November 27, with a second letter once a fix is available. Electrek also wrote that Volkswagen told the regulator in an October 6 letter that the root cause is still unknown, that charging the high-voltage battery above 80% in some vehicles could trigger thermal propagation, that it had received 22 U.S. claims between January 18, 2024, and August 7, 2026, and that its product safety committee had asked cell supplier SK Battery America for further clarification on production and a possible clean date.

### 4. A Science paper says correcting the math turns the U.S. vehicle-emissions rollback into a roughly $670 billion net cost (Emissions policy / United States)
**Summary:** The Associated Press reported on October 8 that a group of economists, writing in Science, found errors in how the Trump administration accounted for fuel savings, technology prices, and mileage when it scrapped greenhouse-gas standards for cars and trucks earlier this year. An EPA cost-benefit analysis had estimated that rescinding the standards would save Americans $600 billion to $790 billion over the coming decades. The authors include more than a dozen researchers from institutions including Yale, MIT, and the University of Pennsylvania. Antonio Bento, a professor of public policy and economics at the University of Southern California, said that even setting the environmental debate aside, the economic case for the rollback falls apart. The authors calculate that correcting several errors would cut the estimated benefit by $1.5 trillion, so that eliminating the rules would cost the country $670 billion. They say the EPA credited car buyers with only 23 cents of every dollar of fuel savings. The EPA said in an email that it stands by its analysis and will not push an EV mandate on the basis of what it called facially implausible projections.

**Links:**

### 11. Chalmers models a pack that bypasses weak cells, with lifetime gains above 20% only as an upper bound (Batteries / Sweden)
**Summary:** Chalmers University of Technology published a news article on October 8 on a Nature Communications study, done with industry partners, of a reconfigurable battery pack that uses switches to bypass a cell that has begun to limit the pack. In the most fine-grained, idealized models, lifetime for certain high-voltage vehicles could extend by more than 20%. The university stresses that this is a theoretical upper bound and not what today's commercial systems should be expected to achieve. A representative case assumes an 80 kWh battery, 12,000 kilometres a year, and a vehicle life of 18.8 years; under those assumptions, battery replacement is deferred by about 14 months. A component-level estimate using automotive-grade hardware puts the extra hardware at about 9% of pack cost, with a break-even threshold around 12%. If the added cost stays below roughly 7% and annual mileage stays below 12,150 kilometres, the architecture is cost-effective in at least 99.7% of the scenarios tested. A privately owned car in Sweden is driven about 11,400 kilometres a year. The university says no mass-produced passenger car or truck yet uses this architecture.

**Links:**

## IV. Supply Chain

### 12. Floods halt more than 10 Thai parts plants; Toyota's October 12 restart is provisional (Supply chain / Thailand)
**Summary:** The Nation reported on October 8, citing the Federation of Thai Industries, that flooding halted more than 10 auto-parts factories at the Amata City Chonburi industrial estate, with suppliers expecting to restart deliveries from October 12. Supoj Sukpisan, honorary chairman of the Auto Parts Industry Club, said suppliers that cannot resume in time may have to import components to meet contracts, at higher cost, and that changing a supplier can take almost a year, especially for pickup trucks for which Thailand is the main base. Suwat Supakarndechakul, chairman of the Automotive Industry Club, said Toyota Motor Thailand has provisionally planned to restart on October 12, pause for the October 13 holiday, and continue on October 14, subject to another assessment. The disruption began on September 30 when flooding kept employees from work, then deepened as parts ran short; four Toyota plants, including Ban Pho in Chachoengsao, were temporarily suspended. Suwat said no assembly plant was directly flooded, Chinese and European carmakers were operating normally because they use different suppliers, and most Japanese manufacturers were affected. The Industry Ministry said five vehicle manufacturers had temporarily stopped because parts could not be made or delivered, or employees could not reach work. After an earthen embankment failed, 34 plants in zones 7, 8, and 9 stopped, more than 10 of them auto-parts factories. Nikkei Asia reported the same day from Chonburi that the rains had forced Toyota, Honda, and other carmakers to suspend operations.
