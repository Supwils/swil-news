## I. Autonomous Driving

### 1. Tesla Cybercabs authorized for commercial use in Texas rise to 169, with Austin still the only city in service (Autonomous driving / United States)
**Summary:** CNBC reported on October 3 that, since Tesla launched the steering-wheel-free, pedal-free Cybercab in Austin on September 3, the number authorized for commercial use in Texas has risen from 45 at the unveiling to 169 as of Friday, according to public records of the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles. Austin is the only city where the Cybercab is part of the driverless ride-hail service. Tesla's Robotaxi fleet in Texas also includes 420 Model Y vehicles equipped with Tesla automated driving systems that are not yet available to individual buyers. Paying passengers have given mixed reviews, including long waits, pickups and drop-offs in the wrong place, and butterfly doors or trunks that fail to close properly. NHTSA opened an audit query after the launch and originally required answers by September 30; the company received an extension. CNBC said no significant safety incidents or collisions involving a Cybercab have been reported in Austin since commercial service began.

### 3. Minneapolis mayor vetoes an ordinance that would have required a human safety monitor in robotaxis (Regulation / United States)
**Summary:** CBS Minnesota reported on October 2 that the Minneapolis City Council voted 7-6 on Thursday for an ordinance requiring a person in the driver's seat of a robotaxi. Mayor Jacob Frey wrote to the council on Friday and vetoed Council Action 2026-034. The veto letter says the ordinance ignores curbside management, emergency response, traffic enforcement, liability and insurance, and privacy and data reporting, and instead requires a driver to sit in driverless vehicles. Frey said that cancels out the safety and reliability benefits for riders who prefer a driverless vehicle, and he called it a back-door ban. Overriding the veto would take nine votes. Alpha News reported on October 3 that the ordinance said no autonomous rideshare vehicle could operate on public roads without a human safety monitor in the driver's seat with immediate access to steering, braking, and acceleration, and that it would not have taken effect until October 2027. Autonomous ride services are not yet available in Minnesota, and Waymo is in the process of bringing service to Minneapolis.

**Links:**

## II. Charging and Energy

### 5. Holiday new-energy travel is about 1.8 times a normal day, and State Grid has placed 599 emergency charging connectors (Charging / China)
**Summary:** China News Service on October 3 carried a CCTV report: reporters learned from the National Energy Administration on October 2 that highway charging on the first day of the holiday reached 28.0469 million kilowatt-hours, up 60.4% from the first day of the 2025 National Day holiday and a single-day holiday record. Daily new-energy travel during the holiday is about 1.8 times a normal day, and highway charging is dispersed on the way out and concentrated on the return. Vehicles at service-area chargers rise sharply from about 10:00 and ease after 21:00. Return-trip peaks are 11:00 to 21:00 on October 5 and 10:00 to 22:00 on October 6. Shi Shuanglong, director of the monitoring center at State Grid Smart Internet of Vehicles Technology, said hot service areas are encouraging drivers to leave at 80% charge and guiding them to public chargers in towns near highway exits. State Grid has placed 599 emergency charging connectors at highway service areas and 32 mobile emergency charging vehicles where transformer capacity is spare. Named busy sites include Leting and Tanghai on the Hebei section of the Qinbin Expressway, Pengcheng on the Jiangsu section of the Lianhuo Expressway, and Luohe on the Henan section of the Beijing-Hong Kong-Macao Expressway.

### 9. European automakers ask for more time on Made-in-Europe battery localization (Batteries / Europe)
**Summary:** On September 30 the European Automobile Manufacturers' Association said a study it commissioned from Mobility Global expects European battery-cell production to grow significantly, to 306 GWh by 2032, and still not catch demand on the localization timeline envisaged in the Industrial Accelerator Act. A new battery plant can take close to a decade from announcement to full production; a project announced today, even without delays, may not reach full industrial scale until 2035. The association said about two-thirds of the value in cell supply chains sits upstream, in raw materials, mining, refining, and components. Bruegel estimates that sourcing EU-produced cells could raise the cost of a battery-electric car by 2,100 euros. The act currently envisages requiring three EU-origin battery components, including cells, six months after adoption, rising three years later to five components, including cells, battery management systems, and cathode active materials. ACEA is asking for three years after adoption for the first requirement and five years for the second, with separate treatment for trucks and buses. Automotive News reported the call on October 3 under the headline that European automakers want a delay to Made-in-Europe EV battery rules.

**Links:**

- [ACEA — Building in Europe takes time. Batteries included.](https://www.acea.auto/message-dg/building-in-europe-takes-time-batteries-included/)
- [Automotive News — European automakers call for delay on ‘Made in Europe’ EV battery rules](https://www.autonews.com/manufacturing/suppliers/ane-acea-battery-local-content-iaa-1003/)

**Commentary:** The request is to move the six-month and three-year thresholds to three years and five years, while keeping the localization requirement.

### 10. FedEx orders 2,000 electric medium-duty trucks from Harbinger in a deal worth more than $300 million (Fleets / United States)
**Summary:** Harbinger announced on October 1 that it has received an order from FedEx for 2,000 all-electric trucks, with a total value exceeding $300 million, which it said is believed to be one of the largest binding orders for electric medium- or heavy-duty trucks. The vehicles are planned for delivery by the end of 2027 for FedEx pickup and delivery operations in the United States and Canada. Harbinger plans to build and deliver all 2,000 within about 18 months, with Canadian dealer Kaizen Automotive Group supporting the Canadian portion. Harbinger estimates that each truck reduces fuel cost by about $20,000 a year versus the diesel vehicle it replaces, or about $40 million a year for 2,000 trucks and about $800 million over a typical 20-year life for Class 5 and 6 medium-duty trucks. Using published U.S. Department of Energy data, it estimates the replacement avoids more than 1.7 million tons of carbon dioxide over those operating lives. The order follows an earlier FedEx deployment and FedEx's co-lead of Harbinger's $160 million Series C. Paul Melander, FedEx senior vice president for safety and transportation and a Harbinger director, said electrifying a fleet at this scale requires vehicles that can do the work and deliver real economic benefits.
