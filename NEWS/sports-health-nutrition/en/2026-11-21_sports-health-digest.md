## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. DIRECT-PLUS secondary analysis: a green Mediterranean diet cut pancreatic fat ~14% over 18 months (Nutrition)

**Summary:** Ben-Gurion University–led investigators published an MRI post-hoc secondary analysis of DIRECT-PLUS in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. About 294 adults with abdominal obesity or dyslipidemia were randomized to guideline-based healthy eating, a standard Mediterranean diet, or a green Mediterranean (Green-MED) pattern emphasizing polyphenol-rich plants, green tea and the aquatic plant Mankai, with less red and processed meat; all arms included physical-activity support. Roughly 290 had usable baseline scans and 233 completed 18-month MRI. Pancreatic fat fell about 14% relative to baseline on Green-MED (~2.4 percentage points), about 6.7% on standard Mediterranean, and about 0.8% on guidelines; pancreatic volume did not shrink significantly. Greater pancreatic-fat loss tracked with improved insulin-resistance measures after adjustment for visceral fat and weight change; intakes of >3 Mankai servings/week or >5.5 walnut servings/week linked to larger drops, while higher refined-carbohydrate intake linked to smaller drops.

**Links:**

- [Ben-Gurion University — Green Mediterranean Diet Clears Pancreatic Fat](https://www.bgu.ac.il/en/news-and-articles/green-mediterranean-diet-clears-pancreatic-fat/)
- [AJCN — Green-Mediterranean diet and pancreatic fat loss (DIRECT-PLUS)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101513)

**Commentary:** This was an exploratory secondary analysis in a mostly male sample—not a Mankai/walnut prescription or a diabetes-prevention claim; prioritize cutting red/processed meat and raising polyphenol-rich plants and nuts over chasing one “superfood.”

### 2. Stanford WHI analysis: saturated fat’s links to LDL and ASCVD were stronger in women at high genetic risk (Nutrition)

**Summary:** A Stanford Medicine–led study published November 18 in JACC: Advances analyzed about 20,940 genotyped Women’s Health Initiative participants with diet questionnaires. A 5% isocaloric increase in saturated-fat calories (replacing carbohydrate) associated with about 1.73 mg/dL higher LDL-C overall, and that association was modified by an LDL polygenic score (P interaction = 0.01). Among 16,835 women without baseline ASCVD followed a median ~10 years, the same 5% increase associated with higher ASCVD risk in the top PGS quartile (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.08–1.47) but not in the bottom three quartiles (HR 1.00). Authors stressed observational design, female-only data, and that polygenic scores are not yet ready to prescribe diets.

**Links:**

- [Stanford Medicine — Diet significantly swings cholesterol for some](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/11/cholesterol-diet.html)
- [JACC: Advances — Saturated fat intake and LDL-C across the genetic spectrum](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.103423)

**Commentary:** Genetic predisposition does not make diet irrelevant—those with higher genetic LDL propensity may be the ones who benefit most from limiting saturated fat; keep guideline limits on red meat and butter rather than treating “my lipids don’t move” as a free pass.

### 3. Crossover trial: nutritionally matched ultraprocessed meals still drove a larger insulin surge (Nutrition)

**Summary:** A Nature Metabolism randomized crossover study enrolled 57 healthy-weight adults (32 completed both metabolic sessions) comparing ~300 kcal ultraprocessed (UPF) versus non-UPF meals tightly matched on energy, macronutrients, glycemic index/load, fiber and sodium. Three-hour glucose AUC was similar, but insulin AUC was significantly higher after the UPF meal, with higher insulin from 40–120 minutes. Energy expenditure was slightly higher after UPF, yet carbohydrate oxidation was relatively blunted and fat oxidation higher. Liking and fullness ratings did not differ meaningfully, suggesting processing can change postprandial metabolism beyond calories and macros.

**Links:**

- [Nature Metabolism — Metabolic and neural responses to ultraprocessed foods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-026-01619-4)
- [PsyPost — Ultraprocessed foods trigger a larger insulin surge](https://www.psypost.org/even-when-calories-and-nutrients-match-ultraprocessed-foods-trigger-a-larger-insulin-surge/)

**Commentary:** Acute meal physiology is not chronic-disease proof; day to day, swap ultraprocessed snacks and ready meals for whole-food equivalents rather than trusting a “macro-matched” label alone.

### 4. CHNS cohort: higher long-term Chinese Healthy Eating Index scores linked to lower incident obesity (Nutrition)

**Summary:** Frontiers in Nutrition analyzed China Health and Nutrition Survey data from Hubei: 1,527 adults ≥20 years without obesity at baseline, median follow-up about 14.0 years (23,193 person-years), with long-term diet quality summarized as the cumulative mean Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) from repeated 24-hour recalls. Each 5-point higher CHEI associated with about 19% lower adjusted hazard of incident obesity (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.96), with an L-shaped dose–response suggesting larger gains at lower scores. Higher component scores for whole grains/mixed beans and dark vegetables associated with lower obesity risk; after mutual adjustment, whole grains/mixed beans remained more robust.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Nutrition — Chinese Healthy Eating Index and incident obesity](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1983457)

**Commentary:** Observational evidence cannot prove causation, but the practical takeaway for Chinese-pattern diets is clear: lock in whole grains, legumes and dark vegetables before chasing a perfect index score.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 5. Meta-analysis: Nordic/eccentric hamstring training cut soccer hamstring injuries ~70%; plyometrics favored power (Training)

**Summary:** A Scientific Reports systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 54 RCTs in soccer players aged 13+. Eccentric (especially Nordic hamstring) training reduced hamstring-injury incidence by about 70% (4 trials; RR 0.29, 95% CI 0.17–0.48), whereas plyometric bounding did not show a comparable injury benefit. Training improved vertical jump (g ≈ 0.70) and sprint (g ≈ 0.75); in subgroups, plyometric and combined programs favored sprint gains, while eccentric work raised eccentric knee-flexor strength (g ≈ 0.82). Authors framed the effects as complementary: eccentric for protection and strength, plyometric for speed and power.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — Eccentric vs. plyometric training for hamstring health in soccer](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-79351-y)

**Commentary:** Recreational soccer and sprint sports should treat Nordic hamstring work as a staple warm-up/strength block, then add plyometrics for performance—progress volume slowly so DOMS does not wipe out the next session.

### 6. Interset-rest meta-analysis: under common prescriptions, longer vs shorter rest for 1RM gains remains uncertain (Training)

**Summary:** A Frontiers in Physiology systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials compared longer versus shorter interset rest for strength adaptation. Under common matched prescriptions, neither upper-body 1RM (4 trials, 88 participants; g = 0.12, 95% CI −0.60 to 0.85) nor lower-body 1RM (8 trials, 165 participants; g = 0.23, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.81) showed a clear between-group advantage; intervals allowed effects in either direction. Selected single-study outcomes favored longer rest for a torque measure under volume matching and shorter rest for some functional tests. Authors advise choosing rest by intended load, subsequent-set quality and available time—not by a universal “longer is better” rule.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Physiology — Longer versus shorter interset rest for strength adaptation](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.2032957/abstract)

**Commentary:** For max strength, rest enough to keep set quality high; when time is tight, shorter rests can preserve density—effective volume and technique matter more than the stopwatch number.

### 7. SLEEP trial: one moderate evening ride before sleep restriction buffered declarative memory via slow-wave sleep (Training/Sleep)

**Summary:** In SLEEP, 88 healthy young adults were randomized to average sleep (~8.5 h), sleep restriction (~4.5 h), exercise then average sleep, or exercise then sleep restriction. Exercise arms completed ~30 minutes of moderate cycling at 7 p.m. (~55%–60% VO₂max) before learning face–name pairs, with polysomnography and delayed recall the next morning. An exercise × sleep interaction emerged: delayed discriminability after exercise-plus-restriction exceeded sedentary restriction and resembled full-sleep groups. Slow-wave sleep (SWS) proportion statistically mediated the association between exercise and delayed recall.

**Links:**

- [SLEEP — Slow-wave sleep mediates exercise effects under sleep restriction](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/49/11/zsag235/8771500)

**Commentary:** Lab findings in young adults do not license chronic sleep debt; if a short night is unavoidable, moderate evening cardio may blunt next-day memory loss, but restoring regular sleep remains the priority.

## III. Public Health & Guidelines

### 8. CUHK Genome Medicine study: a three-locus ADD-GRS flags younger Chinese type 2 diabetes onset via beta-cell pathways (Public Health)

**Summary:** CUHK investigators performed a GWAS of age at diabetes diagnosis in about 22,611 Chinese people with type 2 diabetes (~20% young-onset, age <40) and reported in Genome Medicine three genome-wide significant independent signals at CDKAL1, PAX4 and KCNQ1, then built a three-marker Age-at-Diabetes-Diagnosis Genetic Risk Score (ADD-GRS). Higher ADD-GRS associated with younger diagnosis, lower C-peptide and higher HbA1c; institutional communications noted people in the top ~5%–20% may be diagnosed about 6–12 months earlier than average. Authors propose low-cost risk stratification and prioritized OGTT, while stressing genes are fixed and lifestyle risks still matter; whether scoring delays disease onset is unproven.

**Links:**

- [CUHK Medicine — Genetic markers of young-onset type 2 diabetes in Chinese](https://med.cuhk.edu.hk/press-releases/cuhk-world-first-study-identifies-genetic-markers-of-young-onset-type-2-diabetes-in-chinese-population-develops-novel-risk-score-for-early-diagnosis-and-prevention)
- [Genome Medicine — GWAS and ADD-GRS for age at diabetes diagnosis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13073-026-01760-5)

**Commentary:** Young adults with early-onset family history should take weight, activity and periodic glucose screening seriously; a genetic score is a triage tool, not destiny, and does not replace lifestyle change.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 10. Pragmatic RCT: a sleep-extension intervention lowered anxiety in young adults but not depression (Sleep/Mental Health)

**Summary:** A Sleep Advances abstract dated November 19, 2026 reported secondary analyses of an Australian pragmatic RCT: 115 young adults habitually sleeping <7 h/night and <6 h on ≥2 nights/week were randomized (99 completers), excluding diagnosed sleep/mental disorders and shift work. Versus placebo control, sleep extension reduced anxiety post-intervention (β = −4.48, 95% CI −7.86 to −1.11) with benefit persisting at six months; depression did not change significantly. Sleep-related daytime impairment improved during the intervention but was not sustained; objective sleep duration and disturbance showed no significant effects. Authors suggested short-term functional gains—not simply longer sleep—may mediate anxiety benefits.

**Links:**

- [PMC / Sleep Advances — Sleep intervention effects on anxiety and depression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13783170/)

**Commentary:** Sleep hygiene can help mild anxiety symptoms, but moderate-to-severe depression still needs clinical care; a concrete step is a fixed wake time with gradual earlier bedtimes—not weekend catch-up sleep alone.

## V. Supplements & Evidence Clarification

### 11. Cochrane: daily iron lowers iron-deficiency anaemia risk in children and adolescents; side effects mostly mild (Supplements)

**Summary:** Cochrane reported on November 17 a Cornell-led review of 114 studies in more than 50,000 children and adolescents assessing daily oral iron alone, with folic acid, or with other vitamins/minerals. Versus no intervention or placebo, iron alone probably reduces iron-deficiency anaemia; height, weight and several common illnesses (cough, fever, respiratory illness) showed little measurable change. Side-effect evidence was mixed: stool discoloration may increase; vomiting may rise when iron is combined with other micronutrients. Authors caution against reading this as a mandate for every child—decisions should reflect individual iron status and local anaemia prevalence.

**Links:**

- [Cochrane — Daily iron supplements reduce iron deficiency anaemia in children and adolescents](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/daily-iron-supplements-reduce-iron-deficiency-anaemia-children-and-adolescents)

**Commentary:** Iron is a targeted tool, not a wellness default; test before treating, because blind long-term supplementation can mask causes and upset the gut.

### 12. FDA proposed rule: if finalized, NAC could be lawfully marketed as a dietary supplement (Evidence/Regulation)

**Summary:** On November 16, 2026, the U.S. FDA issued a proposed rule that would reverse NAC’s exclusion from the dietary-supplement definition, allowing compliant N-acetyl-L-cysteine products to be marketed as supplements—the agency’s first use of that exclusion-clause rulemaking authority. FDA stated a comprehensive review identified no safety concerns for NAC as a supplement and intends to continue temporary enforcement discretion during rulemaking. The proposal is open for 60 days of public comment; finalization is not guaranteed until the process concludes.

**Links:**

- [FDA — Proposed rule on NAC products as dietary supplements](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-issues-proposed-rule-nac-products-dietary-supplements)

**Commentary:** Regulatory clearance is not a reason for everyone to supplement; without a clear indication, skip the NAC stockpile, and check with a clinician if you have liver/kidney disease or take other medicines.
