## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Nature Communications mouse study: Acute overconsumption of low-digestible sugars can weaken colonization resistance to Salmonella Typhimurium (nutrition / evidence check)
**Summary:** ETH Zurich and collaborators acutely dosed mice with low-digestible sugars (LDS) such as arabinose, lactulose, and polyols before Salmonella Typhimurium infection; the paper went online Nov 3, 2026. Versus controls, LDS groups showed >100-fold higher fecal pathogen loads on day 1 post-infection, plus systemic dissemination and colitis. Mechanistically, rapid arabinose fermentation acidified the gut lumen, perturbed microbiota, altered short-chain fatty acids, and raised amino-acid availability that can support pathogen growth and broader tissue tropism. The authors stress animal-model limits; human infection susceptibility remains inferred.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Low-digestible sugar overconsumption and Salmonella susceptibility](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78218-0)

**Commentary:** “More fiber or sugar alcohols is always better” is wrong—keep polyol/lactulose doses moderate, and avoid mega-loading during GI illness until human evidence catches up.

### 2. HEXA-G prospective cohort: >2 weekly servings of high-sugar foods linked to ~28% higher breast-cancer risk; fruit sugar showed no similar signal (nutrition)
**Summary:** Shin et al. examined diet and breast cancer among women aged 40–69 in Korea’s Health Examinees-Gem cohort; European Journal of Nutrition published the analysis, with News-Medical coverage around Nov 2–3. Consuming high-sugar foods (sodas, sweet teas, cakes, sugary cereal, ice cream, etc.) more than twice weekly versus less than once monthly was associated with HR ≈ 1.28 (95% CI 1.02–1.61). Total dietary sugar and fruit intake were not significantly associated. Over ~9.2 years, 713 incident cases occurred; single baseline diet, no natural-vs-added sugar split, and observational design preclude causality.

**Links:**

- [European Journal of Nutrition — High-sugar foods and breast cancer risk](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-026-04134-0)
- [News-Medical — Nov 2 coverage](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261102/Eating-sugary-foods-more-than-twice-a-week-was-linked-to-2825-higher-breast-cancer-risk.aspx)

**Commentary:** Cut frequency of processed sweets first; don’t scapegoat fruit—and don’t treat an association as “two desserts cause cancer.”

### 3. UC San Diego × CMGH humanized-mouse work: Breast-milk sugar 3′-sialyllactose supports gut maturation and bilirubin clearance (nutrition)
**Summary:** Tukey’s team used a humanized-UGT1 neonatal hyperbilirubinemia model comparing milk lacking 3′-sialyllactose (3′SL) versus controls; Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology accepted the paper, and UC San Diego issued a Nov 3 briefing. Pups deprived of milk 3′SL had worse survival and growth, shorter intestines, more severe jaundice, and markedly lower intestinal UGT1A1, with downregulated maturation/secretory-lineage markers. St3gal4 deficiency later heightened adult DSS colitis vulnerability. This is mechanistic animal work, not a ready human dosing protocol.

**Links:**

- [CMGH — Maternal milk 3′SL, intestinal maturation, and hyperbilirubinemia](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcmgh.2026.101924)
- [UC San Diego — Nov 3 news release](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/how-a-breast-milk-sugar-supports-infant-gut-development-and-prevents-jaundice)

**Commentary:** Keep prioritizing breastfeeding and pediatric jaundice follow-up; don’t self-dose newborns with over-the-counter oligosaccharide powders based on mouse data alone.

### 4. 2026 ACC Scientific Statement: Heart nutrition hinges on overall diet quality and processing—not single nutrients (guidelines)
**Summary:** The ACC statement Food and Health: Nutrition for Prevention and Management of Cardiovascular Disease synthesizes RCTs, cohorts, and mechanisms; ACC clinical updates around Nov 1 reiterated the take-homes. It centers minimally processed plant foods plus healthy fats and proteins, while limiting refined starches, added sugars, sodium, unhealthy fats, and poor-quality ultra-processed foods. Prefer water and unsweetened drinks; generally limit or avoid alcohol. Obtain long-chain omega-3s mainly from regular fish; routine omega-3 supplements are not recommended for primary CVD prevention.

**Links:**

- [JACC — 2026 ACC cardiovascular nutrition scientific statement](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.08.025)
- [ACC — Overall diet quality and heart health](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2026/11/01/01/new-in-clinical-guidance-overall-diet-quality)

**Commentary:** Your grocery pattern beats any single pill—build a Mediterranean/DASH/plant-forward template, and use supplements only for documented gaps.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 5. Sports Medicine - Open meta-analysis: >8 weeks of plyometric jump training improves jump, sprint, and agility in adult female team-sport athletes (training)
**Summary:** A search through March 2026 included 31 articles (n=609), with 25 meta-analysed; open access published Nov 2, 2026. Versus controls, plyometric jump training (PJT) improved CMJ height (ES ≈ 0.55), drop-jump RSI (ES ≈ 0.93), 10–30 m sprint times, and Illinois/T-test agility. Interventions ≥8 weeks outperformed ≤6-week programs on several outcomes. Certainty of evidence was low to moderate; two studies reported three possible related injuries. Findings apply to healthy adult female team-sport athletes.

**Links:**

- [Sports Medicine - Open — PJT in female team-sport athletes](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01136-4)

**Commentary:** For women’s team preseason, schedule progressive plyometrics across ≥8 weeks with landing technique and load monitoring—short “jump blasts” likely underdeliver.

### 6. Scientific Reports crossover trial: Exhaustive treadmill running spikes plasma cfDNA, but urinary excretion is not the main clearance route (training / recovery)
**Summary:** Medical University of Lodz studied 15 moderately trained men (VO₂max ≈ 45.5 ml/kg·min) in a crossover of exhaustive treadmill running at ~70% VO₂max (~11.2 km, ~67 min) versus 1 h rest; published Nov 3, 2026. Plasma cell-free DNA rose ~25-fold immediately post-exercise and returned to baseline by 4 h. Urinary albumin and creatinine rose, yet urinary cfDNA did not increase significantly, and urinary cfDNA/creatinine and cfDNA/albumin ratios fell. Authors conclude urine is not a major route clearing exercise-released cfDNA; sample was small and male-only.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — Exhaustive running and urinary cfDNA](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-78947-8)

**Commentary:** For athletes, post-hard-session urine protein/creatinine swings can occur without equaling kidney injury; for research biobanks, moderate exercise need not overly restrict urine donors for cfDNA work.

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 7. 10/66 five-factor score: Conversation-based predictors estimate 3-year dementia risk in LMICs (public health)
**Summary:** A Northumbria-led team built a Fine–Gray score from 11,143 older adults across seven 10/66 sites; American Journal of Epidemiology published ahead of print, with a university briefing ~Nov 2 and media on Nov 3. Predictors are age, social engagement, physical activity, hypertension, and difficulty managing money (score 0–9; ~1% three-year risk at 0 vs nearly 19% at 9). Pooled c-statistic ≈ 0.75 (95% CI 0.72–0.78). Authors position it for research recruitment and resource planning—not routine population screening—and call for validation in South Asia, MENA, and sub-Saharan Africa.

**Links:**

- [American Journal of Epidemiology — LMIC dementia risk prediction model](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwag116)
- [Northumbria University — Nov 2 news release](https://www.northumbria.ac.uk/about-us/news-events/news/new-dementia-risk-tool-designed-for-the-countries-that-need-it-most/)

**Commentary:** Midlife actions that still matter: control blood pressure, stay active, stay socially connected—risk tools stratify; they don’t replace the behaviors.

### 8. AHA meeting observational abstract: In adults with prediabetes and obesity, GLP-1s more often normalized glycemia than metformin (public health)
**Summary:** Mayo Clinic investigators analyzed U.S. records of ~256,000 adults with prediabetes and obesity—about half on GLP-1s (e.g., semaglutide, tirzepatide) and half on metformin—reported via Healthline on Nov 3 from a conference abstract. GLP-1 users had ~1.5× higher odds of returning glucose to the normal range, ~31% lower risk of progressing to type 2 diabetes, ~41% lower all-cause mortality, and fewer reported MI, stroke, and heart-failure events. Observational, not yet peer-reviewed as a full paper; confounding by indication and access/cost remain major caveats.

**Links:**

- [Healthline — Nov 3: GLP-1s and prediabetes prevention coverage](https://www.healthline.com/health-news/glp-1s-lower-diabetes-heart-risks-better-than-standard-prevention)

**Commentary:** Prediabetes care still starts with weight, diet, and activity; drug choice needs clinician judgment—this abstract is not a guideline rewrite ditching metformin for everyone.

## IV. Sleep, Mental Health & Recovery

### 9. AHA Scientific Sessions abstract: Irregular sleep schedules linked to higher cardiovascular event risk (sleep)
**Summary:** Nguyen et al. (Boston Children’s / Harvard Medical School) analysed ~6 months of wearable sleep from 10,362 All of Us adults (>1 million nights) with median 3.7-year follow-up; AHA press release Nov 2, Chinese health media follow-up Nov 3. Large night-to-night shifts in bedtime/wake time linked to ~20–23% higher relative risk of serious CV events; each ~29-minute increase in sleep-duration variability associated with ~24% higher combined CV risk. Meeting abstract, observational—cannot prove causation; shift work and comorbidity may jointly drive sleep and heart outcomes.

**Links:**

- [American Heart Association — Sleep regularity and heart health release](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/is-a-consistent-sleep-schedule-important-to-heart-health)
- [UDN Health — Nov 3 Chinese summary](https://health.udn.com/health/story/5977/9853834)

**Commentary:** Keep aiming for ~7–9 hours nightly, and lock a wake time—weekend catch-up is fine, but large Friday-late/Sunday-long swings are worth shrinking.

### 10. BMC Sports Science systematic review: Sleep extension often improves performance; restriction hurts endurance and cognition (sleep / training)
**Summary:** Fifteen RCTs/crossover trials (2011–2025 literature) were narratively synthesized; open access published Nov 3, 2026. Sleep extension associated with better shuttle-run distance, lower fatigue index, faster reaction time, improved sprint/accuracy, and ~+3% endurance; restriction linked to ~−3% endurance plus higher fatigue and cognitive decrements. Daytime naps were context-dependent: 60–90 min naps more often improved MVC and distance and cut perceived fatigue/sleepiness; ~20 min naps were inconsistent. Heterogeneous designs precluded quantitative meta-analysis.

**Links:**

- [BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation — Sleep manipulation and athletic performance](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-02218-2)

**Commentary:** In the 1–2 weeks before competition, prioritize a bit more and more stable sleep over a last-minute 20-minute “performance nap”—short naps after debt are unreliable.

## V. Sports Medicine & Injury Prevention

### 11. JPOSNA survey: Time burden and low motivation are main barriers when youth girls’ soccer coaches run ACL neuromuscular programs (sports medicine)
**Summary:** Twenty-seven all-female youth soccer teams were randomized to CAP (core/agility/plyometrics) or FIFA 11+ across an 8-week season; published online Nov 3, 2026. Coaches reported ~77.7% CAP completion versus ~51.2% for FIFA 11+. By month two, most rated time commitment “too much”; only ~15.4% said players were very motivated and ~34.6% not motivated at all, while teachability was mostly rated standard/easy. Survey response rate ~41%; overall participation and motivation were low.

**Links:**

- [JPOSNA — Barriers to ACL injury-prevention program implementation](https://www.jposna.com/article/S2768-2765(26)00165-3/fulltext)

**Commentary:** Clubs should pick a short program they will actually finish every practice—completion rate beats brand-name warmups for real-world ACL prevention.
