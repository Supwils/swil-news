## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Advances in Nutrition / ICQC: An adaptable International Food Guide Pyramid puts plant foods and carbohydrate quality at the base (Guideline)
**Summary:** Brigham Young University’s Karen Della Corte and the International Carbohydrate Quality Consortium (ICQC) published an evidence-informed framework in Advances in Nutrition, covered October 29 by Health Chosun and related outlets. The team compared major national/regional guidelines, synthesized evidence from 250-plus sources plus expert consensus, and used EAT–Lancet quantitative proportions for visual hierarchy. The plant-forward omnivorous pyramid places vegetables, fruit, whole grains, and traditional low-GI staples at the base; prioritizes plant protein and unsaturated plant oils; includes modest animal foods with fish/poultry preferred over red and processed meat; limits saturated fat, refined grains, and sugar-sweetened beverages; and accommodates vegan and other primarily plant-based patterns. Authors frame it as culturally adaptable guidance, not a single mandatory menu.

**Links:**

- [Advances in Nutrition — International Food Guide Pyramid for human and planetary health](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2026.100738)
- [Health Chosun — October 29 report on the international food guide pyramid](https://health.chosun.com/english/site/data/html_dir/2026/10/29/2026102903150.html)

**Commentary:** Skip perfect-plate perfectionism—swap refined staples for whole/low-GI grains and trade some red/processed meat for legumes, fish, or poultry to match this consensus.

### 2. BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health: Secondary analysis of a low-fat vegan crossover—shifting animal to plant protein tracked weight loss more than total protein amount (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Hana Kahleova and colleagues at the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine published a secondary analysis of a 36-week randomized crossover in 62 overweight adults in BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health (NCT03698955), with a PCRM release dated October 29. Participants completed 16 weeks each of a low-fat vegan diet and a PREDIMED-style Mediterranean diet (4-week washout), without calorie limits; 52 (84%) finished. On the vegan diet, total protein fell 33.3 g/day, animal protein 47.7 g/day, and plant protein rose 14.4 g/day; none of the three changed significantly on the Mediterranean diet. Changes in total and animal protein correlated positively with weight change; plant-protein change correlated with greater weight loss; reductions in isoleucine, lysine, methionine, and tyrosine also tracked weight loss. Authors stress concurrent drops in fat and energy density—findings are hypothesis-generating, not proof that protein source alone causes weight loss. The parent trial reported ~6.0 kg loss on vegan eating and no significant Mediterranean weight change.

**Links:**

- [BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health — Plant vs animal protein and body weight on vegan and Mediterranean diets](https://nutrition.bmj.com/content/early/2026/10/20/bmjnph-2026-001511)
- [PCRM — October 29 release on swapping animal for plant protein and weight](https://www.pcrm.org/news/news-releases/swapping-animal-protein-plant-protein-not-cutting-protein-overall-linked-weight)

**Commentary:** For weight goals, prioritize beans, lentils, tofu, and whole grains over chasing a protein gram target alone—this analysis does not isolate protein source from energy density.

### 3. JACC: Advances / UK Biobank: Among cancer survivors, several healthy dietary patterns linked to lower CVD risk; MIND stood out for mortality and life expectancy (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Investigators including Yang-Xin Chen (Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital) analyzed 8,716 UK Biobank cancer survivors free of CVD at baseline; over a median 12.3 years, 1,116 had incident CVD. ACC channels, MedicalXpress, and Scienmag covered the JACC: Advances paper on October 29. Top-versus-bottom quintile HRs for CVD included Mediterranean 0.72, MIND 0.74, AHEI-2010 0.63, and DASH 0.65, with protective signals also for healthful plant-based and anti-inflammatory scores; a healthy low-carbohydrate pattern did not show benefit. AHEI-2010 tracked lower ischemic heart disease and stroke, DASH lower heart failure; MIND had the most favorable all-cause mortality and life-expectancy estimates. At age 45, highest versus lowest healthy-diet adherence gained an estimated 1.66–3.32 years of life expectancy. Observational design cannot prove causation; authors favor flexible, evidence-based patterns over one “ideal” diet in survivorship care.

**Links:**

- [JACC: Advances — Healthy dietary patterns and incident CVD in cancer survivors](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.103376)
- [MedicalXpress — October 29 report on healthy diet after cancer and heart risk](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-10-healthy-diet-heart-disease-cancer.html)

**Commentary:** After cancer treatment, treat vegetables, whole grains, legumes, fish, and less processed red meat as cardio-oncology homework—pick Mediterranean, MIND, or DASH you can sustain rather than hunting a single perfect diet.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. BMC Sports Science meta-analysis: Across 44 RCTs, sprint intervals modestly beat moderate continuous training on relative VO₂max, max HR, and total cholesterol (Training)
**Summary:** Li and colleagues pooled 44 randomized trials (1,034 healthy adults) comparing long-term sprint interval training (SIT) with moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) in a three-level meta-analysis registered as CRD420251231005; Bioengineer covered the BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation paper on October 29. SIT showed greater gains in relative VO₂max (Hedges’ g = 0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.38) and maximal heart rate (g = 0.46, 0.11–0.80), and a favorable total-cholesterol difference (g = −0.59, −1.12 to −0.05). Most other cardiorespiratory, metabolic, and performance endpoints did not differ. Authors stress generally small effects, wide prediction intervals, and exploratory subgroups only—SIT is not universally superior and should be individualized.

**Links:**

- [BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation — SIT vs MICT systematic review and meta-analysis (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-02176-9)
- [Bioengineer — October 29 report on sprint intervals versus steady moderate training](https://bioengineer.org/sprint-intervals-edge-out-steady-moderate-training-in-meta-analysis-of-44-trials/)

**Commentary:** Short on time with a solid base? Trade some steady cardio for sprint intervals—but if joints and adherence matter more, MICT remains “good enough” for most outcomes.

### 5. European Review of Aging and Physical Activity: In adults 65–75, 12 weeks of unstable-surface resistance improved balance and co-activation more; stable training raised muscle–tendon stiffness more broadly (Training)
**Summary:** Ping Liu, Lele Chang and colleagues randomized 50 independently mobile adults aged 65–75 to stable or unstable resistance training (25 each; 46 completers), three ~60-minute sessions weekly for 12 weeks with matched exercises and dose; Bioengineer/Scienmag reported October 29 on the European Review of Aging and Physical Activity trial. Unstable work (BOSU, balance disc, physioball) reduced antagonist co-activation more and improved BESS and Y-Balance scores more, with a larger knee-pain drop; stable training increased stiffness across a wider set of quadriceps/hamstring muscles and tendons. There was no non-training control, falls were not measured, registry entry was retrospective, and authors warn balance gains must not be read as proven fall-risk reduction.

**Links:**

- [European Review of Aging and Physical Activity — 12-week unstable-surface RT and balance (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s11556-026-00430-0)
- [Bioengineer — October 29 report on unstable resistance training in older adults](https://bioengineer.org/unstable-resistance-training-outperforms-stable-training-for-balance-and-coordination-in-older-adults/)

**Commentary:** Choose unstable surfaces (with spotting) when balance/coordination is the goal, and firm-ground loading when broader stiffness is the goal—neither result is a fall-prevention verdict.

### 6. Frontiers in Physiology: In resistance-trained men, eight weeks of squat-then-jump versus jump-then-squat yielded nearly identical strength, jump, sprint, and COD gains (Training)
**Summary:** Fu, Zhang, Zhao, and Li (Guangdong Ocean University, Beijing Sport University, et al.) randomized 24 resistance-trained men to back squat then maximal countermovement jumps or the reverse order, twice weekly for eight weeks with matched volume, intensity, and rest; Scienmag covered the Frontiers in Physiology RCT on October 29. Both groups improved vertical jump, relative peak power, standing long jump, 20-m sprint, and T-test times, with nonsignificant group-by-time interactions. Back-squat 1RM rose ~13.1 kg (plyometric-first) versus ~10.2 kg (resistance-first) in the primary analysis (p = 0.039), but a baseline-adjusted ANCOVA was nonsignificant (p = 0.072); authors urge caution. Limits include n=12 per arm, men only, and CMJ-only plyometrics.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Physiology — 8-week resistance-to-CMJ sequence and lower-body performance (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2026.1982010)
- [Scienmag — October 29 report on squat versus jump exercise order](https://scienmag.com/exercise-order-barely-matters-squats-first-or-jumps-first-yields-similar-gains/)

**Commentary:** Order squats and jumps by energy and logistics; matched dose matters more than a sacred sequence over an eight-week block.

### 7. BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders update: Proprioceptive training cut relative ankle-injury occurrence by about 34% in athletes (Sports medicine)
**Summary:** Shen, Chen, and Han’s updated systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 randomized or cluster-randomized trials (search through June 5, 2026) appeared in BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders on October 28. Proprioceptive training—alone or as an identifiable component of multicomponent prevention—yielded a pooled relative effect of 0.659 (95% CI 0.558–0.778; I² ≈ 22.7%), about 34% lower ankle-injury occurrence versus controls. Sport type and intervention composition did not show significant effect modification. Overall certainty was rated moderate; optimal dosing and generalizability across levels still need work.

**Links:**

- [BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders — Proprioceptive training to prevent ankle sprains in athletes](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12891-026-10677-4)

**Commentary:** Build single-leg balance, unstable-surface work, and landing control into team warm-ups before waiting for the next sprain.

## III. Sleep & Mental Health

### 8. Molecular Psychiatry target-trial emulation: In adults 70+, high UPF intake tracked ~12% higher depressive-symptom risk; anti-inflammatory diets ~7% lower (Mental health)
**Summary:** Belayneh Mengist and colleagues (Deakin/Monash and ASPREE collaborators) emulated a dietary target trial in 7,220 adults aged 70+, median follow-up 5.7 years; Health Chosun covered the Molecular Psychiatry paper on October 29. Depressive symptoms were CES-D 10 ≥ 8. After IPTW, UPF ≥ 4 servings/day versus < 4 had RR 1.12 (95% CI 1.03–1.21); an anti-inflammatory versus pro-inflammatory diet had RR 0.93 (0.86–1.00). Neither polygenic depression risk nor low-grade inflammation (hs-CRP) significantly modified associations. Residual confounding and self-reported diet remain limits—this does not prove diet treats clinical depression.

**Links:**

- [Molecular Psychiatry — Diet quality and depressive symptoms in older adults (target-trial emulation)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-026-03762-6)
- [Health Chosun — October 29 report on high-quality diet and depression risk](https://health.chosun.com/english/site/data/html_dir/2026/10/29/2026102901366.html)

**Commentary:** In later life, keep ultra-processed snacks and drinks occasional and lean on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish, and olive oil as a mental-health adjunct—not a substitute for clinical care.

## IV. Public Health & Evidence Checks

### 9. AHA/AAP/ACOG/SMFM Circulation trilogy: First shared Female Life Stage framework ties periods, pregnancy, and menopause to CVD prevention windows (Guideline)
**Summary:** The American Heart Association, American Academy of Pediatrics, ACOG, and Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine released a presidential advisory, scientific statement, and science advisory in Circulation; Healio continued October 29 coverage of the October 26 package. The framework defines perimenarche, ovarian cycling, perimenopause, and postmenopause by hormonal change, with pregnancy as a cardiovascular “stress test” that can occur in any stage. Highlights: menstrual patterns as a female-specific vital sign; adverse pregnancy outcomes as lifelong risk enhancers; perimenopause metabolic shifts as an inflection point; and most postmenopausal events as largely preventable with earlier action. CVD kills more than 400,000 U.S. women yearly, yet only about 41% recognize it as their top threat. Societies call for cross-specialty continuity and Centers of Excellence for women’s cardiovascular care.

**Links:**

- [Circulation — Female life-course CVD prevention and management presidential advisory](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001483)
- [AHA — Leading groups redefine heart health for girls and women](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/leading-health-groups-redefine-heart-health-for-girls-and-women)

**Commentary:** Bring menstrual history, pregnancy complications, and perimenopausal lipid/glucose changes to checkups—those details now map directly onto this shared prevention frame.

### 10. Neurology Brazilian cohort (ScienceDaily, Oct 29): Highest intake of six common low-/no-calorie sweeteners tracked ~62% faster global cognitive decline (~1.6 extra years of cognitive aging) (Evidence check)
**Summary:** Claudia Kimie Suemoto and colleagues followed 12,772 Brazilian adults (mean baseline age ~52) for about eight years in Neurology; AAN materials were widely recirculated via ScienceDaily on October 29 (noting prior analytic corrections left main conclusions intact). Versus the lowest tertile (~20 mg/day), the highest combined LNCS tertile (~191 mg/day—roughly one aspartame soda’s worth) showed about 62% faster decline in overall thinking and memory (~1.6 extra years of cognitive aging); the middle group was about 35% faster. Links were clearer under age 60 and among people with diabetes. Aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, sorbitol, and xylitol associated with faster decline; tagatose did not. Authors and coverage stress association, self-reported diet, and residual confounding—not proof that sweeteners damage the brain.

**Links:**

- [Neurology — Low- and no-calorie artificial sweeteners and cognitive decline (8-year prospective study)](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000218380)
- [ScienceDaily — October 29 report on six sugar substitutes and brain aging](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261028234238.htm)

**Commentary:** Do not treat diet sodas as a brain-friendly daily default—cut sugary drinks first, keep any sweeteners modest and varied, and wait for causal data rather than panic-banning or free-pouring.

### 11. PLOS Medicine NIH-AARP: Total UPF intake was not linked to colorectal cancer; risk tracked food components, not the “ultra-processed” label alone (Evidence check)
**Summary:** Erikka Loftfield and colleagues analyzed 461,682 cancer-free NIH-AARP adults (ages 50–71 at enrollment) with follow-up through 2018 and 10,075 colorectal adenocarcinomas; Chinese science media relayed the findings on October 29 after the October 15 PLOS Medicine publication. Highest versus lowest total UPF quintile HR for CRC was 0.97 (95% CI 0.91–1.03). Whole grain, fiber, dairy, and calcium from UPF sources associated with lower CRC; meat from UPF sources associated with higher CRC. Authors conclude CRC prevention still means more whole grains, fiber, dairy, and calcium and less processed meat—not treating all UPFs as equal. Limits include observational design, a single baseline diet questionnaire, and residual confounding.

**Links:**

- [PLOS Medicine — Food processing, dietary risk factors, and colorectal cancer (NIH-AARP)](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1005251)
- [China Science Daily relay — Total UPF intake not tied to overall colorectal cancer risk](https://news.sig.ai/cn/article/cmvtc80wr0004n5kfnpvke1xv)

**Commentary:** Judge the food, not only the Nova tag—keep whole-grain and dairy products if they fit your pattern, and cut processed meat and sugary drinks first.

### 12. Cureus NIH DSLD census: About 5.1% of U.S. adult vitamin B6 products direct ≥100 mg/day (the UL), often in products not sold as B6 (Supplements)
**Summary:** A STROBE-guided census of the NIH Dietary Supplement Label Database snapshot from October 4, 2026 (plus eight labels on October 13) merged 15,091 B6-labeled records into 11,913 products (11,367 adult). Median maximum directed adult dose was 10 mg/day; 575 products (5.1%) directed ≥100 mg/day (U.S. adult UL), 167 (1.5%) more than 100 mg/day, and 10 at least 500 mg/day, with a high of 1000 mg/day as pyridoxine-salt compound weight. Of the 575 high-dose products, 56.2% were not marketed as vitamin B6 or B-complex; 38.7% exceeded Europe’s commonly cited 12 mg/day threshold. Authors call for neuropathy warnings on high-dose labels and B6 checks when evaluating neuropathy. This is a label census, not a measure of blood levels or neuropathy incidence.

**Links:**

- [Cureus — Vitamin B6 doses on U.S. supplement labels versus the UL (DSLD census)](https://www.cureus.com/articles/543387-vitamin-b6-doses-on-united-states-dietary-supplement-labels-relative-to-the-tolerable-upper-intake-level-a-census-of-the-national-institutes-of-health-dietary-supplement-label-database)

**Commentary:** Flip to Supplement Facts on multis and “energy/sport” blends—chronic doses far above tens of milligrams are rarely needed, and numbness warrants bringing the bottle list to a clinician.
