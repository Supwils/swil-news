## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. AJCN DIRECT-PLUS secondary analysis: Green Mediterranean diet cuts pancreatic fat ~14% relatively (nutrition/metabolism)
**Summary:** Iris Shai’s team at Ben-Gurion University published an MRI secondary analysis of DIRECT-PLUS in *The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, with EurekAlert coverage on November 12, 2026. About 294 adults with abdominal obesity/dyslipidemia were assigned to healthy dietary guidelines (HDG), Mediterranean (MED), or polyphenol-rich Green-MED (green tea plus Mankai); both MED arms received ~28 g/day walnuts and exercise support. Roughly 290 participants had usable baseline MRI and 233 completed 18-month imaging. Relative pancreatic-fat reductions were about −14% for Green-MED (absolute ≈−2.41 percentage points), −6.7% for MED, and −0.8% for HDG; pancreatic volume did not change significantly. Greater pancreatic-fat loss tracked with better waist and insulin-resistance markers even after adjusting for visceral fat and weight loss. This was an exploratory secondary endpoint—not proof of diabetes prevention—and the sample was mostly middle-aged men.

**Links:**

- [American Journal of Clinical Nutrition — Green-MED and pancreatic fat](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(26)00322-9/abstract)
- [EurekAlert — November 12 release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1151661)

**Commentary:** For metabolically high-risk adults, prioritize a polyphenol-rich plant pattern, fewer refined carbs, and regular activity—use walnuts and leafy/aquatic greens as diet-quality levers, not as a single “pancreas-clearing” superfood.

### 2. Stem Cell Reports: High-fat Western diet leaves chromatin “memory” in mouse intestinal stem cells; re-exposure amplifies tumor risk (nutrition/mechanism)
**Summary:** Miyeko Mana’s Arizona State University group fed mice a Western-style high-fat diet for about six months; findings were reported around November 12, 2026, in *Stem Cell Reports* (DOI: 10.1016/j.stemcr.2026.103146). High-fat feeding increased intestinal stem-cell numbers and proliferation; genetically predisposed mice developed more and larger tumors. After switching to low fat, stem-cell behavior normalized, but roughly 18% of chromatin-accessibility changes remained at four weeks; re-challenge produced faster, stronger stem-cell responses and greater adenoma burden. Remodeling depended on PPAR-δ/α signaling rather than simply their canonical FAO transcriptional targets. This is animal epigenetic work—not a direct human “how many months of high fat causes cancer” rule.

**Links:**

- [MedicalXpress — High-fat diet and intestinal stem cells (November 12)](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-high-fat-diet-colon-cancer.html)
- [PMC — Diet-induced chromatin states influence intestinal stem cell memory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934732/)

**Commentary:** For everyday readers, the prudent takeaway is to avoid yo-yo returns to high-fat ultra-processed patterns—chronic exposure may matter more than a one-off “cleanse,” while remembering the evidence remains mouse-level.

### 3. AJCN RCT: 56 g/day almonds do not raise flu-vaccine seroconversion; some strain antibody titers are higher (nutrition/evidence)
**Summary:** A King’s College London–led RCT randomized 90 healthy adults aged 40–64 to 56 g/day almonds or an isocaloric pretzel snack (~86 g) for eight weeks, with seasonal influenza vaccination at week 4; results appeared in *The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, with International Nut & Dried Fruit Council commentary on November 12. The primary endpoint—seroconversion to ≥1 vaccine strain—was already very high and identical between arms (~93% vs 95%); seroprotection likewise did not differ. Secondary analyses favored almonds for A/Wisconsin/67/2022 (H1N1) post-vaccination titers and fold-change, and for B/Yamagata fold-change, though some contrasts used small subgroups. Authors note a ceiling effect in healthy midlife adults and no proof of fewer infections or higher clinical protection.

**Links:**

- [American Journal of Clinical Nutrition — Almonds and influenza vaccine responses](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(26)00405-3/fulltext)
- [INC — November 12 briefing](https://inc.nutfruit.org/almond-consumption-may-improve-certain-antibody-response-to-flu-vaccination-in-first-whole-food-trial/)

**Commentary:** Almonds can replace refined snacks as a quality choice, but they are not a “booster companion”—get vaccinated on schedule and do not let snack marketing overshadow the shot itself.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. Mayo Clinic Proceedings comparative review: Activity and cardiorespiratory fitness link more strongly to all-cause mortality than diet quality (training/public health)
**Summary:** Arizona State University’s Glenn Gaesser and Kaiser Permanente’s Robert Sallis compared meta-analyses and cohorts on diet quality, physical activity, and cardiorespiratory fitness versus all-cause mortality; the analysis appeared in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings*, with ASU News coverage on November 12, 2026. Several meta-analyses associated ~11,000–16,000 daily steps with 45%–67% lower mortality than ~2,000–5,000 steps. Lowest diet quality (ultra-processed foods, red/processed meat, sugary items, etc.) tracked with about 3%–20% higher mortality risk, versus about 46% to over 163% for near-total sedentary living. Adding ~2,000 steps/day among sedentary people associated with ~25%–50% lower mortality risk; raising fruit, vegetables, whole grains, and fiber to U.S. guideline levels associated with ~6%–20% lower risk. This is a comparative narrative of existing evidence, not a new RCT; the authors do not dismiss healthy eating.

**Links:**

- [ASU News — You aren’t just what you eat (November 12)](https://news.asu.edu/20261112-health-and-medicine-you-arent-just-what-you-eat)
- [Mayo Clinic Proceedings — related article PDF](https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/S0025-6196(26)15032-0/pdf)

**Commentary:** If time and willpower are scarce, default to “about one extra mile of walking and less sitting,” then upgrade the plate—exercise is not a free pass for junk food, but public-health priorities often undervalue movement.

### 5. Journal of Physiology: Extreme heat cuts exogenous carbohydrate oxidation ~27%; nine-day heat acclimation does not restore it (training/sports nutrition)
**Summary:** Tim Podlogar, Gareth Wallis and colleagues at Birmingham/Exeter had 17 recreational cyclists complete ~90-minute submaximal rides at 20°C and 40°C (~20% humidity), ingesting ~90 g/h isotope-labeled glucose with breath tracing and muscle biopsies; a ~nine-day isothermal heat-acclimation block preceded a repeat hot trial. Results are in *The Journal of Physiology* (DOI: 10.1113/jp289304), covered by News-Medical and MedicalXpress on November 12, 2026. Exogenous carbohydrate oxidation fell ~27% in the heat and stayed suppressed after acclimation despite better core temperature, heart rate, and perceived effort; the bottleneck appears downstream of gut absorption—at muscle/tissue uptake or use. Authors advise against cutting carbs to prioritize fluids; practice combined fueling and hydration in the heat.

**Links:**

- [News-Medical — Extreme heat and carbohydrate use (November 12)](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261112/Extreme-heat-stops-athletes-from-fully-using-carbohydrates-for-fuel.aspx)
- [MedicalXpress — Extreme heat creates hurdle for athletes](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-extreme-hurdle-athletes-carbohydrates-muscles.html)

**Commentary:** For summer long sessions or races: hydrate and cool aggressively, but do not “smartly” slash gels—train gut fueling near cool-weather carb targets, then layer ice drinks and external cooling.

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 6. Alzheimer’s Research & Therapy meta-analysis: Type 2 diabetes raises adjusted Alzheimer’s relative risk ~53%; incidence ~4.7 per 1,000 person-years (public health)
**Summary:** Curtin University’s Emmanuel Adewuyi and colleagues systematically searched through April 2026, pooling 40 studies (~27.1 million data points) focused on type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease rather than mixed dementia labels; the paper appeared November 9, 2026, in *Alzheimer’s Research & Therapy*, with university and News-Medical push on November 12. Pooled incidence was about 4.71 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 3.31–6.71); adjusted relative risk was about 1.53 (95% CI 1.38–1.70). Heterogeneity was extreme; all studies were observational, so causality is unproven and no anti-diabetes drug is shown to prevent dementia. Authors urge integrating cognitive-risk prevention into diabetes care.

**Links:**

- [Alzheimer’s Research & Therapy — T2D and Alzheimer’s meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13195-026-02254-7)
- [News-Medical — November 12 report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261112/Meta-analysis-quantifies-annual-Alzheimers-incidence-in-type-2-diabetes.aspx)

**Commentary:** If you live with diabetes, glucose, blood pressure, lipids, and exercise management are not only for heart and kidneys—they also belong in a longer-horizon brain-risk conversation; raise memory concerns earlier with clinicians.

### 7. AHA Scientific Sessions abstract: Medically tailored meals improve diet, blood pressure, and HbA1c over eight months in hypertension plus type 2 diabetes (public health/food-is-medicine)
**Summary:** Columbia’s Nour Makarem and colleagues presented preliminary findings at American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026, with Healio follow-up in mid-November. About 110 adults with coexisting hypertension and type 2 diabetes (mean age ~63) were randomized to eight months of medically tailored meals: a standard arm (~10 meals/week plus counseling) versus a gradual dose-down arm (same start, tapering to ~5 meals/week by the final month), both with heart-health education and cooking support. Across arms at eight months, Mediterranean diet scores rose ~3.15 points, odds of better food security roughly doubled, HbA1c fell ~0.5 percentage points, and systolic/diastolic BP fell ~3.7/4.1 mm Hg, without significant arm-by-arm interaction. This is a conference abstract pending full peer review.

**Links:**

- [American Heart Association — medically tailored meals release](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/medically-tailored-meals-improved-high-blood-pressure-type-2-diabetes-and-diet-in-adults)

**Commentary:** For people juggling glucose and blood pressure with limited resources, meal support plus skills to cook at home may beat a handout diet sheet—education and environment still matter when meal counts taper.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 8. Scientific Reports TemPredict: Lower wearable sleep regularity tracks with worse depression, anxiety, and loneliness (sleep/mental health)
**Summary:** UCSF, UC San Diego and collaborators linked the Sleep Regularity Index (SRI) from ~22,894 TemPredict smart-ring users to monthly self-reported depression, anxiety, and loneliness; the paper appeared in *Scientific Reports* on November 12, 2026. Longer-term data (mean ~154 days) showed a graded pattern: mean SRI was about 74.9 with severe depression versus ~83.7 within normal limits; severe anxiety ~78.9 versus ~83.7; elevated loneliness also paired with lower SRI. Each 5-point SRI drop associated with higher odds of worse symptom categories. Observational design cannot settle directionality; authors call for trials testing whether stabilizing schedules improves mental health.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — Sleep regularity and mental health (TemPredict)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-77545-y)
- [MedicalXpress — Erratic sleep and mental health (November 12)](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-erratic-mental-health.html)

**Commentary:** Beyond “hours in bed,” shrink the day-to-day swing in wake and bedtime—including weekends—as a low-cost, self-monitorable sleep-hygiene lever.

### 9. Scientific Reports SCAPIS: Short and long sleep, plus difficulty initiating sleep, associate with modestly lower post-bronchodilator lung function (sleep/respiratory)
**Summary:** A cross-sectional SCAPIS analysis of 27,740 adults roughly aged 50–64 (≈52% women; mean age ≈57) related questionnaire sleep duration and insomnia symptoms to post-bronchodilator spirometry and DLCO; results appeared in *Scientific Reports*, with Chinese science coverage on November 12, 2026. Versus 6–8 hours, short (<6 h) and long (≥9 h) sleep both associated with lower FEV₁, FVC, and DLCO; multivariable links were often stronger for long sleep (e.g., FEV₁ % predicted β ≈−1.82). Difficulty initiating sleep associated with lower FEV₁ and DLCO and higher odds of chronic airflow limitation (OR ≈1.22); more insomnia symptoms tracked with lower DLCO in a graded pattern. Cross-sectional data cannot separate “poor sleep harms lungs” from reverse causation; effect sizes were modest.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — SCAPIS: sleep and lung function](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-79571-2)
- [Ebiotrade — November 12 Chinese report](https://www.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-11/20261112063914914.htm)

**Commentary:** If you have chronic cough, dyspnea, or abnormal spirometry, bring sleep duration and sleep-onset trouble into the visit; conversely, persistent short or long sleep is worth mentioning at checkups—without self-diagnosing COPD from sleep alone.
