## I. Nutrition and Diet

### 1. JAMA Network Open: Among 8,783 middle-aged Canadian adults, the top ultra-processed-food quartile had 1.40-fold odds of vascular brain injury (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Russell J. de Souza and colleagues published a cross-sectional analysis in JAMA Network Open on October 15 (doi: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.39118); McMaster University and the Population Health Research Institute issued releases the same day. The sample came from the Canadian Alliance for Healthy Hearts and Minds and PURE-MIND: 8,783 adults aged 35 to 69 with no cardiovascular disease at baseline, mean age 57.7 years, 4,917 women (56.6%). Ultra-processed food was ranked in quartiles of servings per 2,000 kcal. Versus the lowest quartile, the highest had body fat 1.7 percentage points higher (95% CI 1.2-2.2), visceral adipose tissue 5.7 mL greater (3.2-8.3), and an adjusted odds ratio of 1.40 (1.10-1.77; P for trend = 0.009) for MRI-defined vascular brain injury. Hepatic fat fraction did not differ significantly. Primary analyses of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test were not significant. In women the quartile odds ratio for vascular brain injury was 1.78 (1.27-2.48); in men it was 1.08 (0.76-1.54), interaction P = 0.04. The authors describe the sex split as exploratory. When exposure was redefined as percent of energy or percent of servings, the vascular-brain-injury association was no longer stable. One scan cannot show which change came first.

**Links:**

- [JAMA Network Open — Ultra-processed food, adiposity, and vascular brain injury](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2855351)
- [PHRI — October 15 release](https://www.phri.ca/ultra-processed-food-intake-linked-to-silent-strokes-in-healthy-middle-aged-adults-study-finds/)

**Commentary:** Middle-aged adults can treat packaged snacks, sugary cereals, and ready meals as something to cut from the daily serving count, in the same direction as body fat and symptom-free brain injury; cognition did not separate here, and the brain-injury link did not hold when intake was counted as percent of energy, so this is not proof that eating less ultra-processed food prevents stroke.

### 2. PLOS ONE: In 1,020 Chennai adults, a higher diabetes-related genetic score lined up with higher cholesterol only when diet quality was low (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Manahil M. Bineid, Dhanasekaran Bodhini, Kandaswamy Ramya, Rajendra Pradeepa, Ranjit Mohan Anjana, Viswanathan Mohan, Vimal Karani S. Vimaleswaran, and colleagues published a cross-sectional analysis in PLOS ONE on October 14 (doi: 10.1371/journal.pone.0357182). The University of Reading reported it on October 15. The sample was 1,020 adults in the Chennai Urban Rural Epidemiology Study (452 men, 568 women, ages 25-80). The genetic risk score used TCF7L2 rs7903146, FTO rs8050136, and FTO rs1588413. Diet came from a 222-item food-frequency questionnaire and the Indian Diet Quality Score, with low, medium, and high tertiles cut near 68.65 and 87.87. A high score was associated with higher fasting glucose (P = 0.005) and glycated hemoglobin (P = 0.006). After correction for multiple testing, the score interacted with diet quality for total cholesterol (interaction P = 0.003) and LDL cholesterol (interaction P = 0.005). At low diet quality, people with a high score had higher values on both lipids than people with a low score (P < 0.001 for the comparisons). The abstract does not give the absolute cholesterol difference. A cross-section cannot show that changing diet would lower cholesterol.

**Links:**

- [PLOS ONE — Diet quality and genetic risk of high cholesterol in Asian Indian adults](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0357182)
- [University of Reading — October 15 news](https://www.reading.ac.uk/news/2026/Research-News/Indian-diet-shift-exposes-hidden-genetic-cholesterol-risk)

**Commentary:** People of South Asian heritage who eat few vegetables, fruit, and pulses can look at their lipids without waiting for a gene test before adding those foods; the interaction says the gap is larger when diet quality is low, and no trial here shows that changing the diet offsets these variants.

## II. Exercise and Training Science

### 3. JACC: The American College of Cardiology writes exercise as a basic prescription for cardiovascular prevention and care, with about 7,000 steps tied to meaningful benefit (Training)
**Summary:** A scientific statement by Jonathan Myers, Theresa M. Beckie, Sebastien Chastin, Jerome L. Fleg, Dalane W. Kitzman, Paul D. Thompson, and Meagan Murphy Wasfy appeared online in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology on October 15 (doi: 10.1016/j.jacc.2026.09.008). It says low cardiorespiratory fitness predicts future cardiovascular disease on a scale comparable to smoking, hypertension, high cholesterol, and diabetes, yet exercise prescriptions are still not a stable part of routine care. For most adults, including people with stable disease, aerobic exercise 3 to 5 days a week, 20 to 60 minutes a session, at moderate-to-vigorous intensity, with gradual progression, is described as appropriate, and resistance training should complement it. A cited meta-analysis of 12 studies and more than 111,000 people found significantly lower all-cause death at about 2,517 steps a day and lower cardiovascular events at about 2,735 steps, with the lowest rates at about 8,763 and 7,126 steps a day. Another meta-analysis of 51 studies associated 7,000 steps a day, versus 2,000, with a 47% lower risk of both all-cause and cardiovascular death. The statement treats about 7,000 steps as a practical target for many adults and says clinicians can also suggest about 4,000 to 8,000 steps a day for healthy patients. Most asymptomatic people do not need routine exercise testing before they start. People with known cardiovascular disease who cannot enter formal cardiac rehabilitation should still receive an individualized prescription.

**Links:**

- [JACC — 2026 ACC scientific statement on exercise prescription](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.09.008)
- [News-Medical — October 15 report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261015/New-scientific-statement-urges-doctors-to-prescribe-exercise-for-heart-health.aspx)

**Commentary:** People who barely move can raise daily walking toward about 4,000 to 7,000 steps before chasing 10,000; that range is how the statement reads existing evidence, not a new randomized trial.

### 4. Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport: Among 23 older adults with type 2 diabetes who finished the protocol, only aerobic plus elastic-band training kept 24-hour mean glucose below the non-exercise control (Training)
**Summary:** A randomized crossover trial published online on October 15, still in press, compared scalable aerobic exercise, elastic-band resistance exercise, and the combination for acute and 24-hour glucose in older adults with type 2 diabetes. Thirty people enrolled and 23 completed all three sessions and entered the per-protocol analysis. Continuous glucose monitoring ran for 14 days, and the non-exercise control was the average of four pre-training days. In exploratory generalized estimating equations adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, and glycated hemoglobin, only combined training had lower 24-hour mean glucose than control (beta = -0.513, P = 0.0019, Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.017). Aerobic training had the steepest acute decline, -0.0256 mmol/L per minute. In secondary pairwise comparisons that remained after correction, combined training had higher time in range than aerobic training, and resistance training had a lower glycemic risk index than aerobic training. The glucose nadir was latest after combined training, at 185 minutes. The authors say no other principal-outcome comparison stayed significant after Bonferroni correction. This is the glucose response to single sessions, not a multi-week trial of glycated hemoglobin.

**Links:**

- [Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport — Elastic-band training and glucose in older adults with diabetes](https://www.jsams.org/article/S1440-2440(26)00611-0/abstract)

**Commentary:** An older adult with type 2 diabetes who can do only one modality should not assume elastic bands alone will lower the 24-hour mean; in this 23-person single-session crossover, the comparison that survived correction was aerobic exercise plus bands, and glycated hemoglobin over several weeks was not measured.

### 5. Neurology: Among 294 people with mild to moderate Parkinson disease, the highest tertile of total activity time had slower cognitive and motor progression (Training)
**Summary:** A longitudinal cohort by Dong-Woo Ryu and colleagues appeared online in Neurology on October 7 (doi: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218682). The University of Cincinnati summarized national coverage on October 15, and Healio spelled out the hour cut points on October 14. The Cincinnati Cohort Biomarker Program enrolled 294 people with mild to moderate Parkinson disease between January 2019 and February 2025, mean age 66.4 years, 33.0% women. Activity from a standardized interview was scored as total time, total volume, and vigorous time. Follow-up reached 5 years for the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and 7 years for the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale, part III. Annual cognitive change in the low, middle, and high tertiles of total activity time was -0.478, -0.090, and -0.007 points, and annual motor change was 0.143, -0.225, and -0.783 points. Versus the lowest tertile, the highest had slower cognitive progression (beta = 0.470, 95% CI 0.203-0.737) and slower motor progression (beta = -0.926, -1.835 to -0.017). Healio described those tertiles as about 0-4, 4-8, and more than 8 hours a week. After adjustment for total activity time, vigorous activity was no longer associated with the outcomes. After adjustment for activity over the previous 10 years, recent activity remained associated with longitudinal change on both scores. The finding is observational.

**Links:**

- [Neurology — Physical activity and Parkinson disease progression](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000218682)
- [University of Cincinnati — October 15 roundup](https://www.uc.edu/news/articles/2026/10/more-physical-activity-may-mean-slower-parkinsons-disease-progression.html)

**Commentary:** People with mild to moderate Parkinson disease can lengthen weekly activity time, counting housework and brisk walking, rather than chasing vigorous intensity alone; a questionnaire cohort of 294 cannot show that activity itself slowed the disease.

## III. Public Health and Disease Prevention

### 6. Nature Communications: In 711 quality-controlled samples from Jamaica and Malawi, one-carbon-metabolism loci were associated with edematous severe acute malnutrition (Public health)
**Summary:** Natasha C. Lie, Neil A. Hanchard, and colleagues published an open-access genetic case-control analysis in Nature Communications on October 15 (doi: 10.1038/s41467-026-74239-x). Edematous severe acute malnutrition, including kwashiorkor and the mixed form, is clinically distinct from non-edematous wasting, and the reason a child develops one rather than the other is still unclear. The primary analysis used DNA from about 833 people in Jamaica and Malawi; after quality control there were 711 samples and 1,639,325 genotyped variants, with cases and controls roughly balanced at each site. Of 95 loci related to one-carbon metabolism, six met the authors' candidate criteria. Four of them, AHCYL1, PRICKLE2, GABBR2, and PLD2, relate to homocysteine or folate metabolism. The lead GABBR2 variant, rs7038285, had an odds ratio of 0.85 (two-sided P = 6.98 x 10^-7), with the minor allele more common in non-edematous malnutrition. Compared with a genome-wide random sample, the cumulative association of one-carbon variants was z = 3.2. Mendelian randomization in an independent Malawian serum set produced effect estimates linking cystathionine and betaine to edematous risk, with heterogeneity among instruments for some metabolites. The authors tie population differences to shared East African ancestry. This is not a folate-supplement trial.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — One-carbon metabolism and edematous severe acute malnutrition](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-74239-x)

**Commentary:** This is not a reason to give children extra folate tablets; it suggests edematous severe malnutrition may be more than "less protein," while treatment still follows existing nutrition protocols and these loci are not yet a usable dietary intervention.

## IV. Pressure and Performance

### 7. Trends in Cognitive Sciences: Sport scientists in Cologne argue that failing under pressure may be a change in processing complexity, not only a shift in what gets attention (Pressure)
**Summary:** The German Sport University Cologne issued a release on October 15 on a theoretical article by Laura Voigt and colleagues in New Zealand, "Beyond attention: Performance pressure shifts information-processing complexity," in Trends in Cognitive Sciences (doi: 10.1016/j.tics.2026.09.008). The article gathers existing evidence and theory and challenges the account that pressure only moves attention off the task. It proposes that pressure also changes how information is processed, integrated, and turned into action. The authors say performance can collapse or rise, depending on whether the mode of processing fits the situation. The piece is theoretical, and the predictions have not yet been tested in a new experiment. The release says the next step is not a blanket instruction to "think less" or to consider every cue, but to find which kind of processing helps in a given setting.

**Links:**

- [German Sport University Cologne — October 15 release](https://www.dshs-koeln.de/en/universitaet/newsroom/news/detail/news/under-pressure-why-we-fail-or-rise-to-the-occasion)

**Commentary:** Before a match or an exam, "think about nothing" is not the only option on the table; this theoretical article changes the research question and does not yet supply a tested pressure routine.

## V. Evidence and Clarification

### 8. PLOS Medicine: In 461,682 older NIH-AARP adults followed a median of 18.8 years, total ultra-processed-food intake was not associated with colorectal cancer (Evidence check)
**Summary:** Leila Abar, Caitlin P. O'Connell, Erikka Loftfield, and colleagues published a prospective cohort in PLOS Medicine on October 15 (doi: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1005251). The NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study enrolled adults aged 50 to 71 in 1995-1996. Among 461,682 participants free of cancer at baseline (274,269 men and 187,413 women), follow-up through 2018, median 18.8 years, found 10,075 colorectal adenocarcinomas. Median ultra-processed intake was 293 g per 1,000 kcal per day, about 43% of energy. The hazard ratio for the highest versus lowest quintile was 0.97 (95% CI 0.91-1.03; P for trend = 0.55). Subgroups pointed in opposite directions: reconstituted meat and fish products had a highest-quintile hazard ratio of 1.10 (1.04-1.18); bread 0.91 (0.86-0.97); frozen desserts and milk drinks 0.82 (0.77-0.88); breakfast cereals 0.84 (0.79-0.90). From ultra-processed sources, each serving of whole grain had a hazard ratio of 0.87 (0.82-0.92), each 10 g of fiber 0.72 (0.64-0.80), and each serving of dairy 0.75 (0.62-0.91); each serving of meat from those sources was 1.08 (1.02-1.14). Replacing 10% of grams of ultra-processed food with minimally processed food had a hazard ratio of 0.98 (0.96-0.99). The cohort was mainly older white adults in the United States at the time, and diet was measured once.

**Links:**

- [PLOS Medicine — Food processing and colorectal cancer incidence](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1005251)
- [Medical Xpress — October 15 news](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-10-total-ultra-food-intake-linked.html)

**Commentary:** Colorectal-cancer eating advice still means more whole grains, fiber, dairy, and calcium, and less processed meat, rather than treating every ultra-processed food as the same carcinogen; the null result for the total exists because bread, cereal, and meat point in opposite directions, and the diet record is one questionnaire from the 1990s.

### 9. U.S. FDA: An October 15 webinar explained a proposal that substances claimed to be generally recognized as safe would have to be notified (Food safety)
**Summary:** The U.S. Food and Drug Administration held an online webinar on October 15 on a rule proposed on August 10. If finalized, the proposal would require a GRAS notice for human and animal food substances claimed to be generally recognized as safe under their intended conditions of use, unless an exception applies. Indirect additions, including substances from food packaging, are within the discussion. The current program, in place since a 1997 proposal, lets companies notify voluntarily. The agency says mandatory notices would show the data behind a safety conclusion, so reviewers can judge whether a use should instead go through premarket food-additive approval. The webinar ran from 2 p.m. to 3 p.m. Eastern, with a recording afterward. Electronic comments go to Regulations.gov under docket FDA-2025-N-3262. The comment period is open until December 9, 2026. This is an explanation of a draft rule, not a safety verdict on any one supplement.

**Links:**

- [FDA — October 15 GRAS proposed-rule webinar](https://www.fda.gov/food/workshops-meetings-webinars-food-and-dietary-supplements/webinar-fdas-generally-recognized-safe-gras-proposed-rule-10152026)
- [Federal Register — Substances Generally Recognized as Safe](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/11/2026-16296/substances-generally-recognized-as-safe)

**Commentary:** An ingredient labeled GRAS was not declared safe or pulled from shelves today; the change under discussion is that companies may have to file the safety conclusion with the FDA, comments run through December 9, and no final rule is in force.
