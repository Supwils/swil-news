## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. BMJ Open Diabetes Research & Care single-arm trial: A 16-week ad libitum low-fat vegan diet cut liver fat by about one-third and improved beta-cell markers in type 2 diabetes (Nutrition)
**Summary:** In collaboration with Yale School of Medicine, Hana Kahleova and colleagues at the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine ran a 16-week single-arm trial (NCT06106035) in 25 adults with type 2 diabetes; Medical Dialogues covered the BMJ Open Diabetes Research & Care paper on October 31. Participants followed a low-fat vegan pattern (~75% carbohydrate, 15% protein, 10% fat—vegetables, whole grains, legumes, fruit; no animal products or added fats), eating to satiety without calorie targets, plus vitamin B12. Mean weight fell 6.0 kg (~−6.6%), fat mass −4.2 kg, and visceral fat volume −290 cm³; hepatocellular lipids dropped ~33%, AGE scores declined, HOMA-IR improved, PREDIM insulin sensitivity rose, and several meal-stimulated beta-cell measures improved; conference materials also reported ~1.0-point HbA1c reduction. Without a control arm, diet composition cannot be separated from energy intake and weight loss; the small sample limits generalization.

**Links:**

- [BMJ Open Diabetes Research & Care — Low-fat vegan diet, ectopic lipids, insulin sensitivity, and beta-cell function](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2026-006472)
- [Medical Dialogues — October 31 report on liver fat and insulin function after a low-fat vegan diet](https://medicaldialogues.in/diabetes-endocrinology/news/low-fat-vegan-diet-cuts-liver-fat-by-a-third-and-improves-insulin-function-in-adults-with-type-2-diabetes-finds-study-181435)

**Commentary:** If your clinician agrees, swap processed meats and added fats for legumes and whole grains and eat to natural fullness—do not read this as “unlimited vegan food cures diabetes,” and keep monitoring glucose and medications.

### 2. JACC: Advances UK Biobank cohort: Several healthy dietary patterns linked to lower CVD risk in cancer survivors (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Qing-Yuan Gao and colleagues analyzed about 8,716 UK Biobank cancer survivors free of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or stroke at baseline (mean age ~60; about two-thirds women), scoring eight healthy dietary patterns from 24-hour recalls. ACC and the journal highlighted the work around October 29–30; it appears in JACC: Advances. Over a median 12.3 years, 1,116 people had a primary CVD event. Comparing top vs bottom quintiles, HRs for total CVD were roughly 0.72 (Mediterranean), 0.74 (MIND), 0.63 (AHEI-2010), 0.65 (DASH), 0.82 (healthful plant-based), 0.67 (reversed energy-adjusted dietary inflammatory index), and 0.80 (healthy low-fat); a healthy low-carbohydrate pattern showed no significant benefit. At age 45, highest vs lowest adherence was associated with an estimated 1.7–3.3 extra years of life expectancy. Observational design, self-reported diet, and residual confounding limit causal claims.

**Links:**

- [JACC: Advances — Healthy dietary patterns and incident CVD in cancer survivors](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.103376)
- [ACC — Healthy diet may lower cardiovascular disease risk after cancer](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2026/10/30/14/29/Healthy-Diet-May-Lower-Cardiovascular-Disease-Risk-After-Cancer)

**Commentary:** Survivorship care does not need one “perfect” diet—Mediterranean, DASH, and AHEI-style patterns (more plants, whole grains; less ultra-processed sugar) have the clearer signal here, while a “healthy” low-carb score did not.

### 3. Nutrition Reviews meta-analysis of 92 RCTs: Low-fat vs high-fat diets lower non-HDL-C, with inconclusive ApoB evidence (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Searching through August 2025, investigators pooled 92 adult RCTs comparing low-fat (≤30% energy from fat) with high-fat (>30% energy from fat) diets; Nutrition Reviews published online October 15, 2026. Versus high-fat diets, low-fat diets reduced non-HDL-C by about 8.00 mg/dL (95% CI −13.1 to −2.89; high-certainty evidence) and also lowered ApoA1 and some HDL subclasses. Effects on ApoB, ApoB/ApoA1, and related measures were nonsignificant with low to very-low certainty. Total cholesterol/HDL-C and triglycerides/HDL-C ratios rose slightly (moderate certainty); Lp(a) was unchanged. Authors frame low-fat diets as a strategy to cut non-HDL-C without strong evidence of harm on lipoprotein ratios, while apo outcomes remain unsettled. Heterogeneity for non-HDL-C was high (I²≈78%); the contrast is not “high-quality low-fat vs high-quality high-fat.”

**Links:**

- [Nutrition Reviews — Low-fat vs high-fat diets on non-HDL-C, apolipoproteins, and lipoprotein ratios](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuag114)

**Commentary:** Keep lipid goals anchored on non-HDL-C/ApoB and overall diet quality—“just lower the fat percentage” is not a universal switch, and fat type and calories still matter.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. Diabetes Care UK Biobank accelerometry cohort: About 4 minutes/day of vigorous lifestyle activity micropatterns linked to lower incident type 2 diabetes (Training)
**Summary:** Among 22,706 UK Biobank accelerometry participants who reported no leisure-time exercise and had no type 2 diabetes at baseline (mean age 62.3; 56.4% women), 665 developed diabetes over ~7.9 years of follow-up. The Diabetes Care analysis—covered by Medscape and others—derived vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity (VILPA; bouts ≤1 min) and moderate-to-vigorous intermittent lifestyle activity (MV-ILPA; bouts ≤3 min) from wrist accelerometers. Median 3.9 min/day of VILPA vs 0 min/day was associated with ~36% lower diabetes risk (HR ~0.64); median 25.3 min/day of MV-ILPA vs a low referent was linked to ~46% lower risk. VILPA bout frequency showed a near-linear inverse association (median ~10.4 bouts/day HR 0.64). Observational design among non-exercisers cannot prove that “four minutes of hard bursts prevents diabetes.”

**Links:**

- [Diabetes Care — Dose–response of intermittent lifestyle activity micropatterns and incident type 2 diabetes](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc25-3018)
- [Medscape — Short daily movement bursts and type 2 diabetes risk](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/short-bursts-movement-daily-life-may-prevent-t2d-2026a1000qfw)

**Commentary:** If formal workouts are unrealistic, repeated short stairs or brisk hurries may still matter metabolically—this is association evidence; people with medical limits should clear intensity with a clinician first.

### 5. The Lancet Primary Care Jiangsu cluster RCT: A 12-month primary-care personalized exercise prescription did not beat usual care for diabetes prevention (Training)
**Summary:** A pragmatic, open-label cluster RCT across 100 communities/villages in 13 primary-care centers in six Jiangsu cities (ChiCTR2400081848) enrolled 2,918 adults aged 30–70 with ADA-defined prediabetes. The Lancet Primary Care paper, detailed in Chinese science media on October 30, assigned clusters to a 12-month primary-care-led, prescription-guided personalized exercise program (updated every 3–6 months, with guidance and motivational supports) plus standard care, versus standard care alone (general diet/activity advice plus four 30-minute exercise education sessions). After ~12 months’ median follow-up, diabetes incidence was 7.2% vs 6.8% (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.81–1.43; adjusted p=0.61). Meeting recommended activity levels stayed low with limited between-group separation; authors cite adherence, differentiation from usual care, and follow-up length as likely constraints. Adverse events were similar.

**Links:**

- [The Lancet Primary Care — Jiangsu primary-care personalized exercise prescription cluster RCT for diabetes prevention](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanprc.2026.100224)
- [Ebiotrade — October 30 analysis of the Jiangsu prediabetes exercise trial](https://www.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-10/20261030082527634.htm)

**Commentary:** An exercise prescription on paper does not equal diabetes prevention—real-world programs need measurable adherence, longer follow-up, and parallel diet/weight support, not just a rewritten plan.

### 6. Frontiers in Physiology three-level meta-analysis: Lower velocity-loss thresholds in resistance training slightly favor jump and sprint outcomes in trained people (Training)
**Summary:** Searching through April 10, 2026, reviewers included 12 RCTs (42 effect sizes) comparing velocity-loss (VL) thresholds during resistance training for jump and sprint performance in trained populations. A three-level random-effects meta-analysis found a small significant advantage for lower vs higher VL on combined jump/sprint outcomes (Hedges’ g=0.29, 95% CI 0.17–0.42). Domain effects favored jumps (g=0.27) and sprints (g=0.32); countermovement jump, 10 m, and 30 m were clearer, while squat jump and 20 m were not. Contrasts of 10% vs 30% and 10% vs 20% VL were stronger than 20% vs 40%. Certainty was low to moderate; moderator analyses were underpowered, so lower VL is an option for speed–power goals rather than a universal prescription.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Physiology — Velocity-loss thresholds, jump and sprint performance: systematic review and three-level meta-analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1893020/full)

**Commentary:** For speed–power blocks, consider stopping sets before bar speed collapses into slow grinders; general hypertrophy training can still prioritize volume and recovery.

## III. Sleep & Mental Health

### 7. Nature Mental Health meta-analysis of 19 RCTs: CBT-I modestly reduces suicidal ideation severity (Sleep)
**Summary:** Cagdas Türkmen and colleagues synthesized 19 RCTs in adults with insomnia (baseline N=4,974) comparing CBT-I (therapist-delivered or self-guided digital) with control conditions on suicidal ideation severity, suicidal behaviors, and suicide deaths; Nature Mental Health published October 23, 2026. Post-intervention, CBT-I was linked to a ~23% reduction in suicidal ideation severity versus controls (ratio of means 0.77, 95% CI 0.66–0.90; high-certainty evidence); follow-up differences had very low to moderate certainty. Across nine trials reporting attempts, two CBT-I participants and none in controls reported attempts; no suicide deaths occurred. Authors stress ideation is not behavior and position CBT-I as a potential adjunct to suicide prevention, with larger high-risk trials still needed.

**Links:**

- [Nature Mental Health — CBT-I as a suicide prevention strategy: systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-026-00743-0)

**Commentary:** Do not manage chronic insomnia with pills alone—seek guideline-directed CBT-I; if suicidal ideation is present, pair sleep care with urgent psychiatric/crisis support rather than treating CBT-I as a standalone fix.

### 8. AASM clinical practice guideline: For chronic insomnia, CBT-I plus medication beats medication alone, but should not routinely beat CBT-I alone (Sleep)
**Summary:** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine issued a clinical practice guideline on combination treatment for chronic insomnia disorder in adults (full text on PMC). It conditionally suggests starting CBT-I concurrently with insomnia medication over medication alone (low-certainty evidence), and conditionally suggests against routinely preferring CBT-I plus medication over CBT-I alone (low-certainty evidence). Versus pharmacotherapy alone, combination improved global insomnia severity and sleep continuity in clinically meaningful ways, with smaller daytime gains. Versus CBT-I alone, combination did not show clinically meaningful advantages on severity or continuity; total sleep time’s point estimate favored combination but the confidence interval included zero. A remark notes patients who value early total sleep time may reasonably choose combination.

**Links:**

- [AASM / PMC — Combination treatment for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: clinical practice guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13076838/)

**Commentary:** Keep CBT-I first-line; add medication as a short-term assist after shared decision-making—do not stack sedative-hypnotics on your own.

## IV. Public Health, Guidelines & Evidence Clarification

### 9. JAMA multicenter RCT: Among underserved adults referred for lung cancer screening, financial incentives plus free cessation meds nearly doubled 6-month sustained abstinence (Public health)
**Summary:** Investigators across four U.S. health systems (five centers) randomized currently smoking, medically underserved adults referred for low-dose CT lung screening; 3,220 were in the primary analysis (median age ~61). Penn Medicine, Henry Ford Health, and others reported the JAMA trial (NCT04798664). Arms were ask-advise-refer usual care; usual care plus free pharmacotherapy (NRT and reimbursement for varenicline/bupropion); that package plus up to $600 in biochemically verified incentives ($100 at 2 weeks, $200 at 3 months, $300 at 6 months); and the incentive package plus a future-thinking mobile tool. Six-month biochemically confirmed sustained abstinence was 4.3%, 5.1%, 8.8%, and 7.2%. Incentives beat usual care and usual care plus free meds; free meds alone were not superior to usual care. Absolute quit rates remained low; cost and sustainability of incentives matter for scale-up.

**Links:**

- [JAMA — Smoking cessation strategies among underserved patients referred for lung cancer screening](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.17192)
- [Penn Medicine — Cash rewards help underserved adults quit smoking](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/cash-rewards-help-underserved-adults-quit-smoking)

**Commentary:** Screening visits are cessation opportunities; for high-burden groups, verified incentives plus free meds may outperform referral alone—individuals still need concrete NRT/prescription and behavioral support.

### 10. AHA-led four-society Circulation trilogy: A Female Life Stage framework links puberty-to-postmenopause cardiovascular prevention (Guidelines)
**Summary:** On October 26, 2026, the American Heart Association, American Academy of Pediatrics, ACOG, and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine released a coordinated presidential advisory, scientific statement, and science advisory in Circulation. They propose four Female Life Stages—perimenarche (~8–15), ovarian cycling (~15–44), perimenopause (~45–51), and postmenopause (~≥51)—with pregnancy framed as a cardiovascular “stress test” that can occur in multiple stages. The package highlights menstrual patterns as a female-specific vital sign, pregnancy complications as risk enhancers, a large postpartum blood-pressure follow-up gap (~60% of women with hypertensive pregnancy complications receive BP follow-up within six months), and a call for Comprehensive Centers of Excellence for Women’s Cardiovascular Care. Scientific statements inform care but are not line-by-line prescribing guidelines.

**Links:**

- [AHA Newsroom — Leading health groups redefine heart health for girls and women](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/leading-health-groups-redefine-heart-health-for-girls-and-women)
- [AHA Professional — Recent guidelines and statements (including the women’s life-course set)](https://professional.heart.org/en/guidelines-statements)

**Commentary:** Put menstrual irregularities, pregnancy complications, and perimenopausal metabolic shifts into the cardiovascular history—do not wait for midlife for the first heart-risk conversation, and ask about postpartum blood-pressure follow-up.

### 11. American Family Physician review: Routine vitamin D screening and “treat-the-number” supplementation lack hard-outcome support in community-dwelling adults (Evidence clarification)
**Summary:** Raman Nohria and colleagues’ October 2026 AFP review concludes evidence does not support routine vitamin D screening or supplementation in the general population for hard outcomes such as all-cause mortality or cardiovascular health, regardless of baseline 25(OH)D. The National Academy of Medicine advises about 600 IU/day through age 70 and 800 IU/day thereafter via diet and/or supplements. Some people with high-risk prediabetes may benefit from ≥3 years of supplementation above recommended intake to slow progression to diabetes (SORT evidence rating B). RCTs for falls/fractures, cancer, dementia, infections, depression, and symptoms such as fatigue or pain are inconsistent. The tone aligns with 2024 Endocrine Society guidance that no longer defines deficiency by a single cut-point for empiric population care.

**Links:**

- [American Family Physician — Vitamin D supplementation in community-dwelling adults](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2026/1000/vitamin-d-supplementation-community-dwelling-adults)

**Commentary:** Meet recommended intake first; do not treat “lab value → mega-dose supplement” as default wellness—discuss higher-dose, longer courses only for defined situations such as high-risk prediabetes with a clinician.

### 12. BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders updated meta-analysis: Proprioceptive training linked to ~34% lower relative ankle injury occurrence in athletes (Sports medicine)
**Summary:** Juan Shen, Shiwei Chen, and Xue Han searched through June 5, 2026, and included 15 randomized or cluster-randomized trials of proprioceptive training alone or as an identifiable component of multicomponent injury-prevention programs. Published October 28, 2026, the pooled relative effect was 0.659 (95% CI 0.558–0.778; I²≈22.7%), about a 34% lower relative ankle-injury occurrence versus controls. Sport type and intervention composition did not show significant effect modification. Overall certainty was rated moderate; optimal dose, duration, and generalizability across competition levels remain open.

**Links:**

- [BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders — Proprioceptive training for prevention of ankle sprains in athletes](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10677-4)

**Commentary:** Build single-leg balance, unstable-surface work, and landing/cutting drills into warm-ups for jumping and field sports—weekly dose and long-term adherence matter more than a one-off preseason session.
