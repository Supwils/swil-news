## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. American Heart Association: DASH groceries did not push potassium into a dangerous range in people on common blood-pressure drugs, excluding advanced kidney disease and high baseline potassium (Nutrition)
**Summary:** On October 8 the American Heart Association lifted the embargo on preliminary research that Marian Budu and colleagues presented at Hypertension Scientific Sessions 2026 in Arlington, Virginia (October 7–11). The secondary analysis of GoFresh and GoFreshRx included 356 Black adults in Boston, mean age 53, 70% women, with systolic pressure from 120 to less than 150 mm Hg and diastolic pressure under 100 mm Hg. Half received three months of weekly DASH-patterned groceries providing about 4,700 mg of potassium and a potassium-to-sodium ratio of about 2:1, plus nutrition counseling; half received a cash stipend. Baseline serum potassium was 4.16 mmol/L in the grocery group and 4.18 mmol/L in the stipend group. Potassium did not rise significantly whether or not participants used ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, beta blockers, or NSAIDs, and medication subgroups within the grocery arm did not differ. This is a meeting abstract, not yet a peer-reviewed paper. People with stage 4–5 kidney disease or already-high baseline potassium were excluded. The American Heart Association funded the work.

**Links:**

- [EurekAlert — AHA release, October 8](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1146590)
- [News-Medical — same-day report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261008/Potassium-rich-DASH-diet-does-not-cause-hyperkalemia-in-individuals-taking-blood-pressure-drugs.aspx)

**Commentary:** People on common blood-pressure drugs whose potassium is already normal and who do not have advanced kidney disease can discuss eating more produce and low-fat dairy in a DASH pattern; this abstract does not set a safety boundary for stage 4–5 kidney disease or potassium that is already high.

### 2. Nature: in hospitalized transplant patients, about 100 extra grams of sweets during broad-spectrum antibiotics tracked with a further 24% drop in microbiome diversity (Nutrition)
**Summary:** A paper by Anqi Dai, Jonas Schluter, Jonathan U. Peled, Marcel R. M. van den Brink, and colleagues appeared in Nature on September 30 and was reported by CIDRAP around October 8. The team logged 9,419 meals from 173 patients hospitalized for hematopoietic cell transplantation and applied Bayesian inference to 158 patients with paired longitudinal microbiome samples, using 1,009 stool samples. Intake of sweets and sugars during antibiotic exposure predicted worse dysbiosis: lower alpha diversity and greater expansion of Enterococcus. CIDRAP reported that each additional 100 grams of sweets after antibiotic exposure was associated with about a further 24% decrease in alpha diversity; formulated drinks such as sports drinks and nutrition shakes were key contributors, and the authors did not equate that signal with naturally occurring sugars in whole foods. In mice, sucrose after antibiotics increased Enterococcus about 16-fold by day 3 and about 33-fold by day 6; sucrose without antibiotics did not.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Sugar-rich foods and antibiotic-related microbiome disruption](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11077-3)
- [CIDRAP — study report](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/antimicrobial-stewardship/study-suggests-sugar-rich-foods-amplify-antibiotic-disruption-gut)

**Commentary:** During broad-spectrum antibiotics, especially around transplant or intensive treatment, cutting sports drinks and nutrition shakes is closer to this evidence than adding a probiotic afterward; whether ordinary short courses in otherwise healthy people behave the same way still needs independent cohorts and trials.

### 3. Microbiome: 75 vegans without known disease showed bacteriome, virome, and multi-compartment metabolome profiles aligned with more favorable cardiometabolic markers (Nutrition)
**Summary:** Florence Thirion, Yong Fan, Tue H. Hansen, Isabel Garcia-Perez, Oluf Pedersen, and colleagues published a cross-sectional multi-omics analysis in Microbiome on October 7, comparing 75 vegans with 75 omnivores who had no known disease. They used shotgun sequencing of fecal DNA plus serum, fecal, and urine metabolomics and systemic cytokines. Vegan diets were higher in fiber and digestible carbohydrate and lower in protein and fat, and vegans showed lower glycemia, atherogenic plasma lipids, and systemic inflammation. Despite lower alpha diversity, vegans were enriched for taxa that tracked directly with fiber intake and scored favorably on the ZOE Microbiome Ranking 2024. Within vegans, bacterial diversity correlated positively with protein intake. In omnivores, microbiome-derived metabolites including trimethylamine-N-oxide and p-cresyl sulfate tracked with adverse metabolic and kidney markers. The protist Blastocystis was more prevalent in vegans and associated with lower systolic pressure. Diet and phenotype were measured together; people were not randomized to a vegan diet.

**Links:**

- [Microbiome — Vegan gut profiles and the multi-compartment metabolome](https://microbiomejournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40168-026-02538-y)

**Commentary:** For blood sugar and lipids, getting enough fiber and keeping protein in a plant-based plate fits this cross-section better than chasing a higher diversity number; the study does not prove that switching to vegan eating prevents heart disease, and it is not a reason to supplement Blastocystis or a single metabolite.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. Nutrients: in 13 young men, losing about 2.2% of body mass during exercise blunted several amino acids 60 minutes after 45 grams of whey (Training)
**Summary:** Zekun Xin, Mengtong Gao, Xinjie Wu, Peng Cheng, and Yi Yan at Beijing Sport University published a crossover trial in Nutrients on October 4; News-Medical reported it on October 8. Thirteen healthy young men (22.7 ± 3.3 years) completed three trials at least seven days apart: no fluid replacement, replacement of about half the fluid loss, and full replacement. Body-mass loss was 2.20 ± 0.19%, 1.18 ± 0.30%, and 0.27 ± 0.35%. Within five minutes after exercise they drank 45 grams of whey protein isolate in 450 mL of water. At 60 minutes, total amino acids, branched-chain amino acids, leucine, isoleucine, and glutamine were lower without fluid replacement than in both replacement trials (all p < 0.05). Incremental area under the curve from 0 to 120 minutes was also lower for total amino acids, branched-chain amino acids, isoleucine, and glutamine. Replacing half the loss looked similar to replacing all of it. Gastrointestinal symptoms stayed low. Ethics approval was 2025294H. The outcome was circulating amino acids, not muscle protein synthesis.

**Links:**

- [Nutrients — Exercise dehydration and the amino-acid response to whey](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/19/3267)
- [News-Medical — October 8 report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261008/Skipping-water-during-exercise-may-limit-your-bodys-response-to-whey-protein.aspx)

**Commentary:** Before the post-workout shake, replace about half the sweat lost in the session; this is an acute blood result in 13 young men, not evidence that drinking water by itself builds more muscle.

### 5. Frontiers in Physiology: across 12 randomized trials, lower velocity-loss thresholds had a small favorable effect on jump and sprint performance (Training)
**Summary:** Mingyue Yin of Shanghai University of Sport, Jie Zhang, and colleagues published a three-level random-effects meta-analysis in Frontiers in Physiology on October 6. Searches through April 10, 2026, yielded 12 randomized trials and 42 effect sizes in trained populations. Lower velocity-loss thresholds versus higher ones gave a pooled Hedges' g of 0.29 (95% CI 0.17–0.42) for combined jump and sprint performance; jump g was 0.27 (0.10–0.45) and sprint g was 0.32 (0.13–0.50). Countermovement jump and the 10 m and 30 m sprints were clearer; squat jump and the 20 m sprint were not statistically significant. Comparisons of 10% versus 30% and 10% versus 20% were significant; 20% versus 40% was not. Certainty of evidence was low to moderate. Registration: CRD420261396083.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Physiology — Velocity-loss thresholds, jumps, and sprints](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1893020/full)

**Commentary:** Trained people whose goal is jumping and short sprints can stop a set when bar speed has fallen by about 10%, which fits this small effect better than training down to a 20–30% loss; if the priority is maximal strength, stopping earlier is not a universal prescription, and the certainty is only low to moderate.

### 6. Sport Sciences for Health: with total rest fixed at 120 seconds, 12 resistance-trained women completed more knee extensions in cluster sets (Training)
**Summary:** Behzad Taati, Fatemeh Karami, Aysu Golchin Samadi, Georgian Badicu, and Luca Paolo Ardigò published a randomized crossover trial in Sport Sciences for Health on October 5. Twelve resistance-trained women each completed three sessions: traditional sets, and cluster sets with 10-second or 30-second intra-set rests, with total rest equated at 120 seconds and sessions a week apart. They performed four sets of bilateral knee extensions at 80% of 1RM, then a muscular-endurance test at 60% of 1RM. Repetitions were 18.4 ± 1.7 with 30-second clusters, 15.8 ± 2.2 with 10-second clusters, and 12.8 ± 1.2 with traditional sets (p < 0.0001). Post-session fatigue ratings were 4.08 ± 1.68, 5.25 ± 1.14, and 7.00 ± 0.74 (p = 0.0002). Fatigue-to-work and cardiovascular-load-to-work ratios were lower in both cluster conditions. Post-set blood pressure, heart rate, and rate-pressure product themselves did not differ. This is one session, not a multi-week hypertrophy trial.

**Links:**

- [Sport Sciences for Health — Cluster sets versus traditional sets](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-026-01944-x)

**Commentary:** If total rest stays the same, splitting it inside the set can buy more repetitions and less perceived fatigue in that session; twelve women and knee extensions only do not make this a long-term muscle-building plan for every lift.

## III. Sleep & Mental Health

### 7. Sleep Science and Practice: in a U.S. cross-section, each extra lifestyle point tracked with about 0.066 more hours of sleep, and depression scores explained part of the link (Sleep)
**Summary:** Hongxia Zhou, Jiafei Yang, and colleagues at Jiaxing University published a weighted cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2013–2020 in Sleep Science and Practice on October 5. The analytic sample was 5,532 adults aged 20 and older (3,857 men and 1,675 women). A 0–6 lifestyle score covered nonsmoking, limited alcohol, body weight, diet quality, activity, and less sedentary time; depression used the PHQ-9. Each additional lifestyle point was associated with 0.066 more hours of sleep (95% CI 0.027–0.105) and an odds ratio of 0.786 (0.730–0.847) for trouble sleeping. Versus scores of 0–2, scores of 5–6 were associated with 0.258 more hours (0.121–0.394) and an odds ratio of 0.413 (0.317–0.537) for trouble sleeping. In an exploratory mediation model, depression accounted for 34.23% of the sleep-duration association and 46.01% of the trouble-sleeping association. The authors state that a cross-sectional design cannot support causal claims.

**Links:**

- [Sleep Science and Practice — Lifestyle, depression, and sleep](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41606-026-00204-8)

**Commentary:** When sleep is short, look at smoking, alcohol, weight, diet, activity, and sitting time together rather than only staying in bed longer; 0.066 hours is about four minutes, and this cross-section cannot say whether depression or poor sleep came first.

## IV. Public Health & Mechanisms

### 8. Nature: in mice, a gut-microbe fatty acid cut atherosclerotic plaque burden by about half at the same enzyme statins target (Mechanism)
**Summary:** Chao Yin, Youzhe Chen, Gan Lin, Mingwei Cai, and colleagues published a mouse study in Nature on October 7. Giving Bacteroides uniformis to atherosclerosis-prone mice lessened atherosclerosis through higher hepatic LDL-receptor expression. Bioactivity-guided screening identified pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), a saturated odd-chain fatty acid, as a principal metabolite. Supplementing it reduced plaque burden by about 50% and improved plasma lipids. Mechanistically it directly inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the statin target enzyme, which suppresses hepatic cholesterol synthesis and promotes clearance of plasma LDL. The authors also write that pentadecanoic acid is markedly depleted in patients with dyslipidemia. This is an animal experiment plus a difference in human levels; the abstract does not give a human supplement dose.

**Links:**

- [Nature — A microbial odd-chain fatty acid and atherosclerosis in mice](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11142-x)
- [Nature — same-day news explainer](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03189-7)

**Commentary:** High lipids still belong with prescribed drugs and diet changes; a roughly 50% plaque reduction in mice is not a reason to buy pentadecanoic acid, and the human finding so far is that people with dyslipidemia have lower levels.

## V. Sports Medicine

### 9. Adelaide commentary: novice runners see about 18 injuries per 1,000 hours; strength training did not, on average, cut running injuries unless it was supervised (Sports medicine)
**Summary:** Hunter Bennett of Adelaide University published a commentary, not a new randomized trial, in The Conversation on October 8. He writes that studies of novice runners report about 18 injuries per 1,000 hours, more than double the rate in more advanced runners, mostly at the knee, ankle, and lower leg, and mostly overuse. He cites a meta-analysis of 25 studies and 26,610 participants in which strength training reduced sports injuries by more than 60%, and a separate runner-specific meta-analysis in which strength training on average did not reduce running injuries, while supervised programs did, which he attributes to adherence. The piece says stretching lacks strong evidence for injury prevention, that aerobic fitness can improve within about two weeks, and that tendons typically need at least 12 weeks of consistent heavier loading. He suggests adding no more than about a couple of kilometres a week and prioritizing calf raises, split squats, squats, and deadlifts. The commentary does not reprint the original pooled effect sizes.

**Links:**

- [The Conversation — October 8 commentary on running injuries](https://theconversation.com/why-do-runners-get-so-many-injuries-and-does-strength-training-really-make-a-difference-292788)

**Commentary:** When runs start to feel easier, add only about two kilometres a week and keep calf- and hip-dominant strength work supervised enough to finish it; a greater-than-60% injury reduction comes from sports pooled together and is not the running-specific estimate.
