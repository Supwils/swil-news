## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Science: A 12-week inulin + resistant starch + β-glucan fiber mix improved PMOS androgens and ovulation via gut androgen conversion (nutrition)
**Summary:** On Nov 5, 2026, Qiao, Pang and colleagues reported in Science a clinical-plus-mechanism study in polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS; the consensus rename of polycystic ovary syndrome). A 12-week dietary-fiber combination of inulin, resistant starch, and β-glucan lowered plasma androgens and restored ovulatory function. Metagenomics showed higher Bacteroides xylanisolvens, inversely correlated with androstenedione and testosterone; in vitro and mouse work indicated the microbe converts androstenedione via microbial 5α-reductase and coproandrosterone synthetase into a less androgenic “secondary hormone” metabolite (coproandrosterone), further reduced by Bifidobacterium longum and others. The public abstract does not state a verifiable enrollment n; mouse/in vitro findings are not clinical prescriptions.

**Links:**

- [Science — Dietary fiber improves PMOS via hormone conversion](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aed8952)

**Commentary:** If you have PMOS/PCOS features, prioritize everyday fermentable fiber (whole grains, legumes, oats)—but do not self-dose high-potency prebiotic powders in place of specialist care, especially if you are on medication or trying to conceive.

### 2. Nature Medicine: In 6,803 Japanese adults, opposing lifestyle–microbiome axes track health versus chronic disease (nutrition/lifestyle)
**Summary:** Using fecal shotgun metagenomes from 6,803 adults in Japan’s 4D cohort (about 58.2% male; mean age ~60.9), researchers linked 28 modifiable lifestyles, general health, and 15 chronic diseases; the paper went online Nov 5, 2026. Plant-based foods, soy, and dairy aligned with a health-oriented mediating axis rich in dietary-metabolizing functions and butyrate-linked anti-inflammatory profiles; refined carbohydrates and sedentary behavior aligned with a disease-oriented axis enriched in oral-derived taxa and pathobionts. Starter cultures from fermented foods did not show statistically significant mediation. Microbiome-based MADE scores distinguished diseases across 13 external cohorts. The core analysis is largely cross-sectional, so reverse causation remains possible.

**Links:**

- [Nature Medicine — Opposing lifestyle–microbiome mediation axes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04744-5)

**Commentary:** “Gut health” here means less refined carbs, more movement, and a plant-forward/fermented-food pattern—not faith in a single labeled starter strain.

### 3. Surrey × Queen’s Belfast modelling in Lancet Planetary Health: Replacing dairy with plant-based alternatives at current fortification raises iodine and other micronutrient inadequacy risk (nutrition/public health)
**Summary:** Nicol et al. modelled equal-weight substitution of milk, yoghurt, and cheese using UK NDNS data from more than 3,500 people aged 18 months–100 years. University and Science Media Centre notices date the Lancet Planetary Health paper to Nov 4, 2026, with continued Nov 5 coverage. Under current market fortification, replacing just 20%–35% of dairy raised iodine inadequacy from about 54% to 65% in adolescent females and from about 32% to 42% in women 19–49; full replacement reached about 80% and 72% in adolescent females and males. Mandatory fortification of plant-based dairy alternatives prevented most declines, though iodine inadequacy in adolescent females remained about 59%; adding iodine-fortified bread lowered it to about 37%. This is scenario modelling of intake inadequacy risk, not confirmed clinical deficiency.

**Links:**

- [The Lancet Planetary Health — Plant-based dairy substitution modelling (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanplh.2026.101515)
- [University of Surrey — Nov 4 news release](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/replacing-dairy-products-unfortified-plant-based-alternatives-could-lead-deficiencies-certain)

**Commentary:** When swapping to oat milk or plant yoghurt, check fortification for iodine, calcium, riboflavin, and B12—especially relevant where salt is not iodised; adolescents and women of reproductive age should not treat “plant-based” as nutrient-equivalent by default.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. Sports Medicine - Open systematic review/meta-analysis: Plyometric jump training improves jump, sprint, and change-of-direction in adult female team-sport athletes (training)
**Summary:** Searching through March 2026, authors included 31 studies (n = 609) of healthy adult female team-sport athletes (mean age ≥18), with plyometric jump training (PJT) ≥2 weeks and comparator groups; science media highlighted the paper on Nov 5, 2026. Versus controls, PJT improved countermovement-jump height (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.55), drop-jump reactive strength index (g ≈ 0.93), 10/20/30 m sprint times, and Illinois/T-test agility times; interventions ≥8 weeks favoured DJ RSI, 10 m sprint, and Illinois agility. Certainty was mostly low to moderate; some sprint/agility prediction intervals crossed null. Two studies reported three possible injuries. The synthesis fills a gap left by male-, mixed-, and adolescent-heavy prior reviews.

**Links:**

- [Sports Medicine - Open — PJT in female team-sport athletes](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01136-4)
- [Bioengineer.org — Nov 5 coverage](https://bioengineer.org/plyometric-jump-training-boosts-jump-sprint-and-agility-performance-in/)

**Commentary:** Female team programs can add 8+ weeks of landing- and COD-focused plyometrics as sport-specific work—after progressive loading and landing technique, because pooled gains still leave room for team-to-team variability.

### 5. Frontiers in Physiology multilevel meta-analysis: Blood-flow restriction training may modestly raise BMD, not proven for fracture prevention (training)
**Summary:** Searching to Sep 1, 2026 across nine databases, 22 reports were included with outcome domains and acute-versus-chronic contrasts separated; the article is dated Nov 5, 2026. Blood-flow restriction training (BFRT) showed a small positive BMD effect (SMD ≈ 0.20, 95% CI 0.08–0.32). A pooled estimate across heterogeneous regulatory biomarkers was exploratorily positive with a prediction interval including no effect; clear pooled effects were not seen for acute or chronic bone-turnover markers. Advantages versus conventional high-load training remain uncertain, and evidence is insufficient for fracture prevention or long-term clinical benefit. GRADE/RoB ratings underscore limited certainty and protocol heterogeneity.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Physiology — BFRT and bone-related outcomes](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1864772/full)

**Commentary:** If heavy loading is not feasible, low-load BFR under proper protocols can be a strength option—but do not treat it as a standalone “bone builder”; calcium/vitamin D status, impact loading, and fall prevention remain the skeletal base.

### 6. BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation review: Sleep extension aids athletic performance and cognition; restriction harms; naps depend on duration and context (training/sleep)
**Summary:** Searching experimental studies from 2011–2025, 15 RCTs/crossovers were included (open access around Nov 3, 2026). Narrative synthesis linked sleep extension to better shuttle-run distance, lower fatigue index, faster reaction time, improved sprinting and accuracy; endurance roughly +3% with extension versus about −3% with restriction, plus higher fatigue and cognitive decrements. Naps of 60–90 minutes related to higher MVC, greater distance, and lower perceived fatigue/sleepiness; ~20-minute naps were mixed and occasionally slightly worse for sprint time. Designs and outcomes were too heterogeneous for formal meta-analysis.

**Links:**

- [BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation — Sleep manipulation and performance](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-02218-2)

**Commentary:** Before a key session or match, prioritize extra sleep over another hard workout; if short on sleep, a 60–90 minute nap may protect performance better than caffeine alone—short naps are not a universal fix.

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 7. WHO NTD Global Report 2026: 63 countries have eliminated at least one NTD; Solomon Islands scabies prevalence fell ~83% after ivermectin MDA (public health)
**Summary:** On Nov 4, 2026, WHO released its midterm assessment of the 2021–2030 NTD road map: by June 2026, 63 countries had eliminated at least one NTD (up from 42 at end-2020); people needing interventions fell to about 1.41 billion in 2024 (−36% since 2010; −19% since 2020). In parallel, Nature Health national surveys found that after two ivermectin-based mass drug administration rounds (~2022–2024) in Solomon Islands, scabies prevalence fell from about 23.2% to 3.9% (~83% relative reduction) and impetigo from about 9.2% to 1.6%; Fiji also declined after one round. The report flags dengue resurgence and data gaps; children under five remain especially vulnerable to scabies.

**Links:**

- [WHO — Progress against NTDs requires sustained action](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-11-2026-progress-against-neglected-tropical-diseases-requires-sustained-action)
- [Nature Health — Scabies/impetigo after MDA in Solomon Islands and Fiji](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44360-026-00162-6)

**Commentary:** For individuals: take clustered itch seriously after travel to endemic areas; for systems: community MDA can move prevalence, but progress needs ongoing surveillance and pediatric follow-up—not a one-and-done campaign.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 8. The Lancet Regional Health – Europe: Fully automated digital CBT-I (SHUTi) still beats online education at ~2 years, with attenuated effects and high attrition (sleep)
**Summary:** In Norway, 1,720 adults with ISI ≥12 were randomised to fully automated digital CBT-I (SHUTi; six cores over ~6 weeks) or static online patient education; the primary 9-week endpoint was published earlier, and this follow-up—covered Nov 5, 2026—reports 6-month and ~2-year outcomes. At a median 28.3 months, only 34.1% (587/1,720) provided data; mean ISI was about 10.7 with dCBT-I versus 13.4 with education (estimated difference −1.77, 95% CI −2.65 to −0.90; d ≈ −0.43). Cost–QALY analyses suggested likely cost-effectiveness over two years; effects were smaller than at 9 weeks and sensitive to missing-data assumptions. Shift workers and people with apnea/hypersomnia were excluded.

**Links:**

- [The Lancet Regional Health – Europe — Digital CBT-I 2-year follow-up](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanepe/article/PIIS2666-7762(26)00103-1/fulltext)
- [Health Chosun — Nov 5 coverage](https://health.chosun.com/english/site/data/html_dir/2026/11/05/2026110502868.html)

**Commentary:** For chronic insomnia, try structured digital or in-person CBT-I before defaulting to sleeping pills—and plan for booster skills, because a one-time app course is unlikely to lock in lifelong gains.

### 9. Nature Mental Health meta-analysis: CBT-I linked to ~23% lower post-treatment suicidal ideation severity; follow-up effects unclear (sleep/mental health)
**Summary:** Nineteen RCTs (baseline N ≈ 4,974 adults with insomnia) compared CBT-I with control conditions on suicidal ideation severity, suicidal behaviours, and suicide deaths. After treatment, ideation severity was about 23% lower with CBT-I (ratio of means 0.77, 95% CI 0.66–0.90; high-certainty per authors)—a relative score difference, not a 23-percentage-point drop in the share of people with ideation. Between-group differences at later follow-ups were unclear (very low to moderate certainty). Across nine trials, two CBT-I participants reported suicide attempts versus none in controls; no suicide deaths occurred. Ideation is not behaviour; high-risk patients still need specialist pathways.

**Links:**

- [Nature Mental Health — CBT-I as a suicide-prevention strategy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-026-00743-0)

**Commentary:** When insomnia coexists with low mood, CBT-I is an evidence-supported add-on—but clear suicide risk requires crisis care and psychiatry, not a sleep app alone.

## V. Evidence & Clarifications

### 10. South Korea MFDS × KCA: 37 of 60 cross-border “height growth/focus” supplements contained banned/restricted ingredients, including 5-HTP (evidence/food safety)
**Summary:** MFDS and the Korea Consumer Agency tested 60 overseas direct-purchase products marketed for height growth or cognitive focus; about 61.7% (37) contained ingredients not permitted in Korean foods/supplements. NutraIngredients and Korean media covered the action on Nov 5, 2026. Height products frequently listed 5-HTP (13 of 19 claimed it, mostly U.S.-sourced), plus L-dopa, L-citrulline, and dolomite; focus products included vinpocetine, L-dopa, and bacopa, among others. Authorities initiated customs holds and sales blocks and added products to the hazard database. In Korea, 5-HTP is treated as a prescription-drug ingredient with possible blood-pressure and GI effects and serotoninergic interaction risks; “height supplements” lack a reliable pediatric prescribing role.

**Links:**

- [NutraIngredients — MFDS flags 5-HTP height/brain supplements](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/11/05/mfds-flags-height-growth-brain-supplements-containing-5-htp/)
- [YTN — Nov 5 KCA interview coverage](https://www.ytn.co.kr/_ln/0102_202611050715597840)

**Commentary:** Do not buy overseas “smart drugs” or “growth powders” for exam or height anxiety—check official blocked-product lists and only supplement against documented needs with clinician input.

### 11. Nutrients replication RCT: 3.5 g/day resistant potato starch again raised Bifidobacterium and normal stool share, but Akkermansia versus placebo missed the primary endpoint (evidence clarification)
**Summary:** Healthy adults repeated the 2023 protocol: 4 weeks, double-blind, parallel 3.5 g Solnul resistant potato starch versus digestible corn-starch placebo. NutraIngredients discussed the trial on Nov 5, 2026 (paper published late October). About 74 were randomised and ~70 analysed. Versus placebo, Bifidobacterium rose in the per-protocol set (secondary) and Bristol Type 3–4 stools were more common; Akkermansia did not differ from placebo on the primary contrast (within-group rise from baseline only). Bowel-movement frequency rose within the starch arm but not versus placebo; days without a bowel movement fell more than placebo. The first author is a company employee with no external funding; effects are small in healthy people and limited for constipation/IBS extrapolation.

**Links:**

- [Nutrients — Solnul resistant starch replication trial](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18213538)
- [NutraIngredients — Nov 5 on prebiotic reproducibility](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/11/05/solnul-study-examines-questions-about-prebiotic-reproducibility/)

**Commentary:** Prebiotic claims need the triad of which microbe, in whom, and versus placebo—reproduced bifidogenesis/stool form is not an Akkermansia miracle; whole-food fiber still comes first.
