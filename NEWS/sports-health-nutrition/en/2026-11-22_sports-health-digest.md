## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Food-provided RCT: with matched ~10% weight loss, keto cut liver fat ~67% vs ~45% on Mediterranean or very-low-fat diets (Nutrition)
**Summary:** A Cell Metabolism randomized trial analyzed 42 adults with obesity, prediabetes and fatty liver who completed assigned ketogenic, Mediterranean or very-low-fat plant-forward diets. All meals were provided with dietitian support so each group lost about 10% of body weight over roughly 4–5 months. Muscle insulin sensitivity rose ~50% in all arms, but hepatic insulin sensitivity improved two- to three-fold more on keto; intrahepatic triglyceride fell about 67% on keto versus about 45% on the other diets. Keto produced larger glucagon rises, insulin falls and drops in hepatic de novo lipogenesis; about 50% of keto participants no longer met prediabetes criteria (about 29% Mediterranean, about 7% very-low-fat). LDL-C, apoB and 24-hour triglycerides did not differ significantly among groups during follow-up.

**Links:**

- [ScienceDaily — Keto diet slashes liver fat by 67%](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261120070904.htm)
- [Cell Metabolism — Diet macronutrient content and cardiometabolic response to weight loss](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/article/S1550-4131(26)00293-7/pdf)

**Commentary:** This was a small, short, fully food-provided trial—evidence that carb restriction can clear liver fat beyond matched weight loss, not a license for everyone to go keto; people with fatty liver/prediabetes should choose a pattern they can sustain under clinical follow-up, including lipids and kidney function.

### 2. Secondary analysis: post-exercise lean pork raised plasma dileucine, weakly tied to muscle protein synthesis change (Nutrition/Training)
**Summary:** University of Illinois investigators published a Molecular Nutrition & Food Research secondary analysis of plasma from two RCTs (~26 healthy adults, mean age ~25). After resistance exercise, participants ingested ~20 g protein from low-fat ground pork, high-fat ground pork, rice-and-beans, an iso-nitrogenous mixed nutrient drink, or a carbohydrate control, with sampling to 300 minutes. Only low-fat pork significantly raised plasma dileucine at 90–120 minutes (reported peak ~10.3 nmol/L, higher than high-fat pork, plant meal and drink controls); dileucine Cmax correlated modestly with the change in muscle protein synthesis (r≈0.44), while leucine Cmax did not. Authors stress that most whole-food proteins do not elevate dileucine and that correlation does not prove lean pork yields greater hypertrophy.

**Links:**

- [PMC — Whole-food protein sources and post-exercise plasma dileucine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13678408/)
- [Health Chosun — Post-workout lean pork raises blood di-leucine](https://health.chosun.com/english/site/data/html_dir/2026/11/22/2026112201630.html)

**Commentary:** A mechanistic clue is not a “must eat pork to build muscle” rule; after training, prioritize adequate high-quality protein and comfortable digestion—lean meat, whey or complete plant combinations all work without chasing one peptide peak.

### 3. Meta-analysis of 24 RCTs: high-protein diets yielded only modest glycemic shifts in type 2 diabetes, with uneven certainty (Nutrition)
**Summary:** A Diabetology & Metabolic Syndrome systematic review and meta-analysis searched through November 2025 and pooled 24 RCTs comparing higher- versus standard/lower-protein diets in adults with type 2 diabetes. Random-effects estimates showed fasting glucose WMD about −5.05 mg/dL (95% CI −8.90 to −1.20), HbA1c about −0.35%, fasting insulin about −2.63 μIU/mL and HOMA-IR about −1.32; most trials had high risk of bias (blinding impractical), and GRADE rated FBS evidence moderate, HbA1c low, and insulin/HOMA-IR very low. Authors note the FBS effect hinged heavily on individual studies and lost significance after trim-and-fill, with no confirmed subgroup or weight-loss–independent extra benefit.

**Links:**

- [Ebiotrade — High-protein diet and T2D glycemic control meta-analysis](https://n.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-11/202611212042130662.htm)
- [Scienmag — High-protein diets modestly lower fasting glucose and insulin in T2D](https://scienmag.com/high-protein-diets-modestly-lower-fasting-glucose-and-insulin-in-type-2-diabetes/)

**Commentary:** Treat a modest protein bump—favoring plant and lean sources—as a plate-level assist for glycemic meals, not a drug or weight-loss substitute; do not inflate a small pooled mean into “high protein reverses diabetes.”

### 4. Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis: protein intake showed weak positive links to lean tissue and strength, without a clear optimal grams/kg (Evidence check)
**Summary:** A SportRxiv preprint updated a systematic review with Bayesian model-averaged dose-response meta-analysis (searched to 14 September 2026): 119 parallel-group RCTs identified, 95 contributing 254 arms and about 4,931 participants to primary analyses. Relative protein intake (g/kg/day) generally showed modest positive associations with whole-body lean/fat-free mass, muscle size, strength and function—especially with resistance training—but contrasts of roughly ±0.2–0.5 g/kg/day rarely met a preregistered meaningful-effect threshold, and no clear optimal intake emerged. Most trials raised bias concerns; publication-bias adjustment pulled estimates toward zero, and GRADE certainty was mostly low or very low.

**Links:**

- [SportRxiv — Protein intake dose-response and lean mass, strength, function](https://doi.org/10.51224/sportrxiv.1166)

**Commentary:** Aiming near the upper end of guideline protein ranges is reasonable if maximizing training adaptations, but there is no magic gram target; lock in resistance training and energy balance first, then fine-tune protein.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 5. Six-week RCT: with equal 45 minutes of brisk walking, frequent short bouts beat one continuous walk for glucose stability (Training)
**Summary:** An Obesity randomized trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02998892) enrolled 30 sedentary adults with overweight/obesity (mean age ~34) who completed 45 minutes/day of brisk walking, 5 days/week for 6 weeks: BREAK as nine ~5-minute bouts across the day versus ONE as a single continuous session. Accelerometry and doubly labeled water showed similar changes in total sedentary time, activity and energy expenditure, but only BREAK reduced prolonged ≥60-minute sitting bouts. Neither arm improved whole-body insulin sensitivity; BREAK improved IVGTT glucose AUC more, raised continuous-glucose time-in-range from about 82% to 94% (ONE fell from about 89% to 80%), and lowered coefficient of variation and average daily risk ratio.

**Links:**

- [CU Anschutz — Short walking breaks may improve blood sugar control](https://news.cuanschutz.edu/health-and-wellness/new-research-short-walking-breaks-may-improve-blood-sugar-control)
- [Medical News Today — Short walks throughout the day and blood sugar](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/short-walking-breaks-may-offer-advantage-blood-sugar-management)

**Commentary:** Small, 6-week, and without a no-exercise control—but actionable: hourly short walks at a desk job may stabilize daytime glucose swings better than saving all exercise for one evening block.

### 6. Nature Medicine RCT: adding resistance training to tirzepatide spared ~1 kg lean soft tissue and preserved strength (Training)
**Summary:** In a two-arm RCT (ACTRN12623001236684), 120 adults with overweight or obesity on tirzepatide were randomized to thrice-weekly resistance exercise or usual activity for 40 weeks; resistance adherence was about 91%. Weight loss was similar (about −22.6 vs −22.3 kg) as was fat-mass loss, but resistance training reduced loss of total lean soft tissue (−3.57 vs −4.67 kg; between-group difference about 1.09 kg, 95% CI 0.43–1.75) and appendicular lean soft tissue. Strength fell with tirzepatide alone on bench press, lat pulldown and knee extension, but rose with combined training (all P < 0.001 versus drug alone).

**Links:**

- [Nature Medicine — Resistance exercise attenuates lean mass loss during tirzepatide](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04752-5)

**Commentary:** Pharmacologic weight loss is not “free” muscle; if using GLP-1/dual agonists, write resistance training into the care plan early to protect lean mass and function.

### 7. Meta-analysis of 27 RCTs: neuromuscular training may lower athletes’ lower-limb injury risk, but dose claims remain very uncertain (Training/Injury)
**Summary:** A Sports Medicine - Open systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 27 RCTs (27,663 athletes). Neuromuscular training (NMT) versus control had a lower-limb injury risk ratio of about 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.82), attenuated after trim-and-fill; structured and multidimensional programmes showed favourable point estimates without significant between-modality differences. Exploratory subgroups favoured programmes 2–3 times/week, 16–25 minutes/session, over about 1.5–12 months, but authors warn these are study-level associations—not causal optimal doses—and overall certainty was very low.

**Links:**

- [Sports Medicine - Open — Neuromuscular training modalities, dose and lower-limb injury](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01142-6)

**Commentary:** Team and jump sports can fold strength, balance, agility and landing control into warm-ups a few times weekly for 15–25 minutes; consistency beats obsessing over an “optimal minute count.”

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 8. Brazilian field study: neighborhoods with Wolbachia mosquitoes saw ~69% fewer dengue/chikungunya infections in schoolchildren (Public health)
**Summary:** Reported at the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene meeting, a four-year Belo Horizonte study blood-tested more than 5,700 children, comparing neighborhoods that received Wolbachia-carrying Aedes aegypti releases versus similar areas under the same pesticide and routine mosquito control. Wolbachia neighborhoods showed about a 69% reduction in combined dengue and chikungunya infections in schoolchildren, sustained through Brazil’s severe 2024 dengue season; Zika cases were too few to assess. The approach, advanced by groups such as the World Mosquito Program, uses bacteria harmless to people that spread to offspring once established in local mosquitoes.

**Links:**

- [Los Angeles Times — Wolbachia mosquitoes and dengue evidence](https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-11-22/scientists-report-strong-new-evidence-that-specially-bred-mosquitoes-can-fend-off-dengue)
- [The Straits Times — Wolbachia mosquitoes cut dengue infections in Brazil](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/scientists-report-strong-new-evidence-that-specially-bred-mosquitoes-can-fend-off-dengue)

**Commentary:** This is community vector control, not a personal supplement; in endemic or travel settings, keep personal bite prevention and standing-water cleanup, and treat vaccines/mosquito control as complementary.

### 9. WHO guideline: insulin use for adults with type 1 diabetes, including emergency and humanitarian settings (Guideline)
**Summary:** On 12 November 2026 WHO published evidence-based guidelines on insulin use for adults with type 1 diabetes across diverse systems, including low-resource, emergency and humanitarian contexts (~125 pages). The guideline strongly recommends intensified multiple daily injections with dose adjustment to glucose values, while allowing context-adapted approaches in crises; both human insulin and analogues may be used, with human insulin generally preferred for lower cost. Coverage includes diagnosis/classification, delivery methods, self-monitoring and clinical monitoring, supporting the 2030 aim of universal affordable insulin and glucose monitoring.

**Links:**

- [WHO — Guidelines on insulin use for type 1 diabetes in adults](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240126244)

**Commentary:** For people living with T1D, the actionable core is sticking to monitor-and-adjust intensified regimens when possible, and planning backup insulin/monitoring with clinicians for travel, supply gaps or disasters.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 10. Nature Communications: human temporal-lobe concept neurons reactivate in slow-wave sleep by content, not event order (Sleep)
**Summary:** Published 22 November 2026, an intracranial recording study sampled 1,415 neurons in neurosurgical patients who learned a story involving specific person/object concepts. After learning, concept neurons reactivated during slow-wave sleep—often conjointly during sharp-wave ripples at lags suitable for synaptic modification—but the temporal sequence of reactivation did not replay the story’s event order. Authors contrast rodent place-cell replay: human concept cells are semantically tuned before learning, so sleep reactivation consolidates content rather than chronological experience sequences.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Concept neuron reactivation during sleep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-79544-z)

**Commentary:** Mechanistic work is not a sleep-gadget prescription, but it reinforces a practical habit: after learning something important, protect slow-wave–rich sleep instead of sacrificing rest for another late-night review pass.

### 11. Meta-analysis of 80 RCTs: resistance training reduced depressive symptoms with small-to-moderate effects, including versus other active comparators (Mental health/Training)
**Summary:** A JAMA Network Open meta-analysis and meta-regression pooled 80 RCTs (6,123 participants; mean age ~57). Resistance exercise training (RET) showed a heterogeneous antidepressant effect versus all comparisons (Δ≈0.32), larger versus inactive controls (Δ≈0.54) than versus active comparators such as aerobic, relaxation or flexibility work (Δ≈0.21). Versus active comparisons, effects were larger in people with mental illness (Δ≈0.40); versus inactive comparisons, point estimates were larger in all-female cohorts, ≥4 sessions/week and sessions ≤30 minutes. Authors conclude RET can yield clinically meaningful symptom reductions and support recommending it in depression care.

**Links:**

- [JAMA Network Open — Resistance exercise training for depressive symptoms](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2856737)
- [PMC — Resistance Exercise Training for Depressive Symptoms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13776590/)

**Commentary:** When mood is low, strength training can sit alongside aerobic exercise as a prescribed option; moderate-to-severe depression still needs professional care—exercise assists, it does not replace therapy or medication when indicated.

### 12. Observational link: higher serum vitamin D associated with longer, less fragmented actigraphic sleep in midlife/older adults (Sleep/Evidence check)
**Summary:** Johns Hopkins and National Institute on Aging investigators analyzed 460 cognitively unimpaired adults aged ≥40 (mean ~71) in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (The Journals of Gerontology: Series A). Higher serum vitamin D associated with longer total sleep time and shorter average wake-bout length; vitamin D deficiency associated with about 23 minutes less total sleep. Associations appeared stronger among Black participants; vitamin B12–sleep links differed by sex and were inconclusive. The design was serial cross-sectional and cannot prove supplements improve sleep or separate sunlight, diet and pill sources.

**Links:**

- [Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health — Vitamin D levels linked to sleep quality](https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2026/vitamin-d-levels-linked-to-sleep-quality)
- [PMC — Association of vitamin D and B12 with actigraphic sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13577193/)

**Commentary:** Test for deficiency before supplementing—do not treat vitamin D as a sleeping pill; for poor sleep, prioritize schedule, screens and possible sleep apnea evaluation first.
