## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. WHI cohort: saturated fat’s links to LDL-C and ASCVD amplify with high LDL polygenic risk (Nutrition)
**Summary:** A Stanford-led analysis in *JACC: Advances* studied 20,940 genotyped Women’s Health Initiative participants, combining food-frequency saturated-fat intake with a multiancestry LDL polygenic score (PGS LDL). The saturated fat–LDL-C association was modified by PGS LDL (interaction P=0.01), with stronger effects at the high end of the score. Among 16,835 women without baseline ASCVD, 1,582 incident events occurred over ~10 years median follow-up; a 5% isocaloric increase in saturated fat raised ASCVD risk only in the top PGS LDL quartile (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.08–1.47), with no association in the lower three quartiles (HR 1.00). Authors caution that polygenic scores are not yet ready to prescribe individualized fat limits.

**Links:**

- [JACC: Advances — Saturated fat, PGS LDL and ASCVD](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.103423)
- [Stanford Medicine — Diet swings cholesterol more in high genetic risk](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/11/cholesterol-diet.html)

**Commentary:** If family lipids run high or genetic LDL risk is known to be elevated, trimming red meat and butter-style saturated fat may matter more for you than for low-risk peers—and higher intake showed no cardiovascular upside even at lower genetic risk.

### 2. Three Nordic birth cohorts: childhood fruit and fish linked to lower Crohn’s risk; teen salty snacks trend the other way (Nutrition)
**Summary:** *Gut* pooled MoBa (Norway), DNBC (Denmark), and ABIS (Sweden): 111,999 participants, ~1.54 million person-years, and 653 IBD cases through 2024. High vs low childhood fruit intake (~ages 7–8) was associated with lower Crohn’s disease risk (aHR 0.66) but higher ulcerative colitis risk (aHR 1.54); high adolescent fruit intake remained linked to lower Crohn’s risk (aHR 0.57). High childhood fish intake associated with Crohn’s aHR 0.65; high adolescent salty-snack intake showed elevated point estimates for IBD/UC (aHR ~1.32 / 1.51, some CIs crossing 1). Observational design cannot prove causation.

**Links:**

- [Gut — Childhood food groups and IBD risk](https://gut.bmj.com/content/early/2026/11/19/gutjnl-2026-339600)
- [medicalXpress — Fruit and fish linked to lower Crohn’s risk](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-childhood-diet-high-fruit-fish.html)

**Commentary:** Fruit, fish, and fewer chips remain sensible family-plate defaults; the opposing ulcerative-colitis signal for childhood fruit is a reminder not to market any single food as universal IBD prevention.

### 3. Systematic review (conference): total diet replacement and intermittent fasting show the highest type 2 diabetes remission rates—weight loss is the main driver (Nutrition)
**Summary:** At the American College of Lifestyle Medicine meeting, Oriuwa et al. (University of Bridgeport) reported a 15-study systematic review (mostly RCTs; n≈25–4,503) of five lifestyle approaches versus usual care for type 2 diabetes remission (meeting definition: HbA1c <6.5% off diabetes drugs ≥3 months). At 12 months, intermittent fasting remission rates were ~36%–61% and total diet replacement ~44%; macronutrient-focused diets ~11%–17% and multicomponent diet-plus-exercise ~11%–23%; mHealth adjunct rates ranged 0%–39% without consistent between-group significance. Weight loss was described as the central driver; open-label designs and outcome heterogeneity limit certainty, and the report is conference-level pending full peer review.

**Links:**

- [Healio — Lifestyle medicine and diabetes remission](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20261123/study-lifestyle-medicine-treatments-tied-to-diabetes-remission)

**Commentary:** Treat “remission” as a weight-loss-plus-clinical-support project, not a diet-label swap; use meeting data as a lead until the full paper clarifies protocols and durability.

### 4. Multicenter RCT: starting oral nutrition at chemoradiotherapy onset cuts >5% weight loss in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Nutrition/Clinical)
**Summary:** An open-label *JAMA Oncology* RCT at six Chinese tertiary hospitals randomized newly diagnosed stage II–IVA nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients (PG-SGA 0–8) planning concurrent chemoradiotherapy to early oral nutritional supplementation (ONS) at treatment start versus routine ONS after predefined nutritional decline. In the modified intention-to-treat set (n=216; mean age ~46.6; ~77.8% male), >5% weight loss occurred in 65.4% (early) vs 80.7% (routine) (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.96); grade ≥3 radiation oral mucositis was 14.0% vs 26.6% (RR 0.53). Short-term cost-utility looked potentially favorable but was exploratory; open-label design and exclusion of adults >70 limit generalization.

**Links:**

- [JAMA Oncology — Early vs routine ONS in NPC CCRT](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaoncology/article-abstract/2856379)
- [ASCO Post — Earlier supplementation and weight loss](https://www.ascopost.com/articles/2026/11-november/nasopharyngeal-carcinoma-initiating-nutritional-supplementation-earlier-may-reduce-chemoradiotherapy-related-weight-loss/)

**Commentary:** Ask the oncology/nutrition team about proactive ONS before weight falls—not after; products and doses belong in a clinical plan, not a DIY supplement stack.

### 5. RCT + cohort: peanut intake shifts a blood lipid signature tied to ~11% lower T2D risk per SD—much of it may run through BMI (Evidence check)
**Summary:** *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* integrated the ARISTOTLE trial (63 healthy adults aged 18–33; 6 months of ~25 g/d skin-roasted peanuts, 32 g/d peanut butter, or control) with EPIC-Potsdam case-cohort samples (774 T2D; 547 CVD). Peanut exposure altered 51 within-class fatty-acid sums, especially VLCSFA-containing sphingolipids, used to build a peanut lipidomic signature (PeLS). Each 1-SD higher PeLS associated with T2D HR 0.89 (95% CI 0.80–0.99) but not CVD; exploratory mediation suggested ~89% of the diabetes link may be explainable by BMI. Authors do not claim peanuts prevent diabetes.

**Links:**

- [AJCN — Peanut-derived lipids and T2D risk](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101527)
- [Medical News Today — Peanut-related blood fats and diabetes risk](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/peanut-related-blood-fats-linked-to-lower-type-2-diabetes-risk)

**Commentary:** Use peanuts to replace chips or candy, not as a diabetes drug—and watch portions because energy density is high.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 6. Nutrients systematic review: energy restriction/fasting makes track athletes lighter—not consistently faster (Training/Nutrition)
**Summary:** University of Portsmouth and University of Ljubljana researchers narratively reviewed prescribed energy restriction, controlled low energy availability (LEA), time-restricted eating, and Ramadan fasting during continued athletics training (*Nutrients*; searches to 2026-09-30; unregistered). About 16 studies and ~234 athletes (~14.5% women) were included. Strategies often reduced body/fat mass, yet none showed a clear performance advantage versus available comparators; the only controlled Ramadan race retest found worse 5,000 m performance during fasting without necessarily large weight or reported-intake changes. Some studies reported adverse shifts in bone-formation markers, hormones, and perceived recovery; certainty was mostly low to very low.

**Links:**

- [Nutrients — Does lighter mean faster?](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/23/3904)
- [News-Medical — Dieting makes athletes lighter, not necessarily faster](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261123/Dieting-makes-athletes-lighter-but-not-necessarily-faster.aspx)

**Commentary:** Don’t equate “lighter on the scale” with a personal best—protect carbohydrate and protein availability, and separate results measured during restriction from those after refueling.

### 7. Meta-analysis: skeletal-muscle DNA methylation sites mark training status with ~0.95 AUC across 12 probes (Training)
**Summary:** Published Nov 23, 2026 in *npj Exercise Medicine and Health*, this meta-analysis pooled 123 skeletal-muscle samples from eight 450K/EPIC-array studies. Methylation variability was driven mainly by array type, probe reliability, data source, and sex; after accounting for sex, training status shaped methylation patterns and epigenetic age. Twelve highly reliable sites predicted training status in both sexes with AUC ≈0.95. This is a molecular-biomarker result, not a consumer at-home fitness test.

**Links:**

- [npj Exercise Medicine and Health — DNA methylation markers of training status](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44437-026-00005-2)

**Commentary:** The practical takeaway is that consistent training leaves measurable molecular traces—keep showing up for resistance and aerobic work rather than waiting on commercial methylation kits.

### 8. Systematic review/meta-analysis: Nordic eccentric work may cut soccer hamstring injuries ~70%; plyometrics better for speed/power (Training/Injury prevention)
**Summary:** *Scientific Reports* compared eccentric (Nordic hamstring–type) versus plyometric programs for soccer-relevant injury and performance outcomes. Eccentric training pooled to roughly a 70% lower hamstring-injury incidence (RR ≈0.29 across four trials), whereas plyometric bounding alone did not; for performance, plyometric and combined programs favored sprint/jump gains, while eccentric work increased eccentric knee-flexor strength. Authors frame the modalities as complementary: eccentric for protection/strength, plyometric for speed/power.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — Eccentric vs plyometric for hamstring health](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-79351-y)

**Commentary:** Field-sport athletes can add progressive Nordic/eccentric work 1–2× weekly plus a little plyometric volume—expect early DOMS and progress landing mechanics carefully.

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 9. College social-network RCT reanalysis: encouraging voluntary 3-day stay-home for flu-like illness may cut respiratory spread (Public health)
**Summary:** Columbia Mailman School of Public Health and collaborators applied a new causal statistical test to eX-FLU trial data, reported via institutional news on Nov 23, 2026 (*PNAS*). Over 10 flu-season weeks, 579 students were followed; half were encouraged (not required) to stay home three days with flu-like symptoms; 93 developed illness and logged face-to-face contacts. Despite imperfect adherence and recall error, the encouragement arm still appeared to reduce transmission. Authors argue voluntary isolation plus simple supports (e.g., meal delivery) is a low-cost, rapidly deployable tool.

**Links:**

- [Columbia Mailman — Encouraging students to stay home when sick](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/news/encouraging-students-stay-home-when-sick-may-reduce-spread-respiratory-illness)

**Commentary:** Skipping class or the office when febrile is both self-care and community care—paid sick leave and basic logistics make follow-through far more realistic.

### 10. Population + mouse data: long-term PM2.5 associates with higher dementia risk and reprograms microglia (Public health)
**Summary:** *Journal of Neuroinflammation* combined ~475,325 eligible UK Biobank participants with mouse experiments: long-term PM2.5 and Alzheimer polygenic risk each independently associated with higher Alzheimer disease and related dementia risk. Institutional coverage reported ~25% higher dementia odds comparing the most- versus least-polluted tertiles after adjusting for age, smoking, diabetes, and other factors. Mice exposed ~20 weeks to concentrated Cleveland ambient PM2.5 showed blood–brain-barrier disruption, impaired hippocampal neurogenesis, cognitive deficits, and microglial transcriptomic/chromatin remodeling; amyloid burden rose further in 5xFAD mice. Human findings remain observational; animal exposures mimic high-pollution settings.

**Links:**

- [Journal of Neuroinflammation — PM2.5 and microglial programs](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12974-026-04118-1)
- [University Hospitals — Air pollution reprograms brain immune cells](https://news.uhhospitals.org/news-releases/articles/2026/11/air-pollution-reprograms-brains-immune-cells-harms-healthy-brains)

**Commentary:** Put air quality on the brain-health checklist—on high-PM days, ease outdoor hard efforts, use filtration/masks, and keep blood pressure, glucose, and sleep in the same long game.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 11. NHANES cross-section: healthier lifestyle scores track with better sleep; depression may mediate ~one-third to nearly half of the link (Sleep)
**Summary:** *Sleep Science and Practice* analyzed 5,532 NHANES 2013–2020 adults (≥20 years) using a 0–6 healthy-lifestyle score (diet, smoking, alcohol, BMI, activity, sedentary time), PHQ-9 depression, sleep duration, and trouble sleeping. Each +1 lifestyle point associated with ~0.066 more hours of sleep and an odds ratio ~0.786 for trouble sleeping; the optimal (5–6) vs poor (0–2) group slept ~0.258 hours longer. Exploratory mediation attributed ~34.23% of the lifestyle–duration association and ~46.01% of the lifestyle–trouble-sleeping association to depressive symptoms. Cross-sectional design cannot establish causality.

**Links:**

- [Sleep Science and Practice — Lifestyle, depression and sleep](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41606-026-00204-8)
- [Scienmag — Healthy habits, sleep and depression mediation](https://scienmag.com/healthy-habits-correlate-with-better-sleep-while-depression-explains-key-associations/)

**Commentary:** Don’t treat insomnia as a sleep-hygiene-only problem—movement, tobacco/alcohol limits, less sitting, and mood care often move together; seek professional help if depression screening is positive rather than stacking sleep supplements first.
