## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Small RCT: 12–8 p.m. eating window in adults with Type 1 diabetes and obesity cut HbA1c ~0.5% without more serious complications (Nutrition)
**Summary:** A University of Illinois Chicago team led by Krista Varady reports in *Diabetes Care* (DOI: 10.2337/dc26-1093; ScienceDaily 2026-09-10) a 6-month, three-arm randomized trial in 32 adults with Type 1 diabetes and BMI 25–50 kg/m² assigned to time-restricted eating (food only 12:00–20:00, no calorie counting), ~25% daily calorie restriction, or no-change control. Weight change did not differ significantly across arms; TRE lowered HbA1c versus calorie restriction by about −0.46% (~0.5%), with little change in insulin needs or mean glucose, and no increase in diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypo-/hyperglycemia. This is a small first TRE trial in Type 1 diabetes; authors stress medical supervision.

**Links:**

- [ScienceDaily — Time-restricted eating and Type 1 diabetes](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260909231740.htm)
- [DOI: 10.2337/dc26-1093](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-1093)

**Commentary:** Treat this as a clinician-supervised meal-timing lead—not a green light for DIY intermittent fasting; any fasting window in Type 1 diabetes requires insulin plan changes and hypoglycemia preparedness.

### 2. 24-week RCT: CGM-guided personalized postprandial diet beat a standard Chinese diabetic diet on HbA1c, weight, and lipids (Nutrition)
**Summary:** *Food Science and Human Wellness* (DOI: 10.26599/FSHW.2026.9250961) randomized 175 Chinese adults with prediabetes or unmedicated type 2 diabetes to a continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)–guided personalized postprandial-targeting (PPT) diet versus a standardized Chinese Diabetic Diet (CDD). PPT dynamically adjusted macronutrients to individual post-meal glucose responses. Versus CDD, PPT achieved a between-group HbA1c difference of about −0.4%, with larger drops in fasting glucose, 2-hour OGTT glucose, and postprandial glucose exposure, ~6 kg more weight loss, and greater LDL-C, triglyceride, ALT, and GGT improvements. Open-label mid-size RCT; long-term sustainability and cost-effectiveness remain open.

**Links:**

- [SciOpen — CGM-guided personalized diet RCT](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.26599/FSHW.2026.9250961)
- [DOI: 10.26599/FSHW.2026.9250961](https://doi.org/10.26599/FSHW.2026.9250961)

**Commentary:** If you already use CGM, use the highest meal spikes to reshape carb quality and portion size—not only to memorize a standard menu; do not self-taper diabetes medicines.

### 3. Case–control: Higher Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) linked to lower nephrolithiasis risk in Chinese adults (Nutrition)
**Summary:** *Frontiers in Nutrition* (2026-09-04; DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2026.1893492), using the Shenyang sub-cohort of the TCLSIH study, matched 278 nephrolithiasis cases with 556 age- and sex-matched controls and scored DI-GM from a validated FFQ. Multivariable conditional logistic regression showed progressively lower odds with higher DI-GM (significant trend); DI-GM ≥8 versus 0–5 yielded OR≈0.17 (95% CI 0.08–0.36), consistent across age/sex strata with no significant nonlinearity. Observational case–control design cannot prove causation; prospective confirmation is needed.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Nutrition — DI-GM and nephrolithiasis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1893492/full)
- [DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2026.1893492](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1893492)

**Commentary:** Stone prevention still starts with fluids and limiting excess animal protein/salt; a more plant-fiber- and fermented-dairy-leaning plate can hitchhike—don’t replace water with a “microbiome index” pill.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. 12-week RCT: Explosive lower-body resistance training lowered sensorimotor lactate and improved objective sleep efficiency in older adults (Training/Sleep)
**Summary:** *GeroScience* (online 2026-09-09; DOI: 10.1007/s11357-026-02519-x) assigned 36 community-dwelling older adults (64–81 years) to explosive lower-body resistance training three times weekly or lifestyle-maintenance control. Outcomes included 7 T MRS sensorimotor lactate and NAA, wearable polysomnography, and subjective sleep scales. Versus control, training reduced resting lactate (d≈0.78) and improved objective sleep efficiency (d≈0.75) and total sleep time (d≈0.61). Sample size was modest (analyzable lactate n≈24), but findings suggest resistance training may jointly aid neural oxidative metabolism and sleep architecture.

**Links:**

- [GeroScience — Resistance training, lactate and sleep](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-026-02519-x)
- [DOI: 10.1007/s11357-026-02519-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-026-02519-x)

**Commentary:** Older adults with fragmented sleep should put supervised lower-body strength work on the calendar—not only sleep aids—with careful landing and joint load control.

### 5. ERP study: One bout of moderate aerobic exercise cut state anxiety in high trait-anxiety youth and improved Stroop inhibitory control in both groups (Training/Mental health)
**Summary:** *Frontiers in Public Health* (2026-09-09; DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1944238) split 42 young adults into low and high trait-anxiety groups (n=21 each), then tested state anxiety, color-word Stroop performance, and event-related potentials around a 30-minute moderate-intensity aerobic session. Exercise reduced state anxiety in the high trait-anxiety group only, shortened reaction times and error rates in both groups on congruent and incongruent trials, and was linked to less negative N400 and larger sustained positive (SP) amplitudes. Acute lab effect—not a long-term anxiety-disorder therapy claim.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers — Acute aerobic exercise and inhibitory control](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1944238/full)
- [DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1944238](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1944238)

**Commentary:** A moderate cardio bout before exams or high-stress days is a low-cost downshift; persistent anxiety still needs clinical evaluation rather than one-off runs alone.

### 6. 12-week four-arm RCT: Resistance training plus essential amino acids raised muscle mass and the follistatin/myostatin ratio in older women (Training/Nutrition)
**Summary:** *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (DOI: 10.1080/15502783.2026.2646626) randomized 96 healthy women ≥65 years without insulin resistance to control, resistance exercise (RE), essential amino acids (EAA 5.5 g twice daily), or RE+EAA. RE was moderate circuit training three times weekly for ~60 minutes. RE+EAA produced the largest muscle-mass gains and senior-fitness improvements; RE and RE+EAA lowered myostatin and raised follistatin, with the highest follistatin/myostatin ratio in the combined arm; IL-6 and IL-1β fell, while TNF-α declined significantly only with RE+EAA. Healthy older women sample—extrapolation to clinical sarcopenia needs care.

**Links:**

- [DOI: 10.1080/15502783.2026.2646626](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2646626)

**Commentary:** Strength training remains the main lever; EAA/high-quality protein is a booster—lock in 2–3 weekly lifting sessions and per-meal protein before buying powders.

### 7. ESSA position statement: Neuromuscular warm-ups can cut ACL risk ~60% in adolescent female ball sports, with a tiered prescription framework (Injury prevention)
**Summary:** *Sports Medicine* (DOI: 10.1007/s40279-026-02450-3) publishes an Exercise and Sport Science Australia (ESSA) position statement on ACL injury prevention, combining systematic searches of exercise interventions and intrinsic non-contact risk factors with video, lab, and biomechanical evidence. Tier 1 favors population-level neuromuscular/structured warm-ups; meta-analyses suggest ~60% lower ACL risk in adolescent female soccer, basketball, and handball when programs blend plyometrics, strength, balance/proprioception, mobility, and agility for 10–30 minutes, 2–3 times weekly. Tier 2 targets deficits such as low knee flexion and dynamic valgus. Evidence remains thinner for males, elites, and some high-risk sports; adherence is the implementation bottleneck.

**Links:**

- [Sports Medicine — ESSA ACL injury prevention position statement](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02450-3)
- [DOI: 10.1007/s40279-026-02450-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02450-3)

**Commentary:** Youth teams should hard-schedule neuromuscular warm-ups; adult recreational sides still benefit, but should not expect the same average risk cut as adolescent girls’ soccer.

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 8. WHO second-edition guidelines on reducing cognitive decline and dementia risk stress life-course lifestyles and comorbidity care (Guidelines)
**Summary:** On 2026-09-02 WHO released the second edition of its dementia risk-reduction guidelines (reference B09867), updating the 2019 edition for adults without dementia (including normal cognition and mild cognitive impairment). The framework covers healthy behaviours, management of related conditions, environmental exposures, and multidomain interventions, with equity and multisector integration as cross-cutting themes. Dementia-specific recommendations include a strong call for physical activity in adults with normal cognition (moderate-certainty evidence) and mostly conditional recommendations for activity and social engagement in MCI, alongside generic WHO guidance on tobacco, alcohol, diet, hearing, and sleep. Global policy/clinical framework—national delivery still depends on health-system capacity.

**Links:**

- [WHO — Dementia risk reduction guidelines executive summary, 2nd ed.](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09867)
- [WHO IRIS — Full guidelines, second edition](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/ea44b7f6-b09f-4e6f-b8c7-2e5b0fcc9a99/content)

**Commentary:** Dementia prevention is not a late-life “brain pill” project—treat movement, blood-pressure/glucose control, quitting smoking, hearing care, and sleep as a long-term brain-health package starting now.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 12. *Nature Neuroscience*: Frontal tau linked to “lonely,” nontraveling slow waves and poorer overnight episodic memory consolidation (Sleep)
**Summary:** UC Berkeley researchers (Omer Sharon and colleagues, directed by Matthew Walker; Berkeley News 2026-09-11) report in *Nature Neuroscience* (DOI: 10.1038/s41593-026-02415-9) that PET-measured frontal tau tracks impaired en masse unity and cortical traveling propagation of NREM slow waves—termed “lonely” waves—and poorer overnight episodic memory retention. In young adults, slow-wave clusters traveled roughly a handspan across the scalp; in older adults they traveled shorter distances and were more solitary. CSF pathology markers in an independent clinical cohort showed a convergent pattern; follow-up linked rising frontal tau with worsening slow-wave coordination and memory consolidation. Correlation does not yet settle causal direction; most participants were cognitively within normal aging ranges.

**Links:**

- [Berkeley News — Deep sleep, tau and memory](https://news.berkeley.edu/2026/09/11/new-uc-berkeley-study-links-deep-sleep-loss-to-alzheimers-related-protein-buildup/)
- [Nature Neuroscience — Lonely nontraveling slow waves](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02415-9)

**Commentary:** Protecting deep sleep—regular schedules, less alcohol, treating sleep apnea—is the actionable front end of this tau–memory story; unverified sleep gadgets are not Alzheimer therapies.

### 13. Clinical + mouse data: Lower gut-derived indole metabolites in depressed adolescents; tryptophan or indole supplementation eased depression-like behaviour (Mental health/Nutrition)
**Summary:** *Molecular Psychiatry* (online 2026-09-09; DOI: 10.1038/s41380-026-03895-8) found reduced *Bacteroidetes* abundance and lower key indole derivatives (IAA, IPA, IAld) in unmedicated depressed adolescents, with levels inversely related to symptom severity; chronic-restraint adolescent mice showed parallel declines. A high-tryptophan diet or direct indole supplementation improved depression-like behaviour, restored microglial morphology, and supported hippocampal neurogenesis via AhR signaling that rebalanced pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators. Includes animal extrapolation; human dosing and long-term safety are unsettled—do not self-megadose tryptophan for depression.

**Links:**

- [Molecular Psychiatry — Indole derivatives and adolescent depression](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-026-03895-8)
- [DOI: 10.1038/s41380-026-03895-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-026-03895-8)

**Commentary:** Adolescent depression still needs professional care first; diverse protein and fiber that support the tryptophan–microbiome axis beat unproven “gut–brain” pills.
