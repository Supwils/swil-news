## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. JHEP Reports RCT: Time-restricted eating adds no extra liver-fat benefit over Mediterranean-diet education; ≥5% weight loss matters more (nutrition/metabolism)
**Summary:** A multicenter randomized trial led by the University of Granada and the Public University of Navarra enrolled about 197 adults aged 30–60 with overweight or obesity, comparing early, late, or self-selected 8-hour time-restricted eating (TRE) plus Mediterranean diet–based usual care versus usual care alone for 12 weeks, with MRI-quantified hepatic fat. Results appeared in *JHEP Reports*, with a university briefing on November 11, 2026. Hepatic fat fell within each TRE arm, but none outperformed usual care; timing of the eating window also did not differ. Liver enzymes, stiffness, and gut microbiota showed no between-group advantage. People who lost ≥5% body weight had larger hepatic-fat reductions; about 41%–44% of TRE participants hit that threshold versus about 16% with usual care. Calories were not prescribed; secondary endpoints were underpowered. The message is not that TRE “does not work,” but that sustainable weight loss beats fretting over breakfast versus dinner windows.

**Links:**

- [JHEP Reports — TRE, liver fat, and fecal microbiota RCT](https://www.jhep-reports.eu/article/S2589-5559(26)00227-2/fulltext)
- [Universidad de Granada — November 11 briefing](https://www.ugr.es/en/about/news/intermittent-fasting-vs-mediterranean-diet-liver-fat)

**Commentary:** For fatty-liver goals, pick an eating pattern you can sustain that actually produces weight loss—do not treat early versus late eating windows as more important than energy balance and diet quality.

### 2. Nature: A gut–brain dopamine pathway drives flavour–nutrient learning; preferences can be reshaped (nutrition/mechanism)
**Summary:** Zachary Knight’s team at UCSF published online in *Nature* on November 4, 2026, with major Chinese science coverage on November 11. In mice, post-ingestive nutrients triggered dopamine release from ventral tegmental neurons into the anterior basolateral amygdala (BLA), preferentially on the left; blocking the pathway preserved normal eating but abolished learning that a novel flavour predicted calories, while stimulating BLA dopamine terminals conditioned preference for a calorie-free flavour. Human PET/fMRI reanalyses likewise showed left-BLA bias after nutrients reached the gut. The work is largely mechanistic with supportive human imaging—not a near-term clinical intervention—and did not test whether GLP-1 drugs act on this circuit.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Neural basis of flavour–nutrient learning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11229-5)
- [Chinese Academy of Sciences — November 11 Chinese report](https://www.cas.cn/kj/202611/t20261111_5124004.shtml)

**Commentary:** For whole grains and bitter vegetables that taste “bad” at first, repeated small exposures fit this learning biology better than overnight willpower overhauls—do not expect preferences to flip in a day.

### 3. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition: Peanut-related plasma lipid signature linked to modestly lower type 2 diabetes risk, largely via BMI (nutrition)
**Summary:** Domínguez-López and colleagues integrated the ARISTOTLE randomized trial with the EPIC-Potsdam case-cohort: 63 healthy young adults were assigned for 6 months to 25 g/day skin-roasted peanuts, 32 g/day peanut butter, or peanut-oil control butter; the cohort contributed 774 incident type 2 diabetes and 547 CVD cases. Peanut-related exposures altered 51 within-class fatty-acid sums, mainly very-long-chain saturated fatty acids in sphingolipids, yielding a peanut lipidomic signature (PeLS). Each 1-SD rise in PeLS associated with lower T2D risk (HR ≈0.89; 95% CI 0.80–0.99) but not CVD; adding BMI attenuated most of the link (about 89% explainable). Mediation was labeled hypothesis-generating; ARISTOTLE lacked a peanut-free arm. Sci.News and others covered the work around November 11, 2026.

**Links:**

- [American Journal of Clinical Nutrition — Peanut-derived plasma lipids and T2D](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101527)
- [Sci.News — November 11 report](https://www.sci.news/medicine/peanut-consumption-lower-diabetes-risk-15190.html)

**Commentary:** If you are not allergic, a small handful of peanuts can fit snacks or salads—but treat them as part of overall diet and weight management, not a diabetes-prevention pill.

### 4. The Innovation Nutrition review: Precision nutrition prioritizes macronutrient quality and sources over calorie cuts alone (nutrition/evidence)
**Summary:** Huang, Wu and colleagues at Shanghai Jiao Tong University reviewed more than 30 large cohorts and meta-analyses in *The Innovation Nutrition*, covered by News-Medical on November 11, 2026. They argue extreme energy restriction is hard to sustain and carries reproductive, bone, and cardiac risks; carbohydrate at roughly 50%–55% of energy associates with lower all-cause mortality when whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and fruit displace refined carbs and sugary drinks; midlife animal protein tracks with higher mortality while plant protein looks favorable, yet after age 65 modestly higher-quality protein may help against sarcopenia; fat type beats fat quantity; about 25–29 g/day fiber shows relatively stable links to lower mortality. Age-band ratio frameworks are research scaffolding, not validated prescriptions; inter-individual postprandial responses can differ ~10-fold, and long-term healthspan gains still need multi-year RCTs.

**Links:**

- [News-Medical — November 11 review coverage](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261111/Precision-nutrition-shifts-the-focus-from-calorie-cuts-to-quality.aspx)
- [The Innovation Nutrition — article DOI](https://www.the-innovation.org/article/doi/10.59717/j.xinn-nutri.2026.100034)

**Commentary:** Day-to-day practice still means better carb sources, swapping some red/processed meat for plant proteins, favoring unsaturated fats, and hitting fiber—not calorie slogans or a one-off “personalized menu” as an anti-aging plan.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 5. Nature Medicine T-REx: Adding resistance training to tirzepatide spares lean tissue and raises strength (training/chronic disease)
**Summary:** Danny Green and colleagues at the University of Western Australia randomized 120 adults aged 50–70 with obesity or overweight plus a weight-related condition (without type 2 diabetes) on tirzepatide to thrice-weekly resistance training or usual activity for 40 weeks in the Tirzepatide and Resistance Exercise (T-REx) trial; results appeared in *Nature Medicine*, with UWA coverage in November 2026. Total weight loss was similar (~−22.3 vs −22.6 kg) and fat loss comparable; resistance training cut lean soft-tissue loss (~−3.57 vs −4.67 kg; between-group difference ≈1.09 kg) and improved upper- and lower-body strength by about 10%, while the non-training arm lost about 10% strength. Exercise adherence was about 91%; the manufacturer supplied drug but did not design or analyze the study. “Drug only” can shrink the scale while eroding function.

**Links:**

- [Nature Medicine — Resistance exercise attenuates lean-mass loss on tirzepatide](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04752-5)
- [University of Western Australia — study briefing](https://www.uwa.edu.au/news/article/2026/november/resistance-training-key-to-maintaining-muscle-function-during-glp1-weight-loss)

**Commentary:** If you use a GLP-1 or dual agonist for weight loss, write 2–3 weekly resistance sessions into the plan—preserve strength and function, not just the number on the scale.

### 6. Hot versus cold recovery: Ice baths ease perceived soreness but do not speed strength recovery (training/recovery)
**Summary:** A November 11, 2026 *The Conversation* article describes a comparison of post-exercise recovery in 40 healthy, active adults randomly assigned after hard exercise to 10°C cold-water immersion, 42°C hot-water immersion, 60°C hot air (sauna-like), or passive sitting for about 30 minutes. No method restored muscle strength significantly faster than the others; cold water did not improve measured recovery markers and showed the slowest strength return at 72 hours (~82% of baseline versus ~88%–89% in other groups), yet it better reduced perceived muscle soreness. With only about 10 people per arm, sex differences could not be robustly tested—“feeling recovered” is not the same as physiological recovery.

**Links:**

- [The Conversation — Hot vs cold after exercise (November 11)](https://theconversation.com/hot-or-cold-our-research-explores-what-works-best-after-exercise-295514)

**Commentary:** Choose an ice bath if you want to feel less sore, but do not expect it to rebuild strength faster than heat or rest—sleep, nutrition, and training load remain the core.

### 7. PLOS One RCT: Acute 3 and 6 mg/kg caffeine improve sprint outcomes in male 100-m athletes, with outcome-specific dose effects (training/supplement)
**Summary:** Mei and colleagues ran a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled three-condition crossover in 21 male 100-m sprinters comparing placebo, 3 mg/kg, and 6 mg/kg caffeine 60 minutes before 60-m sprints, jumps, and a 10-s Wingate; published November 10, 2026 in *PLOS One*. Versus placebo, 3 mg/kg shortened cumulative 10/30/60-m times by about 0.056/0.083/0.123 s, and 6 mg/kg by about 0.075/0.115/0.175 s; 6 mg/kg added further benefit versus 3 mg/kg mainly at 30 and 60 m. Both doses improved reaction time, RPE, jump height, and Wingate power measures; stride length/frequency were unchanged. Results apply to trained male sprinters—not casual gym-goers—and sleep/cardiovascular tolerance matter.

**Links:**

- [PLOS One — 3 and 6 mg/kg caffeine in male 100-m sprinters](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0361864)

**Commentary:** If you race and tolerate caffeine, start near 3 mg/kg and track sleep and palpitations—do not assume doubling the dose doubles the payoff.

## III. Public Health, Sleep & Evidence Clarification

### 8. JAMA ATTAIN-OSA: Oral orforglipron over 52 weeks lowers AHI in obesity/overweight with moderate-to-severe OSA (sleep/pharmacology)
**Summary:** Two 52-week phase 3 double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs enrolled about 712 adults with BMI ≥27 and moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea—one without and one with positive airway pressure use—randomized to once-daily oral GLP-1 receptor agonist orforglipron (maximum tolerated dose) or placebo. Results appeared online in *JAMA* on November 9, 2026, with Chinese media follow-up on November 11. Mean AHI change was about −17.8 versus −4.5 events/h without PAP (difference ≈−13.2) and −17.3 versus −5.3 with PAP (difference ≈−12.0). Weight fell on the order of ~10%; several secondary endpoints (hypoxic burden, inflammation markers) favored the drug; gastrointestinal events were more common (~70% on drug). Diabetes and mild OSA were excluded; hard long-term cardiovascular outcomes remain open. This is prescription-drug evidence, not a supplement claim.

**Links:**

- [JAMA — ATTAIN-OSA: orforglipron for OSA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2856406)
- [American Heart Association — news release](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/daily-oral-glp-1-medication-reduced-episodes-of-sleep-apnea-in-adults-with-excess-weight)

**Commentary:** For obesity with moderate-to-severe apnea, weight-loss medication may be a discussable adjunct to PAP—but it needs sleep-medicine oversight, not DIY “get thin and quit the machine” shopping.

### 9. EFSA: Updated Alternaria mycotoxin risk assessment flags five genotoxic toxins; multiple foods drive exposure (food safety)
**Summary:** On November 11, 2026, the European Food Safety Authority updated its assessment of eight Alternaria toxins in food and feed. Five (including AOH, AME, ATX-I/II, and STTX-III) were judged genotoxic. Tenuazonic acid (TeA) was the most frequently detected toxin in foods such as mulberries, paprika powder, and tomatoes; data were insufficient to conclude genotoxicity, while a conservative non-genotoxic assessment found a low probability of adverse effects at current dietary exposure (stated with about 98% certainty). Adverse effects from altenuene and tentoxin were considered unlikely at current exposures. Major contributors included cereal foods, fruit products/juices, tomatoes, vegetables, and plant oils. This is a risk-assessment update, not a brand recall.

**Links:**

- [EFSA — Alternaria toxins risk assessment update (November 11)](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/alternaria-toxins-food-and-feed-efsa-updates-its-risk-assessment)

**Commentary:** For individuals, dietary variety and avoiding chronically moldy grains or one spice eaten in huge amounts beat panicking over a single vegetable; regulators and industry still do the heavy lifting on contamination.

### 10. JAMA Network Open RCT: Urolithin A does not improve peripheral endothelial function in midlife obesity; task-evoked brain blood-flow rises without cognitive gains (evidence clarification)
**Summary:** A University of Oklahoma Health Campus double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT randomized 50 adults aged 40–64 with BMI ≥30 to 1,000 mg/day urolithin A or placebo for 4 weeks; published in *JAMA Network Open*, with a campus news push on November 11, 2026. Change in brachial flow-mediated dilation did not differ by group, nor did other peripheral endothelial measures. Task-evoked cerebrovascular hemodynamic responses rose significantly with urolithin A versus placebo (notably left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex), but memory/attention task performance did not improve. Single-site, small, short, and mostly female—insufficient to recommend the supplement for “brain” or “vessel” protection.

**Links:**

- [JAMA Network Open — Urolithin A and endothelial/cerebrovascular function](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2855121)
- [University of Oklahoma Health — November 11 briefing](https://www.ouhsc.edu/news/details/clinical-trial-explores-potential-brain-health-benefits-of-dietary-supplement)

**Commentary:** When headlines say a supplement “boosts brain blood flow,” check the fine print: hemodynamic signal ≠ cognitive benefit, and it is not a buying cue—evidence remains exploratory.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 11. PLOS Mental Health: Frequent sleep starts (hypnic jerks) link to poorer sleep quality, insomnia severity, and anxiety (sleep/mental health)
**Summary:** Grünberger, Schabus and colleagues surveyed 194 adults in Austria and Germany online—96 with weekly abrupt awakenings plus other symptoms and 98 controls—with coverage dated November 11, 2026 pointing to *PLOS Mental Health*. The sleep-starts group reported worse sleep quality, greater insomnia severity, more dysfunctional sleep beliefs, and higher anxiety and depressive symptoms; start frequency tracked with sleep quality, sleep beliefs, and anxiety more than depression. Participants linked starts more to psychological stress than to sleep deprivation alone. Items were researcher-developed without objective sleep recording, so causality cannot be inferred; validated instruments and polysomnography are needed.

**Links:**

- [Medical Xpress — November 11 report](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-jolting-poorer-higher-anxiety-survey.html)

**Commentary:** If you often jolt awake at sleep onset with panic, prioritize stress and catastrophic sleep beliefs—not only “catch up on hours”—and seek sleep/mental-health assessment if it persists.

### 12. HKU Nature Mental Health: 35 everyday predictors of psychological resilience from 26,000 residents (mental health/prevention)
**Summary:** Michael Ni and colleagues at HKUMed used the FAMILY Cohort and an environment-wide association approach to screen 208 candidates in 26,210 Hong Kong residents aged 15+, identifying 35 validated predictors of psychological resilience after multiplicity control and internal validation; published in *Nature Mental Health*, with an HKU release on November 11, 2026. Domains spanned mental/physical health, life satisfaction, meaning (e.g., religious activity), character/virtues (e.g., volunteering), close relationships, financial stability, and health behaviors (e.g., meeting WHO activity levels). Interactive weekend family activities (exercise, meals, conversation) associated with higher resilience; passive family time did not. Observational design cannot prove causality; associations varied by age and sex.

**Links:**

- [HKUMed — November 11 press release](https://www.med.hku.hk/en/news/press/20261111-hkumed-reveals-keys-to-psychological-resilience-in-a-study-of-26000-hong-kong-residents)

**Commentary:** Resilience is not pure grit: schedule interactive social time, physical activity, and volunteering into the week—closer to the evidence than stockpiling motivational quotes.

### 13. Bristol/Exeter: At least weekly park visits linked to better children’s social-emotional development (lifestyle/pediatrics)
**Summary:** Using Children of the 90s (ALSPAC) data, researchers examined park/playground visit frequency from birth to age 11 and social-emotional development in 4,219 children; results in *Epidemiology and Community Health* were announced by the University of Bristol on November 11, 2026. After adjusting for socioeconomic status and family adversity, children taken to parks at least weekly across that period scored about 16.5% better on mental-health–related assessments than those visiting less than weekly; periods of more frequent visits also tracked with better social-emotional scores. Observational design; park access and quality may still reflect structural inequities.

**Links:**

- [University of Bristol — November 11 press release](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2026/november/going-to-the-park-at-least-once-a-week-can-improve-childrens-mental-health.html)

**Commentary:** For families, treat “at least one outdoor public-space visit a week” as a low-cost mental-health habit; policy still needs safe, welcoming parks—not slogans alone.
