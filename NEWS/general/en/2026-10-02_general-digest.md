### 2. The G7 agrees to release 100 million barrels over four months and not to restrict energy exports (Energy)
**Summary:** After a virtual meeting chaired by French President Emmanuel Macron on Friday, the G7 said it would release up to 100 million barrels of strategic oil reserves over the next four months, coordinated through the International Energy Agency, including a front-loaded substantial diesel release within the first 20 days. The group also said members would not restrict exports of energy and petroleum products to one another. Trump wrote that Europe had "just agreed" to release a massive amount of diesel stocks immediately. He had threatened to ban US diesel exports if Europe did not tap its reserves; the EU said earlier Friday that it fully rejected that threat. POLITICO reported that US diesel accounts for more than half of Europe's diesel imports. Macron denied that the discussion was conducted as a threat and said Trump had been clear there would be no export ban. Al Jazeera, citing AAA, said the US national average for diesel was $6.37 a gallon on Friday, after a record $6.52 on September 22, and that US diesel inventories hit a record low of 107.9 million barrels as of September 11. The G7 statement framed the 100 million barrels as implementation of March commitments after counting what has already been fulfilled. POLITICO said some diplomats viewed the step as a reaffirmation of the March IEA-led release rather than an entirely new volume. In March, 32 IEA members agreed to unlock 400 million barrels, which officials say has not been fully completed.

## III. Europe

### 7. Eurozone inflation rises to 3.8 percent, and an October ECB hike is not fully priced (Central banks)
**Summary:** Inflation in the 21 countries using the euro jumped to 3.8 percent in September from 3.2 percent in August, above the 3.6 percent expected in a Reuters poll, Eurostat said Friday, Reuters reported. Fuel, natural gas, and to a lesser extent food drove the rise. Core inflation, excluding food and fuel, rose to 2.5 percent from 2.4 percent as services prices picked up. The ECB's deposit rate is 2.5 percent after two hikes this summer. Jack Allen-Reynolds of Capital Economics said the data do not change the view that the next hike is most likely in December, though an October move would not be a big surprise if energy prices rose further in the coming weeks. Investors see up to three more hikes over the coming year, treat an October move as negligible, and do not fully price the next increase until January. Subsidies so far total about 0.1 percent of the bloc's GDP. The spread of French debt over similar German bonds has risen to multi-decade highs, and Reuters said the next rate decision may turn on financial stability as much as on inflation.

### 10. Greer says US-China trade will be "managed," with no date for $60 billion in tariff cuts (Trade)
**Summary:** US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said after the G20 trade ministers' meeting in Milwaukee on Thursday that Washington wants a "managed trade relationship" with China rather than a fundamental change in Beijing's economic system, IANS reported on October 2. He said the US goods deficit with China was $300 billion at the start of Trump's second term and is now on track for about $140 billion annualised, a drop of more than half. Under the US-China Board of Trade, each side has selected about $30 billion of non-sensitive goods that the two governments do not want used as leverage in future trade measures. He said that basket covers about 30 percent of US exports to China and about 10 percent of Chinese exports to the United States. Asked for a timeline on tariff cuts, he answered "No," and said later steps must go through US legal procedures. The Edge reported the same day that there is no date for lower tariffs on the $60 billion of goods. US exports that could face lower Chinese tariffs include meat and dairy; Chinese goods under the arrangement would include small appliances, toys, and holiday decorations. Greer said the comment process and a legal determination reduce the chance that US consumers see lower rates before the holiday season.
