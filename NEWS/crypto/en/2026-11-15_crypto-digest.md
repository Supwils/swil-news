### 2. German Bundestag expands customs powers to seize high-value assets of doubtful origin, including crypto (Regulation)
**Summary:** Per Bundestag coverage and mid-November reports from Crypto Briefing and Coindaro—still circulating on November 15—the Bundestag on November 12 passed the Zollfinanzgerechtigkeitsgesetz strengthening customs tools against financial crime. Authorities may administratively secure assets—including crypto-assets as defined under MiCA—when there are factual grounds to doubt lawful origin and values typically exceed about €100,000, without first opening a criminal case. An initial freeze can last up to about 30 days, with one finance-court extension of up to about 150 days; permanent confiscation requires a finance-court decision. The law still needs Bundesrat approval and is planned to take effect January 1, 2027. Government revenue estimates from 2028 onward assume success rates that have not been independently verified.

**Commentary:** Europe is folding on-chain holdings into the customs AML toolkit—exchanges and cross-border rails will feel diligence and freeze risk before most retail holders do.

## V. Security & Exchanges

### 8. South Korea’s Coinone discloses customer-support contractor breach; passwords, IDs, and wallet addresses may be exposed (Security)
**Summary:** Coinone said in a November 15 notice that an attacker accessed servers of support contractor MetaM around 4 a.m. on November 12; Coinone was notified about 9:20 a.m. the same day. Potentially exposed fields include phone numbers, names, emails, dates of birth, bank account numbers, passwords, resident registration numbers, ID issuance dates, home addresses, wallet addresses, and TXIDs left during chat or phone support—actual items vary by customer. Coinone said its account, trading, and wallet systems are segregated and showed no evidence of direct intrusion as of the disclosure; it blocked attack IPs, shifted outsourced support to email, and reported to KISA, the Personal Information Protection Commission, and the Financial Supervisory Service. Users without support history were said to be unaffected; anyone who shared passwords during support was told to change them. Affected customers may seek damages or statutory damages of up to about 3 million won under Korean law.
