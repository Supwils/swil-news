## I. Regulation & Policy

### 1. South Korea’s FSC to issue no-action letter letting exchanges freeze deposits and withdrawals on suspected hacks (Regulation)
**Summary:** South Korea’s Financial Services Commission (FSC) said on November 12, after a virtual-asset industry security meeting, that it will issue a no-action letter allowing operators to block deposits and withdrawals when a hack or intrusion is only suspected—without waiting for a confirmed breach. Under the Virtual Asset User Protection Act, operators generally may not freeze flows without a justifiable reason and may restrict them only once a hacking incident has been clearly established; the industry had flagged legal ambiguity around early warning signs such as abnormal large transactions. The meeting also called for 24/7 monitoring and incident response, maximizing cold-wallet ratios, expanding insurance and reserves, and pushing more operators to join the Financial Security Institute; a forthcoming digital-asset law draft would ultimately raise security duties and liability toward bank-like standards.

### 2. Thailand’s SEC consults on 5 million baht (~$148,000) daily stablecoin deposit and withdrawal caps (Regulation)
**Summary:** Thailand’s Securities and Exchange Commission on November 12 opened consultation on draft rules for stablecoin flows through digital-asset operators, with comments due November 26. The draft would cap inbound and outbound stablecoin transfers separately at 5 million baht (about $148,000) per customer per day at each operator, require amounts to match income and financial standing, and allow transfers only to and from wallets verified as the customer’s own—banning third-party wallets—while applying Travel Rule checks and blockchain analytics. Transfers between a customer’s accounts at Thai operators would be exempt from the cap if both sides apply the Travel Rule; exemptions also cover operator self-accounts, Bank of Thailand–approved models, market makers and related cases. The package also tightens market-maker and liquidity-provider disclosure and sets a minimum of 3 million baht for certain off-platform trades.

### 8. MSCI confirms removing Strategy and other bitcoin treasury firms from indexes effective after the Nov. 30 close (Institutions)
**Summary:** In its November index review, MSCI confirmed it will delete Strategy (MSTR)—the largest publicly listed bitcoin holder—from the MSCI USA Index and related broad benchmarks, with Tokyo-listed Metaplanet also among deletions; changes take effect after the November 30 close. The move follows MSCI’s October “non-operating company” screen, which excludes issuers with fund-like characteristics from operating-company benchmarks. Exclusion does not force Strategy to sell its bitcoin (holdings reported above 850,000 BTC) but can trigger selling by passive funds that track the indexes; Strategy previously estimated about $2.8 billion of related passive selling pressure, while other banks have floated larger outflow scenarios if peers follow. Analysts say the bigger risk is pressure on Strategy’s mNAV premium and its ability to raise equity cheaply to keep buying bitcoin.

### 12. Binance CSO’s earlier warning on fake job interviews closely matches the entry vector in the ~$6.3 billion hack (Security)
**Summary:** Cointelegraph and others reported on November 12 that Binance chief security officer Jimmy Su had described in a July interview how fake job interviews could trick employees into running malicious coding assessments on work devices and harvesting credentials—steps that can look legitimate one-by-one and evade antivirus tools. Binance’s preliminary investigation found that in September a software engineer downloaded a malicious assignment onto a work computer during a staged interview, forming part of the multi-stage campaign behind the October theft of about $6.3 billion from hot wallets; attackers also leveraged an internal AI tool for lateral movement. Su had said Binance’s red team had run monthly phishing simulations—including fake recruiters—for years, and stressed that employees may seek outside roles but the firm must stop unsafe code execution on company devices.
