## I. Space and Deep Space

### 1. Expedition 75 opens Dragon and starts the new experiments (Spaceflight)
**Summary:** NASA's station blog, updated at 1:15 p.m. EDT on October 16, said Expedition 75 had begun unloading the SpaceX CRS-35 cargo Dragon that docked on October 15 and was activating its new science. After NASA flight engineer Anil Menon finished leak checks and equalized pressure, he opened the hatch; Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney of NASA and Joshua Kutryk of the Canadian Space Agency followed him in and moved samples from portable freezers and incubators into station racks. The log says artificial-retina and heart-tissue work will begin soon, while investigations starting Friday and over the weekend include protein crystals aimed at more precise cancer treatments and the genetic response of plants in microgravity for spacecraft life support. A pair of roll-out solar arrays remains in the unpressurized trunk; ground teams will use Canadarm2 to stow them on truss logistics platforms, and Menon and Kutryk are scheduled to finish installation on spacewalks on October 29 and November 5. The crew also wrapped up a week-long Teledroid exoskeleton study of remote robot control.

### 2. MMX enters the launch window for a Phobos sample return (Deep space)
**Summary:** On October 16, New Scientist reported that JAXA's Martian Moons eXploration mission is set to launch from Tanegashima on October 20 to bring back material from Phobos and test whether Mars's two moons are captured asteroids or debris from an impact on Mars. ESA's October 13 mission note gives liftoff as 04:41 JST on October 20 (21:41 CEST on October 19), with NASA, CNES, DLR, and ESA as partners. If the plan holds, the spacecraft takes about a year to arrive, enters Mars orbit in August 2027, and orbits Phobos from September 2027; New Scientist expects the return to Earth in 2031. The landings will release the small rover IDEFIX and collect surface material with a gas puff and a hollow coring cylinder. ESA says the sample capsule is planned to land in Australia before transfer to Japan.

**Links:**

- [New Scientist — Japan is launching a mission to Mars’s largest moon Phobos](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2594332-japan-is-launching-a-mission-to-marss-largest-moon-phobos/)

### 3. Hubble frames NGC 1827 edge-on as a satellite-galaxy survey passes halfway (Astronomy)
**Summary:** On October 16, NASA released a Hubble Advanced Camera for Surveys view of the edge-on intermediate spiral NGC 1827, about 37 million light-years away in Columba. The frame is part of a survey of 260 satellite galaxies around 22 Milky Way-like hosts within about 32 million light-years of Earth. Hubble has observed 111 of them; each of the rest needs one more orbit, 149 orbits in all. Bright blue regions and dark dust lanes along the disk mark likely sites of star formation. NASA says low-mass galaxies of this kind test cosmological models on small scales, where those models still struggle to match observations.

**Links:**

- [NASA — Hubble spies spiral’s edge](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasas-hubble-spies-spirals-edge/)

**Commentary:** The edge-on frame is one exposure in a census; whether small-scale satellite galaxies match cosmological models waits on the remaining 149 orbits.

## II. Biomedicine and Life Science

### 4. Brain organoids turn Braille letters into decodable neural signals (Biohybrid computing)
**Summary:** The University of Bristol said on October 16 that a paper published the same day in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence shows human forebrain organoids on microelectrode arrays can take electrical patterns from a tactile sensor and return neural activity that identifies Braille letters. Each organoid is less than a millimeter across and contains about 10,000 neurons plus support cells. The team recorded touch for all 26 Braille letters, converted those records into stimulation, and decoded the responses with a support vector machine. Accuracy was about 61 percent for one organoid and about 83 percent when three were combined, and the trio held up better than direct decoding once artificial noise was added. First author Tianyi Liu and senior author Benjamin Ward-Cherrier said the experiment shows open-loop encoding, transmission, and decoding, not learning over time.

**Links:**

- [University of Bristol — Mini brain organoids reading Braille](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2026/october/mini-brain-organoids-reading-braille-marks-step-towards-biohybrid-computin.html)
- [Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence — Encoding tactile stimuli for Braille recognition with organoids](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2026.1800219)

**Commentary:** Three organoids lift Braille decoding from about 61 percent to about 83 percent, and the authors leave learning itself for a later closed-loop test.

### 5. Brain phospholipase PLA2G2F tracks cognitive resilience in aging (Neuroscience)
**Summary:** A Nature news story on October 16 covered a Nature Neuroscience paper from October 15 that links the phospholipase gene Pla2g2f to inflammation, neuronal connectivity, and spatial memory in the aging brain. Amar Sahay's team at Massachusetts General Hospital found that mice lacking the gene developed more inflammation and neuronal degradation with age, while mice with extra Pla2g2f had less inflammation and better-connected neurons. Young knockout mice scored lower than controls on spatial-memory tests, and older mice with boosted Pla2g2f scored higher. Human data sets show PLA2G2F activity in the brain rising with age. In donor brains from people who had been cognitively tested, activity was highest in resilient individuals whose cognition held despite amyloid-beta deposits. Sahay described the enzyme as a compensatory resilience mechanism.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Memory stays strong in old age when this brain enzyme is active](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03303-9)
- [Nature Neuroscience — Vicidomini et al.](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02465-z)

**Commentary:** Raising or removing the gene shifts inflammation and memory in mice; the human result is still higher activity in resilient brains, not a validated drug target.

### 6. A six-species mitochondrial atlas flags parasite-only protein families (Cell biology)
**Summary:** Nature's October 16 news report describes a mitoproteome catalogue led by Vamsi Mootha at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute. Nine papers published on October 1 in Cell, Molecular Cell, Current Biology, and related journals cover five single-celled parasites and one plant. In Giardia lamblia, a cause of diarrheal disease, the team identified 59 mitochondrial proteins, 27 of them previously unknown and 34 of unknown function; the news story cites an estimate of about 280 million infections a year. Combined with known human and yeast mitochondrial proteins, the six organisms yield 8,619 proteins in 3,199 families. About 43 percent of those families are absent from Pfam, and 33 families are conserved in eukaryotic pathogens and missing in humans. Mootha called the pathogen-only proteins possible drug targets that would spare human proteins. The same catalogues reopen the debate over the mitochondrion's endosymbiotic origin about two billion years ago.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Unprecedented atlas explores mitochondria across the tree of life](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03195-9)
- [Current Biology — Giardia mitoproteome](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.07.074)

**Commentary:** The 33 pathogen-only families are a target list, and the share of unknown functions means the atlas has enlarged uncharted metabolism before it has delivered a compound.

## III. Climate and Environment

### 7. Mid-Holocene biomarkers pull future Arctic winter sea ice lower (Climate)
**Summary:** Nature Geoscience on October 16 published work by Crystal Fu and M. B. Osman of the University of Cambridge with Marco Aquino-López of CIMAT in Mexico. They compiled what they describe as the largest Arctic database of mid-Holocene sea-ice biomarkers, about 5,000 to 7,000 years old, with 46 observations, and used Bayesian weighting to score 27 climate models against that warm interval. The unweighted ensemble over-predicted winter ice. Weighting removed the positive bias in historical simulations and cut projection uncertainty by as much as 85 percent, down to the scale of interannual variability. Applied to future scenarios, the same weights put winter sea-ice extent nearly 1 million square kilometers below earlier estimates across coming decades and emissions pathways. Cambridge's release also says the revised weights imply that snowfall over southeast Greenland and rainfall in the Mediterranean may be underestimated.

**Links:**

- [Nature Geoscience — Palaeoclimate constraints narrow uncertainty in future Arctic winter sea ice projections](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02123-w)
- [University of Cambridge — Winter sea ice loss may be underestimated](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/winter-sea-ice-loss-may-be-underestimated-according-to-ancient-clues-from-a-warmer-arctic)

**Commentary:** The constraint is sea ice from a warm interval 6,000 years ago, not a new satellite year; it tightens winter extent and uncertainty, while the first ice-free summer remains a separate question.

### 8. Temporary carbon storage still damps slow climate variables (Climate)
**Summary:** Nature Communications published an open paper on October 16 by Mitchell Dickau and H. Damon Matthews at Concordia University. Using carbon storage-years, the time-integrated amount of carbon held, they find that temporary removal produces avoided changes that stay proportional in 2100 to several inertial climate variables, across storage amounts and emissions scenarios: permafrost carbon, ocean carbon, the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, ocean temperature, and thermosteric sea-level rise. By 2300 the proportionality still holds for sea-level rise, ocean temperature, and ocean carbon. The authors conclude that the climate benefit of temporary storage outlasts the storage itself, with some of that benefit persisting for centuries, and that the benefit is largest when temporary storage is paired with ambitious emissions cuts.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Temporary carbon storage can mitigate slow-responding climate changes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78620-8)

**Commentary:** The simulations replace "if it does not last, it does nothing" with avoided change counted in storage-years, and the largest benefit still requires emissions to fall at the same time.

## IV. Energy, Materials, and Basic Research

### 9. A lactate ligand lets metallic zinc release hydrogen on demand (Energy)
**Summary:** Nature Synthesis on October 16 published work from Zhexuan Liu, Yifei Zhu, Guangmin Zhou, and colleagues at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. They use reversible zinc oxidation as an energy carrier for distributed, high-purity hydrogen. Machine-learning screening identified lactate, which outcompetes hydroxide for Zn(II) in alkaline media, doubling the soluble Zn(II) concentration to 1.12 molar and allowing hydrogen production without an external circuit. The system runs from 0 to 80 degrees Celsius at about 99 percent Faradaic efficiency, with a volumetric hydrogen capacity of 137.9 to 218.4 kilograms per cubic meter. The authors present that capacity as higher than many existing carriers and aim it at remote communities, mobile platforms, and off-grid sites rather than long pipelines.

**Links:**

- [Nature Synthesis — Reversible zinc oxidation as a route to on-demand hydrogen delivery](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44160-026-01190-6)

**Commentary:** The volumetric capacity and near-99 percent Faradaic efficiency show a zinc-lactate route that stores hydrogen in metal and releases it on site; cycle life and field safety are outside what this paper closes.

### 10. A nickel-cobalt cocatalyst steers glycerol photoreforming toward hydrogen (Energy)
**Summary:** On October 16, the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation described a photoreforming study led by Denny Gunawan and Rose Amal at the University of New South Wales, published in Applied Catalysis B: Environment and Energy. Biodiesel production yields about 100 kilograms of glycerol per tonne, and crude glycerol can be worth as little as about 0.11 US dollars per kilogram. Adding small amounts of nickel and cobalt to zinc indium sulfide raised the hydrogen evolution rate under visible light about tenfold versus the unmodified material and increased the share of pathways that make glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone. The paper's abstract is more specific on selectivity: Lewis acid sites on the unmodified surface favor dehydration, with 94.9 percent selectivity to hydroxyacetone, while nickel-cobalt lifts dehydrogenation selectivity from 3.9 percent to 34.8 percent under visible light. In situ X-ray absorption at the Australian Synchrotron showed nickel and cobalt shifting to more reduced active states in less than a second of illumination, then reversing when the light went off.

**Links:**

- [ANSTO — Sunlight gives biodiesel by-product a second life as hydrogen and valuable chemicals](https://www.ansto.gov.au/news/sunlight-gives-biodiesel-by-product-a-second-life-as-hydrogen-and-valuable-chemicals)
- [Applied Catalysis B — Ni–Co/Zn3In2S6 glycerol photoreforming](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apcatb.2026.127458)

**Commentary:** A tenfold rise in hydrogen and a dehydrogenation selectivity shift from 3.9 percent to 34.8 percent show the pathway can be steered; hydroxyacetone remains the main product on the unmodified surface.

### 11. A hierarchical iontronic skin resolves 300-micrometer texture on a humanoid (Materials)
**Summary:** Nature Machine Intelligence on October 16 published an iontronic skin from Chuan Fei Guo and Taihong Wang at the Southern University of Science and Technology, Chaoyang Song at Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, and colleagues. The array packs 1,110 units per square centimeter and more than 10,000 units in total. Frequency encoding sends signals in parallel at 100 frames per second, and back-end convergent compression cuts bandwidth use by hundreds of times, avoiding the latency and crosstalk of serial readout as density rises. On a humanoid robot the skin resolved surface features of 300 micrometers and detected nociceptive stimuli that triggered self-protective responses. The paper maps the layered encoding, convergence, and cognitive model onto human touch rather than adding a wire for every sensor.

**Links:**

- [Nature Machine Intelligence — Large-scale iontronic skins with neural-hierarchical processing architecture](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-026-01325-6)

**Commentary:** Density and frame rate address how to read a very large array; 300-micrometer discrimination and pain-like protection are demonstration tasks, not a deployed industrial tactile standard.

### 12. Helical orbitals turn a driving current into one-way molecular rotation (Chemistry)
**Summary:** Nature Chemistry on October 16 published a molecular-motor experiment from Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Charles University in Prague, and collaborators. Feringa-type motors reach a high-energy state and then relax in only one direction because the other path is sterically blocked. This paper shows a different route: a platform, an upright axle, and a chiral rotor let orbital angular momentum from the driving current pass through helical orbitals in the axle and turn the rotor, without steric hindrance or that transitional excited state. The paper includes rotation in a scanning-tunneling-microscope junction, with computations and raw data deposited on Zenodo. The abstract does not report a rotation rate or a count of continuous working turns.

**Links:**

- [Nature Chemistry — Unidirectional rotation in a molecular motor driven by angular-momentum transfer via helical orbitals](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-026-02264-w)

**Commentary:** One-way rotation in the microscope junction shows a current can drive the rotor through helical orbitals; it is not yet a molecular machine that accumulates useful work.
