## I. Physics and Astronomy

### 1. The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics goes to IceCube and high-energy astrophysical neutrinos (Neutrino astronomy)
**Summary:** On October 6 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics to Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin–Madison “for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.” He receives the full 12 million Swedish kronor. Nature notes that this is the first physics prize since 1992 to go to a single laureate. Halzen was born in 1944 in Tienen, Belgium, received his PhD from KU Leuven in 1969, and in 1988 proposed using South Pole ice to track neutrinos. IceCube is 5,160 light sensors in a cubic kilometre of glacial ice, completed in 2011 at a cost of US$271 million with U.S. National Science Foundation funding; the collaboration now includes more than 450 researchers. Within two years of completion it had seen extremely high-energy neutrinos from beyond the Solar System, and a 2013 Science paper established a population that could not have been produced inside the Solar System.

**Commentary:** The prize recognizes an Antarctic telescope that has already been taking data for more than a decade, not a detector switched on today.

### 2. Orbital-symmetry reactions under strong correlation can be classified by crossings of Green's function zeros (Theoretical chemistry)
**Summary:** Nature Physics on October 6 published a paper by Ziren Xie, Amir Mirzanejad, and Lukas Muechler of The Pennsylvania State University. Woodward–Hoffmann rules sort pericyclic reactions as symmetry-allowed or symmetry-forbidden according to whether molecular-orbital energies cross along the reaction coordinate, but strong electronic correlation breaks the usual topological band framework. The authors use Green's functions instead: symmetry-forbidden paths are marked by crossings of zeros, not by the pole crossings expected from molecular-orbital theory, and they define symmetry-resolved invariants that can be read at the endpoints of a symmetry-preserving path. The examples are benzene and cyclobutadiene, the allowed and forbidden butadiene-to-cyclobutene pathways, and a 4π photoswitch in which symmetry is weakly broken. The work was supported by the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Basic Energy Sciences, CPIMS program (DE-SC0025352).

**Links:**

- [Nature Physics — Topological transitions in orbital-symmetry-controlled chemical reactions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03455-5)

**Commentary:** This is a many-body extension of a selection rule and a computational diagnostic, not a newly discovered laboratory reaction and not a reported synthetic yield.

## II. Space and Deep Space

### 3. NASA sets the six-satellite solar-radio mission SunRISE for no earlier than mid-2027 (Heliophysics)
**Summary:** NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory said on October 6 that SunRISE, the Sun Radio Interferometer Space Experiment, will launch no earlier than mid-2027. Six toaster-oven-size small satellites will fly in formation slightly above geosynchronous orbit, about 22,000 miles (35,000 kilometers) up, and operate as one giant radio telescope to track radio bursts from energetic particle events in the solar corona that can threaten satellites and astronauts. The mission will ride a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Kennedy Space Center as a rideshare with a payload sponsored by the U.S. Space Force's Space Systems Command. The announcement updates the launch window; the satellites have not launched.

**Links:**

- [NASA JPL — NASA's SunRISE Mission Targets No Earlier Than Mid-2027 Launch](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-sunrise-mission-targets-no-earlier-than-mid-2027-launch/)

**Commentary:** “No earlier than mid-2027” fixes the earliest window, and the radio-burst measurements still wait on the formation reaching orbit.

### 4. ESA rehearses Rosalind Franklin operations with the desert rover prototype Charlie (Mars)
**Summary:** ESA reported on October 6 that in a September exercise in Spain's Tabernas desert, the Airbus-built Mars rover prototype Charlie drove 220 metres over eight days on commands from a science team about 1,200 kilometres away, with about 100 scientists and engineers from across Europe. The site was laid out from the local geology and mineralogy to stress-test science operations for Rosalind Franklin, which is planned to land on Mars in 2030 to search for signs of ancient microbial activity. Charlie ran the guidance, navigation, and control software intended for the flight rover. On day six it travelled more than 60 metres with no operator at the wheel and less than a 1% navigation error. The Rover Operations Control Centre in Turin used panoramic and close-up cameras, a spectrometer, and ground-penetrating radar to plan the next day. The 20-day campaign was organized in Spain by Airbus UK, with The Open University running the Italian control centre. The next field test, in summer 2027, is planned to add the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter.

**Links:**

- [ESA — European rover trains for Mars in the desert](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/European_rover_trains_for_Mars_in_the_desert)

**Commentary:** The 220 metres and sub-1% navigation error demonstrate an operations drill in a terrestrial desert, not a drive already completed on Mars.

### 5. Webb releases a new view of the stellar nursery NGC 7129, about 3,300 light-years away (Star formation)
**Summary:** On October 6 NASA released a James Webb Space Telescope image of the star-forming region NGC 7129. The nursery lies about 3,300 light-years from Earth, and dust had hidden many of its protostars and stars. In the image, hot atomic hydrogen is shown in gold and cooler molecular hydrogen, shocked by embedded protostars, in red. Webb's spatial resolution shows finer gas and dust filaments than NASA's retired Spitzer Space Telescope, along with many background galaxies. NASA said astronomers will keep using the data to study how the stars and protostars in the region shape the surrounding gas and dust. Webb is led by NASA with ESA and the Canadian Space Agency.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — NASA's Webb Captures Commotion From Nebula's Stellar Jets](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasas-webb-captures-commotion-from-nebulas-stellar-jets/)

**Commentary:** This is a public image release; the October 6 text does not report a new mass, age, or jet-speed measurement.

## III. Biomedicine

### 6. Masaka, Uganda, gives the first doses of a Bundibugyo Ebola vaccine candidate that has no licensed product (Vaccines)
**Summary:** The London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine reported on October 6 that the MRC/Uganda Virus Research Institute and LSHTM Uganda Research Unit vaccinated the first participants that day in Masaka City with ChAdOx BDBV. It is a Phase I safety and immunogenicity study, designed by the Oxford Vaccine Group and the Pandemic Sciences Institute and sponsored by the University of Oxford, of a candidate against Bundibugyo virus, an Ebola species with no licensed vaccine. The trial is funded by the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations and follows a first-in-human Phase I study in the United Kingdom. The Serum Institute of India manufactured and stockpiled about 620,000 doses in two weeks for potential future use, supplied 300 vials to the UK, and supplied about 3,000 investigational doses for the Uganda trial. The Pan African Clinical Trials Registry lists a plan to enroll 110 healthy adults aged 18–55 in three cohorts: 50 in a single-dose placebo-controlled group, 20 receiving a booster at six months, and 40 people previously vaccinated against other Ebola viruses receiving a single dose, with 12 months of follow-up. The unit says local transmission in Uganda has been contained by surveillance, while the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo continues.

### 7. The FDA accepts the oral infigratinib application for children with achondroplasia and grants Priority Review (Genetic bone disease)

The company said that, if approved, it would be the first oral therapy for the condition. It cited the global Phase 3 study PROPEL 3: at week 52 the least-squares mean difference in annualized height velocity versus placebo was +1.74 cm per year, the mean difference was +2.10 cm per year (p<0.0001), and the key secondary endpoint of change in height Z-score had p<0.0001. A European marketing authorization application is planned for the fourth quarter of 2026. The drug is not approved. The company says about 55,000 people in the United States and European Union have achondroplasia, including up to 10,000 children and adolescents with open growth plates.

### 8. Claustrum neurons in seven epilepsy-surgery patients tracked uncertainty and prediction error (Neuroscience)
**Summary:** Nature Neuroscience on October 6 published an open-access paper by Eyiyemisi C. Damisah and colleagues at Yale School of Medicine. During robotic implantation of depth electrodes to locate seizure onset, seven patients with drug-resistant epilepsy played an avoidance-learning game in which they steered a spaceship away from asteroids. The team recorded single neurons in the claustrum, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala. Of 110 claustrum neurons, 78 (71%) changed their firing significantly around task events. Both claustrum and anterior cingulate encoded model-derived uncertainty and prediction error, but on different clocks: only the anterior cingulate carried uncertainty during the intertrial interval, while both regions encoded uncertainty and prediction error during active avoidance; the amygdala showed minimal latent-variable modulation. Crash rates fell from 66% ± 2.9% in the first quarter of trials to 54.9% ± 3.6% in the last quarter (P = 0.0469).

**Links:**

- [Nature Neuroscience — Human claustrum neurons encode uncertainty and prediction errors during aversive learning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02475-x)
- [Yale School of Medicine via Newswise — Hidden Part of the Brain Controls How We Prepare for Uncertainties](https://www.newswise.com/articles/hidden-part-of-the-brain-controls-how-we-prepare-for-uncertainties)

**Commentary:** These are the first human single-neuron recordings from epilepsy-monitoring electrodes in seven patients, not a clinical account of how the claustrum makes decisions in healthy people.

## IV. Climate and Energy

### 9. Marine primary organic aerosol number concentrations at Mace Head are about three times a common model parameterization (Clouds and aerosols)
**Summary:** On October 6 EurekAlert released results from Wei Xu of the Institute of Urban Environment and Ru-Jin Huang of the Institute of Earth Environment, both of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, with Jurgita Ovadnevaite of the University of Galway; the paper is in National Science Review. Using clean North Atlantic air at the Mace Head station on Ireland's west coast, they report the first size-resolved number concentrations of marine primary organic aerosol: a bimodal distribution with one mode near 60 nanometres and another between 100 and 200 nanometres. Several widely used models instead use a single mode centered at 120 nanometres and omit the smaller mode. For particles larger than 35 nanometres the parameterization underestimates the total number by about a factor of three, and between 30 and 70 nanometres by five- to sevenfold. Assuming a 30% reduction in surface tension, the cloud-condensation-nuclei contribution is underestimated by about 16% at 0.25% supersaturation and about 64% at 0.5% supersaturation. Extrapolating the Mace Head size distribution to the global ocean gives a first aerosol indirect effect on the order of −0.03 to −0.04 watts per square metre, and −0.1 to −0.2 watts per square metre over biologically productive waters when surface tension is included. The authors describe this as a single-site sensitivity estimate.

**Links:**

- [EurekAlert — Marine organic particles that seed ocean clouds may be three times more abundant than climate models assume](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1146633)
- [National Science Review — DOI 10.1093/nsr/nwag604](https://doi.org/10.1093/nsr/nwag604)

**Commentary:** The threefold underestimate comes from long-term measurements at Mace Head; the global radiative figures assume the world's oceans resemble that one station, and they are not a rerun climate-model ensemble.

### 10. Water-based organic solar cells reach 12.59% certified efficiency, with T80 beyond 2,000 hours at 85°C (Photovoltaics)
**Summary:** Nature Communications on October 6 published an open-access paper led by Wei Ma, Zhaozhao Bi, and Ke Wang at Xi'an Jiaotong University, with collaborators including The Chinese University of Hong Kong, the China Spallation Neutron Source, Shandong University, Wuhan University, and Peking University. Water-based organic-semiconductor nanoparticle inks avoid hazardous organic solvents, but efficiency and stability have stayed low. By tuning the water–oil interfacial tension during synthesis, the authors turn a donor-dominated, highly ordered shell into a mixed polycrystalline surface, improving particle fusion, phase connectivity, and packing. The water-based cells reach 12.64% efficiency, certified at 12.59%, above the previous 11.1% benchmark for water-processed devices, with a T80 exceeding 2,000 hours at 85°C. Neutron scattering was done at the China Spallation Neutron Source, and X-ray data came from the Advanced Light Source in the United States.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Water-oil interfacial kinetics control enables water-based organic solar cells with 12.59% certified efficiency](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78233-1)

**Commentary:** The 12.59% figure is a certified laboratory efficiency for a small-area water-processed cell, still far from commercial silicon-module efficiency and outdoor lifetime.
