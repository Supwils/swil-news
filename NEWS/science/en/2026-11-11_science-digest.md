## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. ESA’s Hera arrives at Didymos and begins “rectangular-orbit” early characterisation (planetary defence)
**Summary:** On November 11, ESA reported that its planetary-defence mission Hera has arrived at the Didymos binary asteroid system, holding roughly 25–30 km from the pair and starting early characterisation. Primary Didymos is about 780 m across; moonlet Dimorphos about 151 m. In such weak gravity, Hera flies near-straight segments at only ~12 cm/s and fires thrusters every three then four days to turn 90°, tracing “rectangular orbits.” NASA’s DART struck Dimorphos at ~6.1 km/s in 2022 and shortened its orbit by about 33 minutes; Hera will now test which impact-model forecasts match reality. Next month the craft is to move to ~20 km for detailed characterisation and later deploy two CubeSats.

**Commentary:** Kinetic deflection moves from a one-shot impact demo to a close-up crash-site investigation with falsifiable models.

### 2. Poland’s Orbital Matter: first structure 3D-printed in open space (~30 cm boom) (in-space manufacturing)
**Summary:** ESA reported on November 11 that Orbital Matter’s printer on the Replicator 2 satellite built a hollow boom about 32.5 cm long and 3.8 cm in diameter in roughly 30 minutes under open-space extremes. The Rubik’s-cube-scale printer sat beneath a stowed solar array so the extending boom could push the array open, confirming success. Prior additive-manufacturing milestones occurred in controlled cabin, suborbital, or otherwise sheltered settings (ISS polymers, DLR suborbital metals, ESA’s 2024 on-orbit metals). ESA materials leads called the demo a step toward sustainable, self-sufficient in-space manufacturing.

**Links:**

- [ESA — First structure 3D-printed in the extremes of open space](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/technology-engineering-and-quality/first-structure-3d-printed-in-the-extremes-of-open-space)

**Commentary:** Moving printers from pressurized labs into vacuum and thermal cycling is what turns in-space construction from demo to infrastructure.

### 3. Independent imaging reconfirms the M87* black-hole shadow: ring ~42.2±1.5 μas (gravity physics)
**Summary:** An Australia-led team published on November 11 in *Nature* an independent interferometric imaging method—free of strong morphological priors and built on observables robust to calibration errors—applied to Event Horizon Telescope data on M87*. The reconstructions unequivocally recover a ring-like shadow with angular diameter 42.2±1.5 microarcseconds, corresponding to about 6.5±0.5 billion solar masses under nominal scaling. High-quality epochs from 2017, 2018, and 2021 yield compact ~40 μas rings brightened south/southwest at ~20° inclination. The result answers critiques since 2022 that the original ring was a reconstruction artefact and largely corroborates the 2019 EHT image.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Independent interferometric observable confirms black hole shadow in M87*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11241-9)
- [Nature News — Iconic first image of black hole holds up to scrutiny](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03642-7)

**Commentary:** When an independent observable reproduces the ring, the black-hole shadow graduates from iconic picture to testable physics.

## II. Biomedicine & Health

### 4. DeepMind launches AlphaGenome 2: predicting DNA-variant effects across hundreds of cell types (genomic AI)
**Summary:** On November 11, Google DeepMind announced AlphaGenome 2, trained on more than 100 million single-cell profiles across 68 tissues to turn static cell atlases into a predictive engine for human genetics. Versus prior tools, it nearly doubles the share of expression-altering causal variants recovered on TenK10K-style benchmarks (~30% vs 16%) and improves directional gene-effect calls; it also computationally recapitulates the IBD finding that a distant ETS2 regulatory variant acts in gut macrophages. The AlphaGenome API is open for non-commercial research, with commercial private preview; the models are research-only and not clinically validated. *Nature* noted the model reproduced aspects of a complex genetics discovery without wet-lab pipetting.

### 5. Gladstone: blood–brain barrier stops importing protective proteins early in Alzheimer’s mouse models (neurodegeneration)
**Summary:** On November 11 in *Cell*, Andrew Yang’s Gladstone team mapped plasma proteins that cross the mouse blood–brain barrier: of 861 labeled proteins, 92 entered the brain, many from distant organs such as the liver. Liver-derived APOA1 reaches astrocytes and triggers cholesterol release needed for synapses. In Alzheimer’s-like mice, the barrier ceases importing APOA1 and other protective proteins before it becomes leaky, coinciding with synapse loss and behavioral changes; boosting hepatic APOA1 raised brain levels and eased some disease markers. The authors argue Alzheimer’s may begin when the brain’s bodily supply line fails; human generalizability remains unproven.

**Links:**

- [Gladstone Institutes — Study rewrites the rules of the blood-brain barrier](https://gladstone.org/news/study-rewrites-rules-blood-brain-barrier/)
- [Nature — New culprit in early Alzheimer’s: BBB blocks protective proteins](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03655-2)

**Commentary:** Recasting the BBB as active logistics—not just a leak wall—points Alzheimer’s early intervention toward restoring peripheral supply lines.

### 6. enGene: detalimogene pivotal cohort hits 56% complete response; BLA to start by year-end (bladder cancer gene therapy)
**Summary:** On November 11, enGene Therapeutics updated pivotal Cohort 1 of the Phase 2 LEGEND trial in BCG-unresponsive high-risk NMIBC with CIS: among 120 efficacy-evaluable patients (125 enrolled), complete response at any time rose to 56% from 54% in the April 2026 cut; preliminary median duration of response is 10.1 months, landmark 12-month CR 24%, and Kaplan–Meier DoR ≥12 months 43%. About 97% of evaluable patients remained free of progression to muscle-invasive or metastatic disease, and 86% avoided cystectomy; serious treatment-related adverse events occurred in ~2%. After FDA pre-BLA alignment on the efficacy population, the company plans to start the BLA by end-2026 and finish in H1 2027—positioning detalimogene as a non-viral plasmid gene therapy usable in routine urology settings if approved.

### 7. Manchester and the Institute for Protein Design: AI-designed enzymes for a lenacapavir amide step (biocatalysis)
**Summary:** The University of Manchester reported on November 11 that, with Gates Foundation support and the UW Institute for Protein Design, it used the RFD4-Proteina generative model to design de novo amide ligases for a hard amide-bond step in synthesizing the long-acting HIV prevention drug lenacapavir. Of 67 designs tested, four showed clear activity; the best converted ~79% of the starting amine with high S-enantiomer selectivity. Earlier directed evolution of an aminotransferase screened >12,000 variants to >98% conversion, 90% isolated yield, and 99.5% enantiomeric purity for a chiral amine intermediate. Related work appears on bioRxiv (11 Nov 2026) and in *JACS*, aiming to cut manufacturing cost for global access.

**Links:**

- [University of Manchester — AI-designed enzymes for medicines](https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/ai-designed-enzymes-for-medicines/)

**Commentary:** Compressing enzyme discovery from tens of thousands of variants to dozens of computed candidates is how generative design attacks global-health cost bottlenecks.

## III. Climate & Environment

### 8. Potsdam Institute: without land-sector mitigation, 1.5°C is out of reach this century (land–energy trade-offs)
**Summary:** A PIK team published on November 11 in *Nature Sustainability* REMIND–MAgPIE pathways under varied AFOLU policy portfolios. Without substantial land-based mitigation, limiting warming to 1.5°C by 2100 after a temporary overshoot is unachievable. To keep peak warming below 1.7°C with a global carbon price alone, the 2040 price would need ~$400/t and 2025–2100 consumption loss ~3.5% versus baseline; full land-sector mobilisation cuts those figures to ~$130/t and ~1.3%. The authors recommend a balanced mix: residual biomass energy, total bioenergy capped near 100 EJ/yr, CH₄ and N₂O pricing capped around $75/tCO₂e, plus strong global forest protection.

**Links:**

- [Nature Sustainability — Cost-effective climate change mitigation through land sector contributions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-026-01977-7)
- [PIK — Why land is so important for climate protection](https://www.pik-potsdam.de/de/aktuelles/nachrichten/so-wichtig-ist-land-beim-klimaschutz)

**Commentary:** In the climate arithmetic, land is not an optional bonus—without it, the energy transition’s price tag becomes socially unaffordable.

### 9. SUSTech: atmospheric CO₂ growth–land carbon sink coupling strengthened over six decades (carbon cycle)
**Summary:** On November 11, Zhenzhong Zeng’s team at Southern University of Science and Technology highlighted a *Nature Geoscience* study combining 1959–2022 atmospheric CO₂ growth-rate records with inversion and dynamic vegetation model land-sink estimates. The negative correlation between detrended CO₂ growth and land-sink anomalies has strengthened over six decades. Regional contributions reorganized: tropics increasingly reinforce the global anticorrelation, while high latitudes often flip sign; in CarboScope, the land-area fraction reinforcing the anticorrelation rose from about 78.1% to 82.7%. The authors stress the coupling is time-dependent, so recent observational constraints may not transfer cleanly to earlier periods or future climates.

**Links:**

- [SUSTech News — Enhanced atmospheric–land carbon coupling](https://newshub.sustech.edu.cn/en/?p=50578)

**Commentary:** As land sinks increasingly dominate year-to-year CO₂ swings, extrapolating modern couplings backward or forward risks systematic bias.

### 10. *Nature*: chordwise-flexible rubber tidal blades passively cut fatigue loads by up to one-third (ocean energy)
**Summary:** An Edinburgh-led team published on November 11 in *Nature* flexible rubber tidal-turbine blades with chordwise trailing-edge deformation. Across simulated tidal cycles they matched the total energy yield of rigid blades with active pitch control while cutting high-frequency fatigue-inducing load fluctuations by up to one-third. Aeroelastic flattening of camber under load provides passive regulation without underwater sensors and actuators. Europe’s tidal resource is estimated near 50 TWh/yr and the U.S. near 220 TWh/yr, but harsh hydrodynamics drive capital and maintenance costs; passive flexible blades offer a path to lower LCOE.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Flexible rubber blades for tidal turbines](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11278-w)

**Commentary:** Tidal power’s bottleneck is underwater mechanical reliability—replacing pitch motors with material flex designs failure points out of the system.

## IV. Fundamental Research & Materials

### 11. MIT and Barabási teams: scale-rich metamaterials turn network heterogeneity into tunable impact absorption (mechanical metamaterials)
**Summary:** MIT and Albert-László Barabási’s group published an open-access *Nature* paper on November 11 introducing “scale-rich” metamaterials that embed network-science multiscale heterogeneity into mechanical architectures—ligament lengths, thicknesses, and connectivity spanning orders of magnitude under just two control parameters. Simulations and experiments show far wider tunable elastic anisotropy (~190-fold across densities in the report), more delocalized nonlinear deformation with high energy absorption, stronger dynamic dissipation, and programmable elastic-wave control versus conventional single-scale lattices. MIT News highlights applications in helmets, packaging, armor, and acoustic lenses.

**Commentary:** Metamaterials finally leave the unit-cell photocopier behind, writing internet-like heterogeneity into mechanical skeletons.

### 12. Penn Engineering: cell-membrane-inspired coating speeds organic electrochemical transistors ~67× (bioelectronics)
**Summary:** Yuanwen Jiang’s Penn Engineering team with partners in Taiwan, Drexel, and Rutgers described on November 11 *Nature Materials* work on a cell-membrane-inspired layer for organic electrochemical transistors that reconciles speed and wet stability. Device response fell from ~87 μs to ~1.3 μs (~67× faster); swelling dropped from ~247% to 19%; devices retained ~96% of initial current after four weeks versus ~59% unmodified and ran stably up to 28 days in simulated physiology. In an animal model implanted in a reward-related brain region, modified transistors tracked rapid dopamine signals with reproducible measurements over seven days. The authors stress long-term safety, biofouling, and system integration remain before human use.

**Links:**

- [Penn Engineering — Cell-membrane-inspired material for faster bioelectronics](https://www.engineering.upenn.edu/stories/inspired-by-cell-membranes-a-new-material-could-make-bioelectronics-faster-and-more-stable/)

**Commentary:** Restricting water made devices faster—showing the bottleneck is often a swollen semiconductor scaffold, not sluggish ions.
