## I. Space and Deep Space

### 1. CRS-35 cargo Dragon docks with the International Space Station (Spaceflight)
**Summary:** NASA's station blog reported that the SpaceX Dragon on the agency's 35th commercial resupply mission docked at 10:27 a.m. EDT on October 15 to the space-facing port of the Harmony module, delivering more than 6,300 pounds of food, supplies, and equipment for the Expedition 75 crew. The cargo includes the station's final set of roll-out solar arrays (IROSAs), to be installed later this fall. It also carries hardware to manufacture artificial retinas in microgravity, 3D heart-tissue models aimed at large-scale drug testing, materials for studying how a new type of glass forms in microgravity, and brain organoids that could reveal treatment targets for Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and multiple sclerosis. Dragon is scheduled to stay until mid-November and then splash down off California with time-critical research. SpaceX's mission page says this Dragon launched at 6:33 a.m. EDT on October 13 from SLC-40 and docked autonomously after about 52 hours; the same spacecraft previously flew CRS-26, CRS-29, and CRS-33.

### 2. Hera's 93-minute braking burn was scheduled for this morning (Planetary defense)
**Summary:** On October 7, ESA said the planetary-defense spacecraft Hera would carry out its hardest sustained braking since launch early on October 15. The spacecraft rotates so its ring of orbit-control thrusters faces the Didymos binary, and three hydrazine thrusters fire together for 93 minutes, cutting velocity relative to the asteroids by a few hundred meters per second. Flight-dynamics engineer Francesco Castellini said a short test burn on October 2 preceded the full command sequence. After the burn, the team planned to reacquire the signal with a 35-meter deep-space antenna at New Norcia, Australia, and to check the achieved delta-v with guidance, navigation, and control telemetry plus ranging and Doppler. A shorter follow-up, BRM-2, is set for early October 22 and lasts about 31 minutes. Hera launched on October 7, 2024, to inspect Dimorphos after NASA's 2022 DART impact. Sources available for this digest describe that plan and do not report a measured post-burn delta-v.

**Links:**

- [ESA — Brake! Time for ESA's Hera asteroid mission to go slow](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Hera/Brake!_Time_for_ESA_s_Hera_asteroid_mission_to_go_slow)

**Commentary:** Until the velocity change is checked, the 93-minute burn remains the approach plan for the DART impact site, not a closed orbit result.

## II. Biomedicine

### 3. First patient dosed in a first-line Phase II trial of WRN inhibitor VVD-214 (Oncology)
**Summary:** On October 15, Bayer subsidiary Vividion said the first patient has been dosed in a global Phase II trial of VVD-214, an investigational oral inhibitor of Werner helicase (WRN). The randomized study compares VVD-214 plus pembrolizumab with pembrolizumab alone as first-line treatment for advanced microsatellite instability-high (MSI-high) or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) colorectal cancer. The company, citing prior literature, said about 15 percent of colorectal tumors are MSI-high, the largest such patient population. Trial NCT07811193 is planned to enroll about 140 patients at sites in about 21 countries across North America, Latin America, Europe, and Asia. The primary endpoint is the six-month progression-free survival rate. A Phase Ib study, NCT06004245, is evaluating VVD-214 plus bevacizumab after prior therapy. The release said Phase Ia data presented at AACR 2025 and published in Nature Medicine on September 29, 2026, showed the drug was well tolerated with early signals of activity.

### 5. First U.S. patient receives an ultra-thin implant for resistant migraine (Neuromodulation)
**Summary:** Salvia BioElectronics said on October 15 that the first U.S. patient has been implanted with the MySalvia System at Vista Clinical Research in Newnan, Georgia, opening U.S. enrollment in RECLAIM-II (NCT07719608). The release described early signs of sustained benefit in those studies and did not report efficacy numbers from the pivotal trial.

## III. Climate and Environment

### 6. A year-long blind test sets bounds on methane-sensing satellites (Climate)
**Summary:** Nature on October 14 published a single-blind test led by Frances Reuland and Adam Brandt at Stanford with collaborators including the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry, which issued a Stanford-based release on October 15. Over one year at Casa Grande, Arizona, the team ran 746 events: 481 metered methane releases of 9.5 to 1,183.7 kilograms per hour and 265 blank controls, timed to overpasses by 12 government, commercial, and nonprofit satellites. Fifteen analysis teams from nine countries produced more than 2,250 estimates. Instruments built for methane had true-positive rates of 92 to 100 percent and false-positive rates of 0 to 11 percent, with quantification slopes of 0.83 to 1.00 and a 44 percent mean absolute percentage error. Repurposed instruments detected 62 to 88 percent of plumes, with a 46 percent mean absolute percentage error. The release added that general-purpose land scanners detected 29 to 37 percent on clear days. Artificial-intelligence methods produced 79 percent of false positives, and ground-measured cloud cover above 20 percent degraded performance across systems. The paper's claim is that satellites can detect and quantify methane point sources accurately when the source location is approximately known.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Evaluating space-based methane detection through a one-year blind test](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11115-0)
- [Max Planck Institute for Chemistry — Satellites accurately detect methane emissions in a large-scale test](https://www.mpic.de/6097847/satellites-accurately-detect-methane-emissions-in-large-scale-test)

**Commentary:** Purpose-built sensors caught nearly all clear-sky test plumes; cloud cover and AI false positives decide where that monitoring can be used.

### 7. Reef-building coral reassessment finds 44 percent of data-sufficient species threatened (Climate)
**Summary:** On October 15 in Mombasa, the IUCN said its Coral Specialist Group had reassessed all 892 reef-building coral species on the Red List, with more than 60 experts and data through 2020. Of 774 species with sufficient data, 340, or 44 percent, are threatened: 33 Critically Endangered, 251 Endangered, and 56 Vulnerable. About one third were threatened in the 2008 global assessment. The species count rose from 845 to 892, and Data Deficient listings fell from 141 to 118. Threatened shares were 43 percent of assessed Indo-Pacific species and 50 percent of assessed Atlantic species. Among Indo-Pacific Acroporidae, 191 of 279 species, or 68 percent, were classified as Endangered. Climate change determined the final status of more than 90 percent of species at highest risk. The Red List Index moved from 0.96 before 1998 to 0.89 in 2008 and 0.74 in 2024. The authors stressed that the assessment predates the 2023-2025 fourth global bleaching event and said the strengthening 2026-27 super El Nino further raises risk, so the share currently threatened is likely higher than 44 percent.

**Links:**

- [IUCN — Nearly half of reef-building coral species face elevated relative risk of extinction](https://iucn.org/press-release/202610/nearly-half-reef-building-coral-species-face-elevated-relative-risk-extinction)

### 8. Gooier marine snow can carry more than six times as much carbon into the deep ocean (Ocean carbon)
**Summary:** The University of Lincoln on October 15 described a study published in Science on October 8 by Bryce Inman, Stuart Humphries, and Farooq Azam. Using molecular rotors, they mapped the nanoscale viscosity of natural marine-snow aggregates. These particles of surface organic matter start more than 10 billion tonnes of carbon sinking each year, and only about 1 to 3 billion tonnes reach the deep ocean; bacteria break down much of the rest. The paper reports uneven viscosity inside aggregates: less-viscous regions are open to bacterial colonization, and more-viscous regions correspond to a barrier against infiltration. More viscous aggregates take longer to degrade and sink farther. Lincoln said the researchers estimate that these particles could carry more than six times as much carbon to the deep ocean as less viscous marine snow. The work began during Inman's postdoctoral fellowship at Scripps Institution of Oceanography and continued in Humphries's lab at Lincoln with EPSRC funding.

**Links:**

- [Science — Marine snow viscosity regulates microbial degradation and the ocean carbon sink](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adx4170)
- [University of Lincoln — Gooey marine snow could help oceans lock away carbon](https://news.lincoln.ac.uk/2026/10/15/gooey-marine-snow-could-help-oceans-lock-away-carbon/)

**Commentary:** The sixfold difference comes from a viscosity barrier to bacteria and a sinking estimate, not from a direct revision of the global carbon-sink inventory.

## IV. Fundamental Research

### 9. Magic-state cultivation on Willow cuts the error by about a factor of 40 (Quantum computing)
**Summary:** Science posted a First Release on October 15 by Emma Rosenfeld, Craig Gidney, and colleagues at Google Quantum AI. On a superconducting processor they implemented magic-state cultivation, including code-switching into a surface code, and a fault-tolerant measurement protocol that bounds magic-state fidelity. The abstract says cultivation reduces the error by a factor of 40, with a state fidelity of at least 0.9999(1), while retaining 8 percent of attempts. New Scientist reported the same day that the experiment ran on the company's Willow processor. It said a 2024 cultivation proposal had argued, in theory, that consequential programs might need about one-tenth as many qubits as previously expected, and that this experiment shows the method works in hardware. The article quoted Zhenyu Cai of Imperial College London saying Willow's errors still need to fall further before it can tackle truly useful problems.

**Links:**

- [Science — Magic state cultivation on a superconducting quantum processor](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aee7961)
- [New Scientist — Google showcases a more efficient way to fix quantum computer errors](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2593999-google-showcases-way-to-fix-quantum-computer-errors-more-efficiently/)

**Commentary:** The 40-fold error cut and 0.9999 fidelity rest on keeping 8 percent of attempts, so the yield is part of the cost of a fault-tolerant machine.

### 10. A monolayer van der Waals kagome lattice raises flat-band electron density more than 220-fold (Condensed matter)
**Summary:** Nature Physics on October 15 published a paper by Hyungkun Bong, Choongjae Won, Seongyong Lee, Soomin Oh, Mingu Kang, and colleagues, including Kin Fai Mak, Jie Shan, and Kyle Shen at Cornell. The team realized monolayer kagome lattices in the van der Waals family A2T3X4, where A is an alkali metal, T a transition metal, and X a chalcogen. Nanofocused angle-resolved photoemission shows an isolated kagome flat band just below the Fermi level down to the monolayer limit. Electrostatic gating can move that band to the Fermi level, giving access to partially filled flat-band physics. Compared with existing moire artificial flat-band systems, the platform has more than 220-fold higher flat-band electron density, which the authors say opens higher-temperature flat-band phenomena than those systems currently allow.

**Links:**

- [Nature Physics — Monolayer van der Waals kagome lattice for high-temperature flat-band physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03492-0)

**Commentary:** A flat-band density more than two orders of magnitude above moire lattices is what makes higher-temperature correlation physics plausible; gating to the Fermi level opens that experiment.

### 11. Mid-circuit resets on an IBM processor pull quantum chaos back under control (Quantum)
**Summary:** The City College of New York reported on October 15 that researchers from Rutgers, IBM, and collaborating institutions including CCNY's Sriram Ganeshan had shown adaptive quantum circuits in Nature Physics; the paper appeared online on October 9. On an IBM superconducting processor they ran a quantum version of the classically chaotic Bernoulli map. The map scrambles quantum information, while local mid-circuit measurements and resets try to steer the dynamics toward a fixed point. That competition drives a dynamical phase transition between quantum and classical dynamics. The abstract says the processor applied up to nearly 5,000 entangling gates and 5,000 non-unitary mid-circuit operations on systems of up to 100 qubits and yielded estimates of the universal critical properties. CCNY said that scale is far beyond the roughly two dozen qubits the team could simulate classically, and that the results matched theory. Collaborators also included the CUNY Graduate Center, Iowa State University, Ames National Laboratory, Pennsylvania State University, and Louisiana State University.

**Links:**

- [Nature Physics — Order from chaos with adaptive circuits on quantum hardware](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03470-6)
- [CCNY — Scientists move a step toward quantum computers that can correct their own mistakes](https://www.ccny.cuny.edu/news/scientists-move-step-toward-quantum-computers-can-correct-their-own-mistakes)

**Commentary:** Measurement and reset on 100 qubits show the hardware can rewrite chaotic dynamics while a circuit runs; the paper measures a phase transition of a Bernoulli map, with fault-tolerant logical qubits still further along that road.
