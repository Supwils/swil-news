## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. NASA: LISA gravitational waves could reveal exoplanets in double white-dwarf systems (gravitational-wave astronomy)
**Summary:** On November 10, NASA Goddard researchers detailed two *Astrophysical Journal* papers showing how the future LISA observatory (ESA-led, NASA partnered; mid-2030s launch) could uncover a new exoplanet population. The Milky Way may host ~10 million double white dwarfs with orbital periods under an hour; LISA is expected to resolve about 10,000 individual systems. A sufficiently massive planet would imprint Doppler sidebands on the nearly continuous gravitational-wave signal. The team also outlines JWST infrared-excess confirmation within a few hundred light-years: Webb could detect ~5 Jupiter-mass companions at ~500 light-years, while LISA could reach sub-Jupiter masses under ideal conditions. No planets around binary white dwarfs are yet known; detections would open a distinct new class.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — Gravitational waves can reveal new worlds](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/lisa/gravitational-waves-can-reveal-new-worlds/)

**Commentary:** Planet hunting expands from “seeing light” to “hearing spacetime ripples,” turning double white dwarfs into a new gravitational-wave hunting ground.

### 2. Caltech: Gravitational-wave data hint that ~10-solar-mass black holes come from failed supernovae (stellar evolution)
**Summary:** A Caltech-led team reported on November 10 in *Physical Review D* an analysis of 257 black-hole mergers from a decade of LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA data. About three-quarters of the sample pile up near 10 solar masses, and the team for the first time identifies a clear scarcity between 12 and 16 solar masses. The 10-solar-mass population also shows a narrower spin distribution, supporting a shared formation channel—massive stars that implode quietly as “failed supernovae” rather than exploding. The authors stress this is not direct evidence of failed supernovae, but the statistics match theoretical expectations; NSF, DOE, and the Simons Foundation funded the work.

**Links:**

- [Caltech — Hints of a star population that failed to explode](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/astronomers-find-hints-of-star-population-that-failed-to-explode)

**Commentary:** Invisible stellar “dark deaths” leave a fossil imprint in the black-hole mass spectrum—merger catalogs are becoming a record of how stars die.

## II. Biomedicine & Health

### 3. FDA approves pimicotinib (Pimjetmi) for symptomatic tenosynovial giant cell tumor (targeted oncology)
**Summary:** On November 10, 2026, the U.S. FDA approved EMD Serono’s kinase inhibitor pimicotinib (Pimjetmi) for adults with symptomatic TGCT when surgery may worsen function or cause severe morbidity. Efficacy comes from the international double-blind MANEUVER trial (NCT05804045): once-daily 50 mg achieved a Week-25 BICR ORR of 56% by RECIST v1.1 versus 3.2% on placebo; among responders, 91% had duration of response ≥6 months and 77% ≥12 months, with significant gains in range of motion, worst pain, and physical function. Common adverse reactions include pruritus, rash, and facial/periorbital edema. The review used Project Orbis and carried Fast Track and Breakthrough Therapy designations.

**Links:**

- [FDA — Approves pimicotinib for symptomatic TGCT](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cancer-approvals/fda-approves-pimicotinib-symptomatic-tenosynovial-giant-cell-tumor)

**Commentary:** For hard-to-resect, recurring TGCT, a 56% objective response rate shifts care from serial surgery toward a measurable systemic option.

### 4. Baylor and Oxford: Blocking miR-31-5p–calcitonin-receptor interaction reverses atrial fibrosis ~30% in mice (atrial fibrillation)
**Summary:** On November 10, Baylor College of Medicine and the University of Oxford described *Nature Cardiovascular Research* findings that atrial fibroblasts from AFib patients overexpress microRNA-31-5p, which suppresses the calcitonin receptor (CTR) and drives scar formation. A selective locked-nucleic-acid target-site blocker restored CTR in human AFib fibroblasts and mouse models. In mice, the treatment prevented atrial fibrosis, reversed established fibrosis by about 30% versus baseline (while untreated controls progressed), and cut the duration and burden of abnormal rhythms. More than 10 million U.S. adults live with AFib, and no approved therapy directly reverses atrial scarring; human efficacy remains unproven.

**Links:**

- [Baylor College of Medicine — Treatment reduces heart scarring and abnormal rhythms](https://blogs.bcm.edu/2026/11/10/new-treatment-reduces-heart-scarring-and-abnormal-heart-rhythms-offering-a-possibility-for-future-therapies/)

**Commentary:** Rhythm drugs rarely remodel the atrium—hitting the fibroblast “brake protein” finally yields experimental evidence that scar can reverse.

### 5. J&J’s Caplyta second Phase 3 win in bipolar I mania: benefit from Day 3, supporting a U.S. filing (neuropsychiatry)
**Summary:** On November 10, Johnson & Johnson said Study 452—the second pivotal Phase 3 trial of Caplyta (lumateperone) 42 mg once daily for manic episodes with or without mixed features in adults with bipolar I disorder—met primary and key secondary endpoints. In the three-week global randomized double-blind trial, Caplyta cut Young Mania Rating Scale totals by 3.7 points more than placebo at Week 3 (effect size −0.43; P<.0001), with significant improvement from Day 3; 43.9% achieved ≥50% YMRS improvement versus 23.7% on placebo. CGI-S severity also improved. Common related adverse events were dizziness, dry mouth, and somnolence/sedation. With prior positive Study 451, J&J plans an FDA submission for bipolar mania; Caplyta is already approved for bipolar depression, schizophrenia, and adjunctive MDD, but not mania.

**Links:**

- [Johnson & Johnson — CAPLYTA Phase 3 Study 452 in bipolar mania](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/johnson-johnson-announces-caplyta-lumateperone-shows-significant-and-rapid-improvement-in-bipolar-mania-in-second-pivotal-phase-3-study)

**Commentary:** Bipolar drugs often treat depression better than mania—two consistent Phase 3 anti-mania signals suggest one molecule may finally bridge both poles.

### 6. California voters approve a record ~$8.4 billion immunology research bond—largest U.S. science vote ever (research funding)
**Summary:** *Nature* reported on November 10 that California voters passed Proposition 38 in the midterm elections, authorizing about $8.4 billion in state bonds for immunology research aimed at harnessing or re-engineering the immune system against diseases such as cancer and Alzheimer’s—the largest science funding package ever approved by a U.S. public vote. Roughly half would go directly to institutes meeting the proposition’s criteria, drawing criticism because medical-device billionaire Gary Michelson wrote the measure, spent about $45 million to pass it, and co-founded the only institute that currently qualifies for direct funding. About $4 billion would fund competitive grants at California public universities and nonprofits. Commercialized therapies would have to be sold at a 20% discount in California, with 10% of royalties returning to the state general fund.

**Links:**

## III. Climate & Environment

### 7. *Nature Climate Change*: Global fossil CO₂ may fall ~0.4% in 2026—mostly an oil-price shock, not a clean-energy peak (emissions trends)
**Summary:** Corinne Le Quéré of the University of East Anglia and colleagues published on November 10 in *Nature Climate Change* that after ~0.8% average annual growth from 2019–2025, global fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions are projected to drop about 0.4% in 2026, driven mainly by ~1.9% and ~0.3% declines in oil and gas emissions partly offset by ~0.6% growth in coal. The authors attribute the dip largely to higher oil prices and supply constraints tied to the Iran conflict, not a lasting transition-driven peak. Solar and wind are estimated to have offset ~4.1 GtCO₂ in 2025 (~2.1 Gt in China); EVs ~0.19 Gt. From 2025–2028, clean tech may offset ~1.64–2.0 Gt of ~2.59 Gt demand-driven growth—most, but not enough for a sustained decline without faster deployment.

### 8. *Nature*: Mongolia’s underground mycorrhizal networks may be key to reversing grassland degradation (ecological restoration)
**Summary:** A November 10 *Nature* news piece covers Toby Kiers’s Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam team and the Society for the Protection of Underground Networks mapping arbuscular mycorrhizal hotspots in Mongolia, where ~77% of land is degraded and warming runs about three times the global average. Degraded sites had at least 44% fewer mycorrhizal species and ~60% less plant cover. Earlier southwestern Mongolia sequencing found 500+ fungal types, ~90% previously unknown. These fungi trade soil nitrogen and phosphorus for plant carbon, aiding sequestration and drought resilience. The team is engaging governments to fold fungal hotspot maps into desertification and climate-resilience plans; three more countries recently signed a UN fungi-conservation pledge, bringing signatories to 17.

**Links:**

### 9. Cornell and CityU: A critical bubble radius for green-hydrogen electrolysis—beyond it, energy losses can jump >50% (hydrogen)
**Summary:** Cornell and City University of Hong Kong researchers described on November 10 a 3-D model (published November 9 in *EES Catalysis*) spanning eight orders of magnitude that quantifies how hydrogen bubbles waste energy in alkaline electrolyzers, with ~1,000× lower computational cost than prior approaches. Blocking active electrode area is the largest bubble-induced loss; once a bubble exceeds a critical radius set by electrode geometry and wettability—not current density—extra voltage rises exponentially and can exceed the bubble-free baseline by more than 50% in some cases. Keeping the detachment-to-critical radius ratio below one could limit single-bubble overvoltage to within ~3%, giving electrode designers a concrete target.

**Links:**

- [Cornell Chronicle — Critical bubble size for green hydrogen](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/11/study-identifies-critical-bubble-size-green-hydrogen-production)

**Commentary:** The green-hydrogen bottleneck may not be the catalyst—shedding bubbles before the critical size can beat another coat of precious metal.

## IV. Fundamental Research & Materials

### 10. CU Boulder: One-atom-wide glass threads show quantized phonon heat flow for the first time (nanoscale thermal transport)
**Summary:** On November 10, Longji Cui’s CU Boulder team reported in *Nature Nanotechnology* that a scanning thermal probe microscope stretched glass contacts into a one-atom-wide thread and recorded quantized heat flow carried by atomic vibrations—each step about 40% of the quantum of thermal conductance for a single channel. Bulk glass is a poor conductor because disorder scatters phonons; in the one-dimensional limit there is nowhere for disorder to hide, so heat passes efficiently. Silicon dioxide glass is a common chip insulator; the result gives a counterintuitive benchmark for nanoscale thermal management and shows the probe can diagnose atomic-scale heat flow in other semiconductors. NSF funded the work with Spanish collaborators.

**Links:**

- [CU Boulder Today — Glass stretched one atom wide shows quantized heat flow](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2026/11/10/glass-does-surprising-things-when-stretched-1-atom-wide-discovery-could-help-shrink)

**Commentary:** An insulator “wakes up” at the atomic limit—before chips shrink further, engineers need to know how heat steps, quantum by quantum, through single-atom channels.

### 11. University of Tokyo: Carbon engineering turns insulating boron nitride into a 2-inch wafer-scale p-type 2D semiconductor (2D electronics)
**Summary:** Via an November 10 EurekAlert release, a University of Tokyo Graduate School of Engineering team and Japan–Taiwan collaborators report in *Nature* that controlled carbon incorporation into hexagonal boron nitride yields monolayer boron carbon nitride (BCN) as a p-type semiconductor, epitaxially grown continuously across a 2-inch Cu(111) wafer. The bandgap is ~1.90 eV. Across 224 FETs from three growth batches, hole mobility reached ~100 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹, on-current exceeded 0.9 mA per micrometer of channel width, and on/off ratios were about 10⁸. High-quality, large-area p-type 2D materials have been scarce for complementary circuits; full complementary demos are still ahead, with next steps on carbon substitution control, lower growth temperatures, and integration with n-type devices.

**Links:**

- [EurekAlert — Wafer-scale p-type boron carbon nitride](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1150744)

**Commentary:** 2D circuits have long lacked a workhorse p-type—carbon-doping insulating BN to wafer scale finally pulls complementary logic closer to a process line.
