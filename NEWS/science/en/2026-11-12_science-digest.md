## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. ESA Euclid releases DR1-Foundation: a billion-object foundation for a 3D cosmic map (dark-Universe survey)
**Summary:** On November 12, ESA announced Euclid’s first major science data drop, DR1-Foundation: about 13 months of observations covering roughly 1900 deg² of sky (about 9500 full Moons) and ~1.7 PB of data, capturing light from more than a billion objects. Catalogues include over 900 million galaxies, about 15,000 strong-lensing candidates, more than 60 million quasar candidates, and spectra for more than 160 million sources; the strong-lens catalogue already exceeds all previous catalogues combined. The release covers only ~14% of Euclid’s eventual survey area; full Data Release 1 is due mid-2027 with weak lensing and cosmic-web placement. Euclid launched in mid-2023 to constrain dark energy, dark matter, and large-scale structure.

### 2. ESA’s Hera images Didymos and a half-hidden Dimorphos from 56 km (planetary defence)
**Summary:** On November 12, ESA released an early Hera preview from about 56 km: one face of the ~780-m primary Didymos shows boulders, smoother regions, and a surprisingly sharp equatorial ridge; the ~151-m moonlet Dimorphos peeks from near Didymos’s top, orbiting a little more than a kilometre away. Spinning-top-shaped Didymos rotates every ~2.2 hours, near breakup; models suggest material may flow toward the equator and even shed to form the moonlet. NASA’s DART hit Dimorphos at ~6.1 km/s in 2022 and shortened its orbit by ~33 minutes. More detailed dual-asteroid images are due November 25, with months of close-up crash-site work and two CubeSats ahead.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** The ridge and half-visible moonlet push kinetic deflection from orbital bookkeeping into geological and mass-distribution forensics.

### 3. Hubble releases a strong-lens gallery to prep Roman’s dark-matter tests (observatory synergy)
**Summary:** On November 12, NASA’s Hubble team released a gallery of strong gravitational lenses as a sampler and modelling training set for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launched in August 2026. Lenses form when foreground galaxy mass bends light from background galaxies into arcs or Einstein rings; Hubble provides high-resolution visible/UV views of individuals, while Roman’s wider infrared field will find many more. Researchers plan to use precise Hubble models to spot tiny arc imperfections that could betray dark-matter clumps of about a million to a billion solar masses—predicted by cold dark matter—versus the Milky Way halo’s ~1.3 trillion solar masses. The same images will train an intelligent search tool for Roman’s wide-field arcs.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — Hubble Offers Cosmic Sampler of Targets for Roman](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasas-hubble-offers-cosmic-sampler-of-targets-for-roman/)

**Commentary:** Hubble calibrates in high definition; Roman surveys in wide angle—small-scale dark-matter tests enter a two-telescope pipeline.

## II. Biomedicine & Health

### 4. BICAN publishes a cross-species basal ganglia atlas of ~25.5 million brain cells (neuroscience)
**Summary:** On November 12, the NIH BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network (BICAN) released the most detailed multi-species cellular atlas of the basal ganglia yet, across roughly 26 papers in Cell Press journals: about 21.0 million human, 3.0 million macaque, and 1.5 million marmoset cells (25.5 million total) from more than 50 institutions in nine countries. The basal ganglia gate movement, habit, and reward and are linked to Parkinson’s, Huntington’s, addiction, and psychiatric disease; cross-primate comparison identified 61 cell types conserved over ~76 million years. The atlas integrates gene expression, epigenetic marks, electrical activity, and nuclear 3D folding, is openly shared, and is framed as the first major working model toward a whole-brain cell parts list.

**Commentary:** Turning the brain’s “behavioural gatekeeper” into a queryable cross-species parts list gives Parkinson’s and addiction research a shared cellular coordinate system.

### 5. Aravax: PVX108 Phase 2 hits primary endpoint; kids’ peanut tolerance rises 2.4-fold (food-allergy immunotherapy)
**Summary:** On November 12, Aravax reported Phase 2 topline results for peptide immunotherapy PVX108 (AVX-201, NCT05621317) in children: 14 US/Australian sites; primary efficacy cohort of 71 children aged 4–11, monthly intradermal dosing for 12 months. In the ITT analysis, the 50 nmol arm raised maximum tolerated peanut dose 2.4-fold from baseline (least-squares geometric mean) versus 1.2-fold on placebo; 39.4% tolerated a cumulative ≥443 mg peanut protein (~1.5–2 peanuts) versus 16.7% on placebo. No treatment-related allergic adverse events (including anaphylaxis) or treatment-related serious adverse events were reported; effects appeared maintained or improved six months off therapy (month 18). Results go to an ACAAI late-breaking oral; the candidate holds FDA Fast Track and remains investigational.

### 6. Therapeutic HPV vaccine VGX-3100 China Phase 3: 52.5% lesion regression plus viral clearance (cervical precancer)
**Summary:** Core China Phase 3 results for therapeutic DNA vaccine VGX-3100 (HPV-16/18), advanced by Beijing-based Orient Gene / Dongfanglue, were highlighted around November 12 as a SITC “late-breaking” oral. Led by the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, across 22 hospitals and 207 patients, 52.5% on VGX-3100 achieved histological regression of high-grade cervical lesions with concurrent HPV-16/18 clearance versus 35.3% on placebo (17.2-point gap, statistically significant); viral clearance was 55.4% versus 38.2%. The company says the week-36 primary analysis met its endpoint, follow-up runs to week 88, with a ~2028 filing goal and expansion toward vaginal, vulvar, and anal indications—aiming at a non-surgical option for younger patients who want to preserve fertility.

### 7. Salk’s light-controlled ASAP tool switches autophagy in seconds and pins nuclear-pore substrates (cell biology)
**Summary:** On November 12, Salk’s Christina Towers lab described ASAP (Autophagic SNARE for Abrupt Photomanipulation) in *Nature Cell Biology*: an optogenetic tool that rapidly, precisely, and reversibly blocks autophagy. Proteomic screens from 5 to 60 minutes after blockade confirmed known targets and revealed nuclear-pore-complex proteins as robust substrates—autophagy clears partially assembled pores while sparing intact ones—and showed condensate buildup secondary to pore-protein accumulation, linking protein synthesis hubs to protein breakdown. The team is taking ASAP into cancer metabolic rewiring and neuronal contexts; the piece notes optogenetics’ broader 2026 Nobel attention.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org / Salk — Light-controlled tool halts cellular recycling](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-tool-halts-cellular-recycling-disease.html)

**Commentary:** Decades of debate over what autophagy “eats” needed a reversible second-scale switch before substrate lists could become time-resolved facts.

### 9. MIT: optimized synthetic scenarios train climate emulators faster and with less data (climate AI)
**Summary:** On November 12, MIT’s Noelle Selin team reported in *PNAS* a method for climate emulators that reproduce Earth-system-model responses to human emissions in seconds. Conventional training needs six to eight “realistic” future scenarios run through full ESMs—costly and brittle on unfamiliar cases. Their algorithm needs roughly one full-model run, then iteratively builds synthetic emissions trajectories with high-frequency wiggles that maximize emulator information gain. Emulators trained on this smaller, optimized set predicted held-out scenarios that traditional emulators failed—including policy-relevant cases that cut one pollutant while others keep rising. The work is part of the BC3 project supported by Schmidt Sciences.

**Links:**

- [MIT News — Faster, trustworthy AI models for climate prediction](https://news.mit.edu/2026/new-research-could-lead-faster-trustworthy-ai-models-climate-prediction-1112)

**Commentary:** Training futures need not look realistic—they need to be the best probes of physical response so policy scenario factories can keep pace with decision clocks.

## IV. Basic Research & Materials Chemistry

### 11. KIT/Ulm: nickel electrodes in water electrolysis are active as nickel dioxide, not oxyhydroxide (catalysis)
**Summary:** On November 12, Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and Ulm University reported in *Nature Catalysis* that the active surface on nickel electrodes during alkaline oxygen evolution is nickel dioxide without hydrogen—not the long-assumed nickel oxyhydroxide. That forces a reassessment of previously postulated adsorption sites and reaction pathways. The authors stress that only a correct surface structure enables rational design of better electrodes, with foundational implications for green-hydrogen electrolyzer catalysts.

**Links:**

- [KIT — Decades-Old Assumption About Nickel Catalyst Disproven](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/202611-decades-old-assumption-about-nickel-catalyst-disproven.php)

**Commentary:** Drop one hydrogen from the catalyst textbook and the design map must be redrawn—green-hydrogen cost cuts start with knowing what the surface actually is.

### 12. Zhejiang / UT Austin: triaxial molecular fabric pulls 97.9% benzene in one pass (separation materials)
**Summary:** Feihe Huang and Guangfeng Li (Zhejiang University) with Jonathan Sessler (UT Austin) report in *Nature* (highlighted this week) a two-dimensional reticular triaxial woven polymer network (TWPN): C₃-symmetric macrocycles woven via B–N dative polymerization into four interlaced molecular nets with chirality and porosity. From a 50:50 benzene/cyclohexane mixture, the material adsorbs benzene to ~97.9% purity in one pass (control macrocycle ~60.5%), regenerates on heating, and works on related aromatic–aliphatic pairs. Benzene and cyclohexane have nearly identical boiling points, making distillation energy-intensive; plant-scale economics and continuous-process data are not yet published, but lab selectivity shows a clear application niche for molecular weaving.

### 13. Cornell: chip-delivered laser cooling cuts ion cool-down to ~150 microseconds (quantum computing)
**Summary:** On November 12, Cornell highlighted work published November 9 in *PRX Quantum*: specially shaped laser fields delivered through a microfabricated chip cool a trapped calcium ion via electromagnetically induced transparency to near the quantum ground state of the targeted motion in about 150 microseconds, versus ~500 microseconds with a conventional laser layout, while simultaneously cooling all three measured motional modes across ~5 MHz. Ions oscillate in three dimensions at different frequencies, so conventional methods often cool modes separately—a bottleneck for scaling trapped-ion quantum computers. Faster multi-mode cooling points to lower cooling overhead and higher compute duty cycle.

**Links:**

- [Cornell Chronicle — Faster laser cooling for quantum computers](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/11/researchers-develop-faster-laser-cooling-quantum-computers)

**Commentary:** In a quantum computer’s time budget, shrinking cooling from a half-millisecond tax toward the 100-microsecond class is what lets logic gates claim they are actually working.
