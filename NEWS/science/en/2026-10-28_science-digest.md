## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. NASA’s Lucy reaches the Jupiter Trojan asteroid region five years after launch (deep-space exploration)
**Summary:** NASA Science reported on Oct 28 that Lucy, having traveled more than 2.2 billion miles since its Oct 16, 2021 launch, is the first spacecraft to enter the Jupiter Trojan asteroid region. Mission engineers track its approach to Jupiter’s L4 Lagrange point via radio ranging and star-field imaging; L4 and L5 are “islands of stability” where Trojans share Jupiter’s orbit, averaging more than 6 million miles apart and about 484 million miles from the planet. Lucy’s first Trojan encounter is with Eurybates on Aug 11, 2027, followed by Polymele, Leucus, and Orus, with a later visit to the L5 binary Patroclus–Menoetius in March 2033. The craft and instruments continue to operate as designed more than five times farther from the Sun than Earth.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — NASA’s Lucy Reaches Trojan Asteroid Region 5 Years After Launch](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/lucy/2026/10/28/nasas-lucy-reaches-trojan-asteroid-region-5-years-after-launch/)

**Commentary:** Entering the Trojan region is a cruise milestone; hard tests of planet-formation theories still await the 2027-and-beyond close flybys.

### 2. Artemis III: SLS core stage joined with solid rocket boosters at Kennedy (crewed lunar prep)
**Summary:** NASA said on Oct 28 that the Artemis III Space Launch System (SLS) core stage was integrated with the twin solid rocket boosters inside High Bay 3 of the Vehicle Assembly Building at Kennedy Space Center. Technicians used a specialized lifting beam to place the 212-foot stage onto the mobile launcher from High Bay 2; after checkouts, the stack will roll to Pad 39B for a core-stage-only liquid hydrogen/oxygen tanking test and countdown practice. Artemis III will fly NASA astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, and Andre Douglas with ESA astronaut Luca Parmitano on Orion to rehearse rendezvous and docking with Blue Origin and SpaceX commercial crew vehicles ahead of later lunar landings.

## II. Biomedicine & Neuroscience

### 3. Nature: perinatal brain eliminates roughly one-quarter of cortical neurons with highly aberrant genomes (neurodevelopment)
**Summary:** Diane Shao of Boston Children’s Hospital with Peter J. Park and Christopher A. Walsh of Harvard Medical School and colleagues reported in Nature on Oct 28 that whole-genome sequencing of 2,357 single neurons from fetal to postnatal postmortem human brains found an average 25.9% (12.7–46%) of prenatal cortical neurons carrying widespread multi-chromosome copy-number variants (CNVs); that share fell about 4.5-fold after birth (Fisher meta-analysis P = 3.5 × 10⁻⁶). Neurons with widespread CNVs were mainly eliminated around birth, roughly parallel to programmed cell death, while surviving neurons were depleted for CNVs hitting dosage-sensitive and neurodevelopmental-disorder genes. snATAC/snRNA-seq of more than 44,000 nuclei linked high CNV burden to abnormal synaptic gene expression. Companion Nature news notes postnatal abnormality rates near 6%.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Perinatal elimination of genetically aberrant human cortical neurons](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11190-3)
- [Nature News — The brain liquidates billions of mutant neurons around the time we’re born](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03444-x)

**Commentary:** The work frames a developmental genome quality-control process; whether its failure drives autism or epilepsy still needs causal tests.

### 4. Largest Alzheimer’s GWAS to date: 127 risk loci and ~16% SNP heritability (neurogenetics)
**Summary:** An international multi-ancestry genome-wide association study led by Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, with University of Exeter’s PROTECT cohort among contributors, published in Nature on Oct 28. Analyzing 109,479 cases, 74,141 proxy cases, 2,131,799 controls, and 499,708 proxy controls, the team identified 127 Alzheimer’s risk loci (118 multi-ancestry plus 9 ancestry-specific), 48 of them new. SNP heritability reached about 16%—nearly threefold prior estimates—and genetic risk enriched in genes upregulated in microglia and downregulated in certain neuronal types. Summary statistics are released to aid target prioritization; authors argue common-variant contribution to AD has been substantially underestimated.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Alzheimer’s disease genetics converges on microglial and neuronal dysregulation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11206-y)

**Commentary:** The locus list and cell-type clues expand sharply; moving from association to druggable targets still requires functional and clinical work.

### 5. Rockefeller TrUDI: ~250× three-photon imaging yield enables ~2 mm cellular-resolution brain imaging (neuroimaging)
**Summary:** Alipasha Vaziri’s Rockefeller University team published Three-photon Ultra-Deep Imaging (TrUDI) in Nature Methods on Oct 28. By systematically matching laser, optics, and acquisition to safe exposure limits, TrUDI raises detected fluorescence photon yield about 250-fold at the same laser power as prior approaches, enabling cellular-resolution calcium imaging of mouse cortex and hippocampus within ~230 × 230 µm² fields at 8.6 Hz down to ~2 mm depth—beyond the prior practical ~1.2 mm ceiling. University coverage says hippocampal and striatal activity can be recorded without removing overlying cortex, with no evidence of inflammation, neuron loss, or DNA damage under the study’s conditions.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** Depth and safety advance together, shifting deep-circuit optics away from cortical resection toward intact-tissue imaging.

### 6. UCLA: millisecond nuclear “squeeze” lifts CRISPRa skin-to-neuron conversion from ~3% to ~40% (cell reprogramming)
**Summary:** Song Li’s UCLA Samueli team described on Oct 28 a Matter study in which a microfluidic device briefly squeezes mouse skin-cell nuclei for milliseconds, temporarily loosening packed chromatin so CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) can reach reprogramming genes. CRISPRa alone converted about 3% of cells into neurons; using the squeeze as a priming step with CRISPRa raised that share to about 40%. Authors frame it as a “last-mile” accessibility fix that could ease production of cells for disease modeling, drug testing, and personalized therapies; results so far are in mouse cells.

**Links:**

- [UCLA Samueli — A Quick Squeeze Helps CRISPR Turn Skin Cells Into Neurons More Efficiently](https://samueli.ucla.edu/a-quick-squeeze-helps-crispr-turn-skin-cells-into-neurons-more-efficiently/)

**Commentary:** Physical epigenetic priming sharply raises mouse yields; human cells and long-term genomic safety remain the next hurdles.

### 7. FDA issues draft BLA guidance for individualized genome editing; Palvella’s QTORIN wins Priority Review (regulation & rare disease)
**Summary:** On Oct 28 the U.S. FDA published draft guidance in the Federal Register on how individualized human genome-editing products that include multiple product variants can be filed in a single Biologics License Application, covering how to define an “individualized feature space,” add variants post-licensure, and handle potency, safety, manufacturing validation, labeling, and naming; comments are due by Nov 27, 2026. The same day, Palvella said FDA accepted and granted Priority Review to its NDA for QTORIN™ 3.9% rapamycin anhydrous gel for microcystic lymphatic malformations, with a PDUFA goal date of Feb 28, 2027. Phase 3 SELVA met all six prespecified efficacy endpoints (all p<0.001); if approved, it would be the first FDA-approved therapy for the indication.

## III. Climate, Energy & Environment

### 8. Oxford: bismuth sulfide’s pseudo-direct bandgap delays electron–hole recombination by ~75% at room temperature (photovoltaic physics)
**Summary:** Laura M. Herz and colleagues at the University of Oxford published an open-access Nature paper on Oct 28 showing that bismuth sulfide is a pseudo-direct semiconductor—pairing a lower-energy indirect transition with a direct transition a few kBT higher—which substantially retards bimolecular electron–hole recombination relative to classic direct-gap materials. Experiments find charges are lost more slowly upon cooling, opposite to most semiconductors; the team estimates roughly a 75% cut in room-temperature charge-loss rate versus equally strongly absorbing conventional materials. University coverage stresses this is a physical mechanism, not a finished cell; database screening or engineering could find analogs useful for photovoltaics and solar fuels.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Delayed electron–hole recombination in a pseudo-direct semiconductor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11223-x)
- [University of Oxford — Semiconductor breakthrough overcomes major challenge in capturing solar energy](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2026-10-28-semiconductor-breakthrough-overcomes-major-challenge-in-capturing-solar-energy)

**Commentary:** Sidestepping Kirchhoff/Planck constraints on strong absorbers is a conceptual leap; device efficiency and durability are not yet reported here.

### 9. UN report: climate change has cut the ocean’s carbon-uptake capacity by ~6%; calls for Ocean Carbon Intelligence (ocean climate)
**Summary:** UNEP’s 2026 Climate Technology Progress Report, issued Oct 28 with UNFCCC partners, states that the ocean absorbs about 30% of human CO₂ emissions and about 90% of excess heat, yet warming, acidification, pollution, and ecosystem degradation have already reduced its carbon-uptake capacity by roughly 6%, with further acceleration possible. The report advances an Ocean Carbon Intelligence framework linking observations, sensors, data infrastructure, and models, and reviews three technology classes: mature ocean monitoring, blue-carbon ecosystems (already sequestering about 407 million tonnes of CO₂ yearly), and still-uncertain marine CO₂ removal. Authors warn ocean-based approaches must complement—not replace—emissions cuts.

### 10. CAS team maps Qinghai–Tibet Plateau soil carbon thresholds: microbial carbon dominates at high elevation (carbon cycle)
**Summary:** Science and Technology Daily reported on Oct 28 that Mou Xiaoming and colleagues at the Northwest Institute of Eco-Environment and Resources, Chinese Academy of Sciences, published in Global and Planetary Change a biomarker study using lignin phenols and amino sugars across local and regional Qinghai–Tibet elevation transects. Absolute plant- and microbial-derived carbon both rise with elevation, but relative shares diverge: plant-sourced fractions fall and microbial-sourced fractions rise. Key thresholds appear near 2,415 m (local) and 3,650 m (regional)—plant carbon dominates below, microbial carbon above. Soil total nitrogen, moisture, and pH are primary controls. Authors say the pattern can reduce Earth-system-model bias in plateau carbon dynamics.

**Links:**

- [Science and Technology Daily — Research team reveals new Qinghai–Tibet Plateau carbon sequestration mechanism](https://www.stdaily.com/web/gdxw/2026-10/28/content_604231.html)

**Commentary:** Elevation thresholds clarify who stores carbon; whether warming shifts those thresholds and weakens the high-elevation sink needs time-series data.

## IV. Fundamental Research & Materials

### 11. Nature: five- and seven-membered carbon nanoribbons behave as covalent metals with near-ballistic conductance (carbon electronics)
**Summary:** Bing Wang, J. G. Hou and colleagues at the University of Science and Technology of China reported in Nature on Oct 28 a hierarchical on-surface synthesis of planar carbon pentaheptite nanoribbons (CPHNs) built only from five- and seven-membered rings, obtained by laterally fusing polypentalene chains derived from polycyclooctadiene precursors. CPHN 1 is an intrinsic metal; CPHN 2 is a narrow-gap semiconductor. CPHN 1 shows near-ballistic conductance approaching one conductance quantum, comparable to metallic carbon nanotubes. Authors see a bottom-up route to all-carbon electrodes and quantum wires, while noting further synthetic optimization is needed for device integration.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Narrow carbon pentaheptite nanoribbon as a covalent metal](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11202-2)
- [Nature Research Briefing — Ribbon made of carbon pentagons and heptagons has metallic properties](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03298-3)

**Commentary:** Atomically precise metallic non-benzenoid nanoribbons are now real; scalable interconnects still need synthesis yield and contact engineering.

### 12. Nature: compensated magnet Mn₅Si₃ injects spins that break time-reversal symmetry (spintronics)
**Summary:** An international team including Fèlix Casanova and Helena Reichlova reported in Nature on Oct 28 electrical spin injection from Mn₅Si₃—a compensated magnet with vanishing net magnetization—into a non-magnetic channel, detected at a remote ferromagnetic electrode in lateral spin valves. Switching between time-reversed magnetic states of Mn₅Si₃ reverses the spin signal across two bias geometries, a signature distinct from conventional ferromagnetic and spin-Hall injection. Symmetry analysis, first-principles calculations, and transport modeling support altermagnetic origin, with calculated spin polarizations of 6.5–10% and inferred spin diffusion lengths of 50–130 nm. Version of record: Oct 28, 2026.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Time-reversal-symmetry-breaking spin injection from a compensated magnet](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11227-7)

**Commentary:** Demonstrates controllable spin currents without net magnetization—an experimental foothold for low-stray-field spintronics.
