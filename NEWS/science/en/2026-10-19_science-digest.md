## I. Space and Deep Space

### 1. An H3 rocket lifts the MMX Phobos sample-return spacecraft (Spaceflight)
**Summary:** At 04:41:03 JST on October 20 (19:41 UTC on October 19), JAXA launched the Martian Moons eXploration spacecraft on H3 Flight 10 from the Yoshinobu Launch Complex at Tanegashima. Space.com described the flight as the rocket's 10th liftoff: the vehicle is about 63 meters tall, with two LE-9 engines and four solid boosters, and the early ascent, including paired booster separation within about two minutes and fairing separation, followed the planned path. The Sankei Shimbun filed before the planned separation and said that, if the flight stayed on schedule, MMX would be released around 05:52:15 JST at about 592 kilometers, after acceleration toward 11.15 kilometers per second. Jiji Press placed separation about 1 hour 11 minutes after liftoff. The spacecraft mass at launch is about 4.5 metric tons, development cost 55.3 billion yen, with two Phobos landings in 2029 aimed at collecting at least 10 grams and a capsule return to Australia in summer 2031. The launch had slipped about two years from 2024 after an H3 failure.

### 2. Euclid finds its first previously unknown comet (Deep space)
**Summary:** On October 19 the European Space Agency said the Euclid space telescope had found its first previously unknown comet, in images taken from September 16 to 24, 2026. The Minor Planet Center confirmed it on October 8 as C/2026 S1 (Euclid). University of Helsinki doctoral student Mikko Pontinen used software he wrote to flag a dusty streak in five fields, 16 exposures in all, in the Euclid Deep Field North, with no match in known-object catalogs. The comet is about 1.3 billion kilometers from Earth, a Sun-centered distance between Jupiter and Saturn, but well out of the planetary plane, on an orbit that will take thousands of years. ESA said this particular comet is not a suitable target for the future Comet Interceptor mission, and that Euclid had previously recorded only comets that were already known.

**Links:**

- [ESA — Euclid discovers new comet](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Euclid/Euclid_discovers_new_comet)

**Commentary:** The detection shows a deep-sky survey can catch a new comet far from the ecliptic, while the orbit still needs ground follow-up and Comet Interceptor did not gain a candidate.

## II. Biomedicine

### 3. CRISPR-Cas13 silences oncogenic fusion transcripts at the breakpoint in cell models (Cancer)
**Summary:** Mohamed Fareh, Wenxin Hu, and colleagues at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre report in Science Advances that PspCas13b can be programmed to recognize the breakpoint sequence created only when two genes fuse, and then degrade that transcript. The authors write that gene fusions are present in about 20 percent of cancers and are bona fide oncogenic drivers in 16.5 percent; BCR::ABL1 in chronic myeloid leukemia and EWSR1::FLI1 in more than 85 percent of Ewing sarcoma are examples. In the proof of concept, nine fusion transcripts, including drug-resistant BCR::ABL1, were silenced in laboratory cell models, with erythroid differentiation and apoptosis after BCR::ABL1 targeting. Medical Xpress noted on October 19 that the method has not been tested in animals or people, and delivery into tumors is the next question.

**Links:**

- [Science Advances — Silencing oncogenic fusion transcripts with CRISPR-Cas13b](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ady0296)
- [Medical Xpress — CRISPR approach targets gene fusions found in 1 in 5 cancers](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-10-crispr-approach-gene-fusions-cancers.html)

**Commentary:** Selectivity comes from a breakpoint that normal cells lack, and without an animal study this remains a programmable nuclease rather than a medicine that can be prescribed.

### 4. The FDA accepts the UGN-103 new drug application for recurrent bladder cancer (Drug review)
**Summary:** On October 19 UroGen said the U.S. Food and Drug Administration accepted the new drug application for UGN-103, a mitomycin intravesical solution, for adults with recurrent low-grade intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer. The Prescription Drug User Fee Act target date is June 14, 2027. The filing rests on the ongoing single-arm Phase 3 UTOPIA trial: 99 patients received 75 milligrams in the bladder once a week for six weeks in an outpatient setting. The complete-response rate at three months was 77.8 percent, and among those responders the Kaplan-Meier estimate of remaining event-free at six months was 94.5 percent. The company said the figures were consistent with the approved product ZUSDURI in the ENVISION trial. UGN-103 keeps the RTGel hydrogel and uses a lyophilized mitomycin formulation meant to simplify reconstitution.

**Links:**

- [GlobeNewswire — FDA accepts the UGN-103 new drug application](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/10/19/3383365/0/en/urogen-announces-fda-acceptance-of-the-new-drug-application-for-ugn-103-an-investigational-treatment-for-adults-with-recurrent-lg-ir-nmibc.html)

**Commentary:** Acceptance starts the review clock; a 77.8 percent complete-response rate from a trial with no placebo arm does not by itself show a gain over existing intravesical mitomycin.

## III. Climate and Environment

### 5. Daily weather raises the modeled glacier contribution to sea level by 5 to 10 percent (Glaciers)
**Summary:** Lander Van Tricht, Harry Zekollari, and colleagues at ETH Zurich and the Vrije Universiteit Brussel extended the Global Glacier Evolution Model to daily climate forcing and applied it to more than 12,500 glaciers representing 94 percent of global glacier ice volume. The work appears in Nature Communications and was reported by Phys.org on October 19. The daily model reproduces extreme melt years better than monthly forcing, including the European Alps in 2022 and 2026, western North America in 2023, Svalbard in 2024, and Central Asia in 2025. Projected through this century, the daily version gives a 5 to 10 percent larger glacier contribution to sea-level rise than the monthly version of the same model. The authors note that a heat wave of only a few days can strip protective snow and expose darker ice.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — Daily weather extremes reveal greater glacier ice loss](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-factoring-daily-weather-extremes-glacier.html)
- [Nature Communications — Short-term meteorological variability amplifies projected glacier loss](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78260-y)

**Commentary:** The 5 to 10 percent figure is relative to the monthly version of this one model, not a uniform upward revision of every existing sea-level assessment.

### 6. Glaciated upper Indus catchments are a net geological source of carbon dioxide (Carbon cycle)
**Summary:** A Nature India research highlight on October 19 describes work from the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Pune and colleagues, published in Chemical Geology. Sulfur and oxygen isotopes in upper Indus river water separate sulfate from gypsum dissolution and from pyrite oxidation. Sulfuric acid from that oxidation reacts with carbonate rock and returns carbon dioxide to the air, offsetting the sink from silicate weathering. The Hindu, on September 9, quoted the same team: in the upper Indus the release rate is about three times the carbon dioxide taken up by silicate weathering; steep mountain reaches are a net source, while flatter downstream floodplains remain a sink. The authors say this is a geological source on million-year timescales, strengthened where glaciers crush rock and expose pyrite. The Wadia Institute of Himalayan Geology and IIT Roorkee were partners.

**Links:**

- [Nature India — A hidden carbon dioxide source in the Himalaya](https://www.nature.com/articles/d44151-026-00201-x)
- [The Hindu — Rapid Himalayan erosion triggers a hidden source of CO2](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/energy-and-environment/rapid-himalayan-erosion-triggers-hidden-source-of-co2-study/article71437440.ece)

**Commentary:** The threefold figure is a weathering budget for a river basin, not an annual inventory of human emissions, and it does not convert directly into this year's rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide.

## IV. Basic Research and Energy

### 7. A distance-7 color code on Helios stores logical information with about five errors per million cycles (Quantum)
**Summary:** On October 19 Quantinuum reported error correction on its commercial trapped-ion computer Helios. A distance-7 color code reached 99.9995 percent logical fidelity per syndrome-extraction cycle, a failure probability of 5 times 10 to the minus 6, about ten times lower than the company's September record. Each step in code distance among 3, 5, and 7 cut logical memory errors by an average factor of about 12. A footnote says the statistics come from 29,000 shots of 20 rounds, about 580,000 rounds, in which three failures were observed. Two-qubit Clifford errors fell by a factor of about 9 when code distance increased from 3 to 5. From that scaling the company estimates that an error rate of 10 to the minus 12 and 100 logical qubits would need about 30,000 physical qubits on this path, against about 900,000 in the superconducting color-code comparison. The authors also note that below-threshold scaling of magic-state preparation has not yet been shown.

**Links:**

- [Quantinuum — Closing in on one million error-free operations](https://www.quantinuum.com/blog/quantinuum-closes-in-on-one-million-error-free-operations)
- [The Quantum Insider — A tenfold cut in Helios logical error rate](https://thequantuminsider.com/2026/10/19/quantinuum-reports-a-10x-reduction-in-logical-error-rate-on-helios/)

**Commentary:** Five errors per million cycles describes logical memory, not a million useful algorithm runs, and the figure of 30,000 physical qubits is an extrapolation from the measured scaling.

### 8. A reworked flavin enzyme uses oxygen to form carbon-phosphorus bonds and make glufosinate (Chemistry)
**Summary:** On October 19 the chemical and biomolecular engineering department at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign described work by Huimin Zhao and colleagues in Science, with a print date of October 8. Enzymatic carbon-phosphorus bond formation is extremely rare. Using semirational and machine-learning-guided directed evolution, the team altered the flavin-dependent enzyme OYE1 so that reactive oxygen species from molecular oxygen become phosphorus-centered radicals, followed by radical addition and enzymatic hydrogen-atom transfer. The reaction runs in light or in the dark. They tested more than 100 variants. The enzyme can make L-glufosinate, the active form of a broad-spectrum herbicide, in one step, and it accepts phosphites, phosphorous acid, and H-phosphinates. The U.S. Department of Energy Center for Advanced Bioenergy and Bioproducts Innovation supported the work. The paper does not report a scaled manufacturing yield.

**Links:**

- [Science — Asymmetric enzymatic hydrophosphorylation through oxygen activation](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aef3001)
- [Illinois CHBE — An engineered enzyme for L-glufosinate](https://chbe.illinois.edu/news/stories/Illinois-Researchers-Engineer-Enzyme-make-Valuable-Agricultural-Chemicals)

**Commentary:** Turning reactive oxygen from a source of enzyme damage into a bond-forming reagent extends the reaction type; making known L-glufosinate is not yet a process that replaces a chemical plant.

### 9. A physics score yields pigment-binding proteins from a single-digit number of designs (Proteins)
**Summary:** Nature Communications on October 19 published an open-access paper from Derek Woolfson and Thomas Oliver at the University of Bristol with the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Protein Design in Copenhagen and collaborators. After deep learning made de novo protein backbones fast to generate, they added a rapid physics-based method that builds isosteric, chemically complementary pockets for small-molecule ligands. Evaluating only a single-digit number of designs produced stable proteins whose binding sites were already formed in ligand-free crystal structures, with micromolar affinities or better for several synthetic and natural chromophores. A two-domain design carries two selective pigment sites and controlled energy transfer between them, and a smaller binder visualized subcellular structures in live mammalian cells. The structures used beamlines at Diamond Light Source and the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Chemical complementarity for ligand-binding protein design](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78764-7)

**Commentary:** Micromolar binding from a handful of designs shows the pocket can be computed before it is built, and the paper does not show nanomolar drug affinity or a function in animals.

### 10. A high-entropy perovskite holds an iridium catalyst through more than 3,000 hours of electrolysis (Hydrogen)
**Summary:** On October 19 Tohoku University described a collaboration with the Changchun Institute of Applied Chemistry and others, published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society. Proton-exchange-membrane electrolysis depends on scarce iridium for oxygen evolution, and the particles tend to clump and fade. The team embedded iridium in a high-entropy perovskite and heated it so the metal exsolved from inside the crystal and rooted on the surface, rather than being deposited on a support. In a PEM electrolyzer the system ran for more than 3,800 hours with very little performance loss, and for more than 3,200 hours under a harsher test. In a comparison, a conventional iridium catalyst degraded markedly within 300 hours, while the new catalyst stayed stable for more than 650 hours. Hao Li of Tohoku University is a corresponding author. The release does not give an iridium-cost figure for hydrogen produced at plant scale.

**Links:**

- [Tohoku University — Iridium catalysts for hydrogen production that last longer](https://www.tohoku.ac.jp/en/press/new_way_to_make_iridium_catalysts_for_hydrogen_production_last_longer.html)
- [Journal of the American Chemical Society — High-entropy perovskite-supported iridium catalysts](https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.6c13780)

**Commentary:** More than three thousand hours shows the support can hold iridium in place; electrolyzer lifetime and the price of green hydrogen still depend on the stack, the membrane, and the electricity, not only on these particles.

### 11. Cryo-electron microscopy freezes the early loading step of a cancer-drug scaffold enzyme (Structural biology)
**Summary:** Phys.org on October 19 reported that Ryo Tanifuji and Hiroki Oguri at the University of Tokyo, with Yoshikazu Tanaka at Tohoku University, used cryo-electron microscopy to see an early state of the bacterial enzyme SacC as it assembles the five-ring core of safracin B, a scaffold shared with trabectedin, a drug used for soft-tissue sarcoma. SacC carries out a seven-step cascade in one pass and forms two new carbon-carbon bonds. The enzyme swings widely during the reaction and clumps in solution. A covalent probe that mimics an unfinished intermediate parked it at the moment the building block is loaded, and a small partner protein, SacE, from the same genomic region kept the sample dispersed. The map reaches near-atomic detail, but the later ring-closing and product-release steps were not captured. The paper is in the Journal of the American Chemical Society.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — Visualizing how a cancer-drug scaffold is built](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-frozen-action-visualizing-cancer-drug.html)
- [Journal of the American Chemical Society — Cryo-EM structure of a safracin B synthetase](https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.6c09555)

**Commentary:** The structure is the first station of the assembly line, not a full route to trabectedin, and the later steps are still out of focus, so it does not yet rewrite a drug synthesis.

### 12. Hard X-ray attosecond pulses are focused to an intensity on the order of 10^20 watts per square centimeter (Physics)
**Summary:** Ichiro Inoue of the University of Tokyo and collaborators at the European XFEL report in Optica an experiment run in April 2026 at the MID instrument; SciTechDaily reviewed it on October 19. In a self-chirping mode, single-spike pulses had a typical bandwidth of about 6 electronvolts, corresponding to an estimated transform-limited duration of about 300 attoseconds. Average pulse energy at the focus was 45 microjoules, with shots up to about 100 microjoules. An advanced Kirkpatrick-Baez mirror pair from Japan, four reflections in all, concentrated the beam to a spot of 180 by 330 nanometers. The paper places the peak intensity on the order of 10^20 watts per square centimeter and checks that scale through saturation of amplified spontaneous emission from a copper foil. The authors note that chirp would make the true duration longer than the transform-limited estimate.

**Links:**

- [SciTechDaily — Record-breaking X-ray flashes in about 300 attoseconds](https://scitechdaily.com/researchers-unleash-record-breaking-x-ray-flashes-in-just-300-attoseconds/)
- [Optica — High-intensity hard X-ray attosecond pulses at European XFEL](https://doi.org/10.1364/OPTICA.613284)

**Commentary:** The intensity record rests on a near-transform-limited pulse duration, and it opens a window for nonlinear X-ray experiments rather than a method that already steers electrons inside a chosen chemical reaction.
