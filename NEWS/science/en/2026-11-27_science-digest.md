## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. Spaceflight｜BepiColombo software workaround after Mercury orbit insertion: 75-hour orbit can still be corrected; Mio separation on track
**Summary:** ESA–JAXA’s BepiColombo completed Mercury orbit insertion on Nov 21 UTC, but a software fault in attitude guidance and thruster control cut short the planned ~37-minute burn after only about 77% of the intended velocity change, putting the craft into safe mode and a ~75-hour orbit (versus ~58 hours planned). On Nov 27, SpaceNews and JAXA’s Mio team reported that flight controllers and Airbus have a workaround so future orbit-control burns can bypass the faulty logic. The spacecraft is healthy; a sequence of lowering maneuvers is expected to restore the trajectory. Separation of Mio (Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter) from ESA’s MPO remains planned for Dec 9/10.

## II. Biomedicine & Health

### 3. Anti-infectives｜MIT drug–antidote protein strategy rehabilitates toxic antimicrobials; clears mouse pneumonia while protecting the liver
**Summary:** An MIT team published in *Nature Communications* and released news on Nov 27 describing a two-part strategy to enlarge the antibiotic candidate pool: conjugate a toxic antimicrobial to a protein that delivers it to the infection site, and co-deliver an “antidote” protein that neutralizes free drug elsewhere. Using the cancer drug calicheamicin as a demonstration, the antidote is CalU19—a protective enzyme from the producing bacterium—engineered to bind albumin for circulation. In mouse pneumonia models with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* or *Staphylococcus aureus*, the combination cleared infection without signs of liver damage; lab tests also killed MRSA, *E. coli*, and *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. Senior author is Sangeeta Bhatia.

**Links:**

- [MIT News — Engineering antibiotics could expand the pool of new drug candidates](https://news.mit.edu/2026/engineering-antibiotics-could-expand-new-drug-candidates-pool-1127)

**Commentary:** Instead of digging a new well, this approach puts navigation and a safety net on molecules once discarded for toxicity—reopening the shelf in the resistance crisis.

**Summary:** A Nov 27 *Nature* research highlight covers a proof-of-concept in *Nature Chemical Biology* by Liang et al., who engineered a tobacco-related plant to synthesize the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel (Taxol), used against breast, lung, and other cancers. ### 5. Neuroscience｜Century-old Wernicke’s-area puzzle cracked with intracranial recordings: more than speech comprehension
**Summary:** A Nov 27 *Nature* highlight describes work by Silva et al. in *Science*: direct human intracranial recordings clarify that Wernicke’s area—long labeled for spoken-word comprehension—also makes a crucial contribution to a related cognitive task, addressing a roughly century-old debate. Implants trained on signals from the region can decode intended speech in some people with vocal-tract paralysis. The highlight stresses that the area’s job is far richer than the textbook tag “understands words.”

**Links:**

**Commentary:** A textbook language zone becomes a recordable computational role—and brain–computer interfaces gain another decodable cortical patch.

### 6. Developmental biology｜UCL world-first live imaging: how embryonic spines “zip” shut; CFL1 loss slows closure ~30%
**Summary:** Researchers at UCL’s Great Ormond Street Institute of Child Health announced on Nov 27 a *PLOS Biology* study with live imaging of the final stages of spinal-cord closure in mouse embryos—how cells on left and right sides reach across a gap, recognize one another, form stable bonds, and rearrange into a continuous skin layer. Without the protein CFL1, the “zipping” process slowed by about 30%; roughly one in three embryos developed spina bifida, and some had fatal brain malformations. Folic acid currently prevents about half of neural-tube defects; the team hopes the mechanism will inform options for cases folic acid does not cover.

**Links:**

- [UCL News — World-first images show how babies’ spines ‘zip’ together to prevent spina bifida](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2026/nov/world-first-images-show-how-babies-spines-zip-together-prevent-spina-bifida)

**Commentary:** Closure failure moves from epidemiology into frame-by-frame cell choreography—a new molecular handle for the prevention toolkit.

## III. Climate, Energy & Environment

### 8. Climate hydrology｜*Science*: record-warm 2023–2024 saw the largest synchronized drop in global runoff and surface water storage since 1991
**Summary:** A collaboration including Stockholm University and Yongqiang Zhang’s team at the CAS Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research reported in *Science* (coverage Nov 26–27) that assimilating streamflow from 150 major basins and satellite data for >1.2 million lakes and reservoirs shows global runoff and active surface-water storage fell about 5.72% and 9.50% versus the 1991–2020 mean in 2023–2024—the largest synchronized decline since 1991. South America accounted for ~70% of the global runoff loss; the Amazon saw roughly 40-year lows. Concurrent El Niño and unusually warm tropical North Atlantic waters drove land-side subsidence and moisture divergence. Extreme warming can trigger abrupt water loss even in humid regions.

**Links:**

- [Science — Abrupt surface water decline during 2023–2024 record warming](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aei2575)
- [IGSNRR CAS — Global surface-water plunge during record warming](https://igsnrr.cas.cn/news/kyjz/202611/t20261127_8317054.html)

**Commentary:** The intuition that “warming accelerates the water cycle so land gets more water” fails the data test—crisis planning must include nonlinear droughts in hot years.

### 9. Hydrogen｜*Nature Synthesis*: amorphous Ni@Mo-CoOx drives seawater electrolysis at 1 A·cm⁻² for over 9,000 hours
**Summary:** A Nov 27 *Nature Synthesis* paper reports amorphous Ni@Mo-CoOx that serves as both a Cl⁻-resistant protective layer and an effective hydrogen-evolution catalyst, stabilizing embedded particles and accelerating water dissociation. In natural seawater it sustains 1,000 mA·cm⁻² at 187 mV overpotential for about 9,000 hours. A noble-metal-free anion-exchange-membrane seawater electrolyzer with this cathode ran stably at 1.0 A·cm⁻² and 1.81 V (60°C) for 1,000 hours. Authors are affiliated with Soochow University, Beihang University, and collaborators.

**Links:**

- [Nature Synthesis — Amorphous electrocatalyst for seawater hydrogen over 9,000 h](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44160-026-01226-x)

**Commentary:** Seawater hydrogen’s bind is corrosion versus active-site access—making the protective layer itself the catalyst is rare engineering elegance.

### 10. Photovoltaics｜*Nature Energy*: phosphonate “molecular lock” on lithium ions yields 26.73% perovskite cells with thousand-hour-class stability
**Summary:** A Nov 27 *Nature Energy* study tackles instability in Spiro-OMeTAD hole-transport layers caused by hygroscopic lithium-salt additives and ion migration by designing phosphonate molecules with extended cyano chains that dual-site coordinate lithium ions, curb tBP loss, and stiffen the interface. Optimized n–i–p cells reach a champion efficiency of 26.73% and retain 95% of initial efficiency after 1,000 h under MPP tracking (ISOS-L-1) and at 85°C (ISOS-L-2), and 91% after 1,600 h under 1.5 V bias. The same day, HKUST researchers reported a related “trans-interface engineering” cell with 27.04% champion and ~26.67% certified efficiency.

**Links:**

## IV. Fundamental Research & Earth Science

### 11. Particle physics｜ATLAS first search for QCD instantons: 3.5σ excess of low-mass isotropic events—a possible hint of vacuum tunnelling
**Summary:** The ATLAS Collaboration released a Nov 27 briefing and preprint of the first LHC search for QCD instantons—non-perturbative tunnelling between topological vacuum sectors—using ~1.6 nb⁻¹ of very low-luminosity 13 TeV pp data from 2015. Neural nets pick spherical, particle-rich events; after data-driven minimum-bias background estimates, an excess of 3.5σ is observed. In the most sensitive region, ~32,789 events include a fitted ~3,900-event possible signal at 2.3±0.6 times the benchmark prediction (experimental uncertainty only). The collaboration stresses this is not a discovery; soft-QCD background modeling and heavy-flavor/mass characterization still need work.

**Links:**

- [ATLAS — Catching the vacuum in the act](https://atlas.cern/Updates/Briefing/Vacuum-Tunnelling)
- [arXiv — Search for low-mass isotropic signature of QCD instantons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22092)

**Commentary:** Half a century after ’t Hooft, vacuum tunnelling may be peeking into collider data—3.5σ warrants excitement and careful re-checks.

### 12. Seismology｜Fiber DAS: a magnitude-2 California quake ruptured in two places 42 ms apart, challenging the “small quake = simple circular rupture” model
**Summary:** Sangwoo Han (University of Washington), YoungHee Kim (Seoul National University), Jonathan Ajo-Franklin (Rice University) and colleagues report in *Science* (Vol. 394), with Rice/phys.org coverage on Nov 27, that distributed acoustic sensing on ~28 km of Imperial Valley Dark Fiber in California’s Brawley Seismic Zone captured 16 small quakes in about three hours. The largest (~M2) showed rupture arrest then dynamic renucleation ~150 m away, only 42 milliseconds later. Most small earthquakes have been modeled as simple circular ruptures; such millisecond detail was previously hard to resolve with conventional networks. The authors argue complex small ruptures can illuminate the physics of larger events.

**Links:**

- [phys.org — Fiber-optic sensing unearths surprising complexity in small California earthquake](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-fiber-optic-unearths-complexity-small.html)
- [Science — Millisecond-scale imaging of fragmented earthquake rupture](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aei0507)

**Commentary:** Fiber turns “unfelt” microquakes into millisecond fault cinema—the lab for large-earthquake physics may already run through underground telecom cables.
