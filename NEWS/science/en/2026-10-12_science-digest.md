## I. Space and Deep Space

### 1. A full-size twin of the Rosalind Franklin rover rehearses leaving its lander more than twenty times (Mars)
**Summary:** On October 12, ESA reported that Amalia, a full-size twin of the ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover, spent about two months at ALTEC in Turin rehearsing one of the mission's most critical steps: driving off the landing platform onto simulated Martian ground. Amalia completed the egress more than twenty times. The platform has a pair of ramps at the front and another at the back, and ground control must choose which side is safe. A ceiling rig carried about two-thirds of the rover's weight to mimic Martian gravity, about one-third of Earth's; the rover weighs 300 kg on Earth. A hydraulic platform then tilted the rover and lander by 20 degrees, and engineers placed obstacles under the ramps. The three-metre aluminium honeycomb ramps were built by Astronika in Poland. Thales Alenia Space, the industrial prime contractor, ran the campaign. ESA ties the operational experience to Rosalind Franklin's surface mission in 2030.

**Links:**

- [ESA — ExoMars rover twin rehearses first drive](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/ExoMars_rover_twin_rehearses_first_drive)

**Commentary:** More than twenty successful exits show that the ramps survived extreme cases; after a real landing there is still only one attempt, chosen from orbital images.

### 2. The UAE shows a half-scale asteroid-belt lander, with launch aimed at a March 2028 window (Asteroids)
**Summary:** Space published on October 12 an account of the Emirates Mission to the Asteroid Belt. A half-scale lander model was shown at an October 1 briefing in Abu Dhabi; the flight unit is to be flown to Colorado and integrated with the UAE Space Agency's MBR Explorer. The plan is a launch from Japan in a roughly three-week window opening in early March 2028, high-speed flybys of six asteroids, and a 2036 rendezvous with 269 Justitia, a roughly 50-kilometre-wide reddish body, after a cruise of about 5 billion kilometres. After about seven months in orbit, the lander would be released from about 250 kilometres, descend for two to three hours, and meet the surface at about 15–27 metres per second. The team expects an impact rather than a soft landing; if it survives, the battery is built for about 20–50 minutes. About 50 people at the Technology Innovation Institute designed and built the lander in just under two years, and it must cruise without commands from Earth or the main spacecraft. The mission director said a comparable launch opportunity would not return for at least a century. The cost was not disclosed.

**Links:**

- [Space — UAE unveils lander for a seven-asteroid mission set to launch in 2028](https://www.space.com/astronomy/asteroids/uae-unveils-lander-for-bold-7-asteroid-mission-set-to-launch-in-2028-photos)

**Commentary:** October 12 is the date of the English feature; the model was shown on October 1, and 15–27 metres per second means a planned high-speed contact, not a gentle landing.

## II. Biomedicine

### 3. Actinium-225 therapy RYZ101 meets the progression-free survival primary endpoint in a Phase 3 interim analysis (Oncology)
**Summary:** On October 12, Bristol Myers Squibb said the planned interim analysis of Phase 3 ACTION-1 (NCT05477576), run by its RayzeBio unit, met the primary endpoint. The trial compares RYZ101 (225Ac-DOTATATE) with investigator's choice of standard care in patients with somatostatin-receptor-positive gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors that progressed after lutetium-177 therapy. The control options are high-dose or high-frequency long-acting somatostatin analogues, everolimus, or sunitinib. The company said blinded independent central review found a statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in progression-free survival, with safety consistent with the known RYZ101 profile. It did not release a hazard ratio, median survival, or enrollment total. Participants already enrolled will be followed for overall survival. The FDA granted orphan-drug designation for GEP-NETs in August 2026. RayzeBio plans to discuss a submission with the FDA; detailed results are reserved for a future medical meeting. The company called these the first Phase 3 results for an actinium-225 targeted radiopharmaceutical in any indication.

**Links:**

- [Bristol Myers Squibb — ACTION-1 interim analysis of RYZ101 meets the PFS primary endpoint](https://news.bms.com/news/details/2026/Bristol-Myers-Squibb-Announces-Positive-Topline-Results-from-the-Interim-Analysis-of-Phase-3-ACTION-1-for-RYZ101-RayzeBios-Radiopharmaceutical-Therapy-in-Patients-with-GEP-NETs/default.aspx)

### 5. Extrachromosomal DNA trapped in micronuclei limits tumor-cell fitness and tracks with better neuroblastoma survival (Cancer genomes)
**Summary:** Nature Genetics published an open-access paper on October 12, with corresponding authors including Anton G. Henssen, Paul S. Mischel, and Howard Y. Chang. Damaged extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), especially under replication stress, is a major source of micronuclei. Replication stress promotes mitotic clustering, asymmetric inheritance, mis-segregation into micronuclei, and lower transcriptional output. Sequencing of single micronuclei shows that several distinct ecDNA species can coalesce in one micronucleus. Cells that inherit ecDNA-positive micronuclei proliferate less well and are more likely to die. In patients with MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma, a higher frequency of ecDNA-positive micronuclei at diagnosis is associated with better overall survival. The authors treat this mis-segregation as a brake on tumor fitness and mitotic retention of ecDNA as a possible therapeutic vulnerability. The abstract does not give a hazard ratio or survival times.

**Links:**

- [Nature Genetics — Micronuclear ecDNA constrains tumor cell fitness](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-026-02782-w)

**Commentary:** The survival link is the frequency of these micronuclei at diagnosis, not a demonstration that forcing ecDNA into micronuclei lengthens patients' lives.

### 6. A CCG repeat in the FAM193B promoter tracks with Alzheimer risk in a Chinese cohort (Neurogenetics)
**Summary:** Nature Neuroscience published an open-access paper on October 12. Using long-read sequencing (n = 11) and short-read sequencing (n = 3,190) from a Chinese cohort, the authors argue that common structural variants contribute to Alzheimer heritability beyond common single-nucleotide polymorphisms. An expanded CCG repeat in the FAM193B promoter reaches genome-wide significance: odds ratio 2.20 per 10-repeat increase, P = 5.5×10⁻¹³. The expansion increases FAM193B expression and mediates a pleiotropic link between brain expression of the gene and disease risk. Analyses of postmortem brain and cultured neurons associate higher neuronal FAM193B expression with DNA-damage and cell-death pathways.

**Links:**

- [Nature Neuroscience — A FAM193B CCG repeat associated with Alzheimer risk](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02481-z)

**Commentary:** The 2.20 figure is an odds ratio per ten extra repeats, and the long-read sample is 11 people; the repeat is a regulatory risk factor, not a ready diagnostic test.

## III. Climate and Environment

### 7. An Earth's Future review says climate-fingerprint evidence is stronger than it was in 2009 (Attribution)
**Summary:** On October 12, the American Geophysical Union announced a review led by Benjamin Santer of the University of East Anglia, with Susan Solomon of MIT, David W. J. Thompson of Colorado State University and UEA, Qiang Fu of the University of Washington, and Gabriele C. Hegerl of the University of Edinburgh. It was published the same day in Earth's Future. The paper traces more than thirty years of fingerprint studies that separate human and natural influences by pattern and timing, and it answers what the authors call a deeply flawed July 2025 U.S. Department of Energy report. They argue that the science behind the U.S. EPA's 2009 Endangerment Finding was already strong and is stronger now, and that the years since 2009 include the ten warmest years in the instrumental surface record. The review focuses on annual, decadal, and seasonal changes and also discusses extremes. Santer called the evidence incontrovertible. The paper does not release a new global temperature detection.

**Links:**

- [AGU — Scientists reaffirm evidence of human influence on climate](https://news.agu.org/press-release/scientists-reaffirm-incontrovertible-evidence-of-human-influence-on-climate/)
- [Earth's Future — Fingerprint evidence after the 2009 Endangerment Finding](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2026EF009129)

**Commentary:** This is a reassessment of existing fingerprint studies aimed at a government report, not a new warming rate calculated today.

### 8. A Science assessment maps sea-level exposure for 34,120 terrestrial vertebrates (Biodiversity)
**Summary:** Liang Ma, Yaping Chen, and colleagues at Sun Yat-sen University, with collaborators, assess future sea-level rise for 34,120 terrestrial vertebrate species in Science. The journal page is timestamped October 8; the university's newsroom dates the Beijing-time release to October 9, and Science and Technology Daily carried the story on October 12. The paper overlays relative sea-level projections for five Shared Socioeconomic Pathways on area-of-habitat maps. Species exposed to inundation rise from up to 13,680 in 2050 to 15,324 by 2100, about 45% of the species studied, including 57–63% of birds, 47–52% of mammals, 28–36% of reptiles, and 20–24% of amphibians. Under SSP5-8.5, species losing more than 10% of habitat rise from 18 in 2050 to 109 in 2100. More than 96% of potential refugia lie outside existing protected areas. The Chinese report also cites the World Meteorological Organization's rate of 4.7 millimetres per year for 2015–2024. The paper stresses that exposure is not the same as losing most habitat or going extinct.

**Links:**

- [Science — Safeguarding terrestrial vertebrates from future sea-level rise](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aed5772)
- [CNR — Science and Technology Daily on habitat inundation for terrestrial vertebrates](https://tech.cnr.cn/gstj/20261012/t20261012_527839444.shtml)

**Commentary:** 15,324 is the upper count of exposed species in 2100; 109 is the high-emissions count of species losing more than a tenth of their habitat, and the two are not an extinction list.

## IV. Energy and Optoelectronics

### 9. Hyperuniform disordered textures are built into ultrathin crystalline-silicon cells to trap light (Photovoltaics)
**Summary:** On October 12, AMOLF described work from its 3D Photovoltaics group that places hyperuniform-disorder nanostructures on ultrathin crystalline-silicon solar cells. The paper appeared the same day in ACS Photonics. The institute says the material is about 30 times thinner than conventional silicon. Up close the pattern looks random; at a larger scale it has no clumps, gaps, or repeating lattice. Inverse design steers light of different colors into the cell. Cells were fabricated at Stanford, designs were made at Southampton, and patterning plus optical and device analysis was done in Amsterdam, with the University of Amsterdam also collaborating. The news post does not give a power-conversion efficiency.

### 10. A Tsinghua–Cambridge deep-blue OLED reaches 35.9% external quantum efficiency and keeps 90% of it at high current (Displays)
**Summary:** Nature Photonics published on October 12 a paper from Dongdong Zhang and Lian Duan at Tsinghua University with Hugo Bronstein, Richard Friend, and colleagues at Cambridge. A dual-sensitizer stack uses nearly all electrically generated excitons on a submicrosecond timescale. The sensitizing host is a blue thermally activated delayed-fluorescence molecule with a C3-symmetric triple donor–acceptor motif. The devices emit deep blue with a chromaticity y-coordinate of 0.10 and a full width at half maximum of 15.4 nanometres. Peak external quantum efficiency is 35.9%, and 90% of that value remains at 297.0 mA/cm². After 632 hours, luminance falls to 80% of an initial 1,000 cd/m². The authors attribute the high-current stability to multiple sensitization paths that suppress exciton annihilation.

**Links:**

- [Nature Photonics — Stable deep-blue OLEDs with reduced efficiency roll-off](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41566-026-02029-4)

**Commentary:** 35.9% and 632 hours are laboratory-device figures; the paper does not show that y = 0.10 deep blue can be manufactured at the same lifetime.

## V. Basic Research

### 11. Up to 100 qubits on an IBM Heron processor are measured and reset repeatedly (Quantum computing)
**Summary:** On October 12, Rutgers University, via Phys.org, described a Nature Physics experiment in which Bibek Pokharel and colleagues used IBM's 156-qubit Quantum Heron processor. On a connected chain of up to 100 qubits, operations that scramble information among neighbors alternated with measurements and resets. The team ran nearly 5,000 two-qubit operations and nearly 5,000 checks and resets. When scrambling dominated, the system stayed chaotic; when checks and resets were more frequent, the qubits moved toward an orderly state the researchers had chosen. Near a 50–50 balance, the system switched abruptly, a phase transition. Jedediah Pixley and IBM's Maika Takita present this as real-time feedback on the way to fault tolerance, not a fault-tolerant computer. The scale exceeds the roughly two dozen qubits they could simulate classically. Coauthors include groups at the City University of New York, Iowa State University, Ames National Laboratory, Pennsylvania State University, and Louisiana State University.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — A quantum computer checks and resets itself while running](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-closer-quantum.html)
- [Nature Physics — Order from chaos with adaptive circuits on quantum hardware](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03470-6)

**Commentary:** Nearly 5,000 resets show that the hardware can intervene while a circuit is running; continuous correction across a full computation is still a different scale and error set.

### 12. Bacterial defense system DRT10 adds tandem DNA repeats in a telomerase-like way (Microbial immunity)
**Summary:** Nature published on October 12 a paper from Samuel H. Sternberg and Stephen Tang at Columbia University, with collaborators at the University of Copenhagen and the University of the Basque Country, describing the bacterial antiviral system DRT10. Bound to a noncoding RNA, it carries out processive, protein-primed synthesis of tandem-repeat DNA. The template boundaries and a characteristic sequence are conserved and required. Cryo-electron microscopy shows two reverse-transcriptase monomers on opposite sides of a pseudo-symmetric noncoding RNA, a 2:1 architecture. Each monomer transcribes the template on its own side, but only one makes the long repetitive product, whose template is set by the distance between two flanking stem-loop anchors. Phylogenetic analysis places eukaryotic telomerase in a monophyletic clade with the DRT family, suggesting a shared origin for the polymerization activity.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Antiviral immunity via telomerase-like reverse transcription in bacteria](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11250-8)

**Commentary:** The parallel with telomerase is the repeat-addition mechanism and the phylogeny, not a claim that bacteria have eukaryotic telomeres.

### 13. High-speed video shows a honey-bee stinger pumping venom with two collapsible pistons (Biomechanics)
**Summary:** On October 12, Macquarie University described a PNAS study in which Fiorella Ramirez-Esquivel and colleagues at the University of New South Wales filmed a detached western honey-bee stinger pumping venom on its own. The roughly 2-millimetre stinger kept working deeper into a clear skin-mimic gel and injecting venom. Video at 300 and 100 frames per second shows two valves inside the bulb opening like parachutes on the downstroke to seal and push venom out, then collapsing on the upstroke and deforming so they can pass each other, which keeps the flow continuous while the valves move in opposite directions. Micro-CT scans were used to 3D-print a larger working model of the bulb and lancets to check the pumping cycle. The authors suggest the collapsible double piston could inspire microfluidic and minimally invasive drug pumps. The DOI is 10.1073/pnas.2603916123.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — High-speed video of a honey bee stinger pumping venom](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-high-video-reveals-honey-bee.html)
- [PNAS — The honey bee stinger as a pump with collapsible double pistons](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2603916123)

**Commentary:** Continuous venom flow comes from the geometry of the two valves; the enlarged model checks the cycle, and it is not yet a medical pump.
