## I. Spaceflight and Deep Space

### 1. Crew-12 splashes down off Los Angeles at 11:34 a.m. EDT aboard Dragon
**Summary:** A NASA release on October 8 states that the SpaceX Crew Dragon Freedom splashed down at 8:34 a.m. PDT in the Pacific off Los Angeles with NASA astronauts Jessica Meir and Jack Hathaway, ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot, and Roscosmos cosmonaut Andrey Fedyaev. The same release gives the mission totals: 237 days in orbit, more than 100 million miles, and more than 3,792 orbits. Crew-12 lifted off at 5:15 a.m. EST on February 13 and docked the next day. The flight was the second for Meir and Fedyaev and the first for Hathaway and Adenot. The release says Meir completed four spacewalks, bringing her career total to seven and placing her third among NASA women, behind Peggy Whitson and Suni Williams. Adenot completed three spacewalks and became the first French woman to go outside the station. ESA the same day confirmed splashdown at 15:34 GMT and said Adenot spent 237 days, 5 hours, and 18 minutes in space, more than 35 days longer than the single-flight record ESA astronaut Luca Parmitano set on Beyond. Her three spacewalks were done in 14 days and totaled 19 hours and 42 minutes. Station work included stem-cell production, on-demand IV fluid, pneumonia-related bacteria and long-term heart damage, nutrition, and blood flow. NASA set the crew science briefing for 3:30 p.m. EDT on October 15 at Johnson Space Center in Houston. Adenot will return to Europe and will not attend. A station log adds that Meir has logged 440 days in space, placing her in NASA’s top 10 for cumulative time.

### 2. NASA and the U.S. Energy Department sign a space nuclear power and propulsion memorandum
**Summary:** NASA release 26-084 on October 8 says Administrator Jared Isaacman and Energy Secretary Chris Wright signed the memorandum “Accelerating American Leadership in Space Nuclear Power and Propulsion” at a summit hosted by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy. The text covers cooperation from research and fuel production through testing, launch integration, and operations, and it describes safety as the requirement running through each step. The agreement takes effect Sunday, November 1. The release cites a December 2025 presidential executive order directing NASA to develop a launch-ready lunar surface reactor by 2030. The same release states that Space Reactor-1 Freedom launches in 2028 and opens the way for Lunar Reactor-1, described as the fission surface power system that would sustain a future Moon base through the lunar night. It also says the Dragonfly rotorcraft to Titan, scheduled for launch in 2028, will use a Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator and 24 lightweight radioisotope heater units, and that NASA and DOE plan to provide 24 similar heater units for ESA’s Rosalind Franklin Mars rover.

## II. Astronomy and the Heliosphere

### 3. MeerKAT and Webb place FRB 20240304B in a dwarf galaxy at redshift 2.148
**Summary:** The South African Radio Astronomy Observatory reported on October 8 that a paper published that day in Science gives the most distant localized fast radio burst yet, FRB 20240304B. The MeerTRAP team detected the flash, intrinsically about one millisecond long, with MeerKAT on March 4, 2024, and localized it, but the largest ground-based telescopes could not see a host. Webb’s near-infrared camera found a galaxy at the position, and its spectrograph measured redshift 2.148, when the universe was about 3 billion years old. The observatory said this more than doubles the previous distance record, and that the signal traveled more than 10 billion years to reach Earth. Lead author Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney said the expected massive star-forming galaxy was absent; the host is a small dwarf that is still forming stars and about 1,000 times less massive than expected. The observatory wrote that most of the galaxy’s stars may have formed within about 30 million years. Neutron-star mergers typically take at least about a billion years, so this burst fits an event such as a starquake on a young magnetar better than a merger. A University of California, Santa Cruz release adds that a blank Keck image was used to show the host was too faint for the ground, and that the sightline also crosses a previously unknown cluster at redshift 0.3 and the Virgo Cluster.

**Links:**

- [SARAO — MeerKAT combines with Webb to pinpoint the most distant cosmic Fast Radio Burst](https://www.sarao.ac.za/news/south-africas-meerkat-combines-with-nasas-webb-to-pinpoint-the-most-distant-cosmic-fast-radio-burst/)
- [Science — A fast radio burst at redshift 2, three billion years after the Big Bang](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adz2675)

**Commentary:** A small, young host pushes a billion-year merger off the explanation for this one source; a magnetar remains an inference, not an image of the burst itself.

### 4. Solar Orbiter at 0.55 AU reads a coronal-loop ion signature inside a magnetic switchback
**Summary:** Nature Astronomy on October 8 published a paper by Jesse T. Coburn, Stephanie L. Yardley, and colleagues. Magnetic field lines in the solar wind sometimes fold back sharply in structures called switchbacks. Spacecraft see them often near the Sun, and how they form is still open. Using heavy-ion measurements from ESA and NASA’s Solar Orbiter at 0.55 astronomical units, the authors report elevated oxygen and carbon charge-state ratios and a depletion of helium relative to protons, indicating that plasma in this switchback came from hot, closed coronal loops. A semi-empirical coronal model constrained by Solar Dynamics Observatory electron temperature and density traces the ion signatures to a coronal-hole boundary or to loops inside the hole, which points to interchange reconnection between closed loops and open field. Proton velocity and magnetic-field fluctuations match an outward-propagating Alfvén wave. The paper concludes that interchange reconnection governs origin and waves or turbulence govern propagation, so the switchback still carries a measurable trace of coronal heating and solar-wind acceleration.

**Links:**

- [Nature Astronomy — On the coronal origin of magnetic switchbacks in the solar wind](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02928-0)

**Commentary:** The ion evidence is for the plasma inside one switchback, not a claim that every switchback comes from the same coronal structure.

## III. Developmental Biology and Medicine

### 5. Two groups write mouse-embryo cell divisions into the genome with prime editing
**Summary:** A Nature news story on October 8 reviewed two independent papers published that day in Science and Cell. Both use prime editing to insert heritable marks as cells divide, then reconstruct lineage by sequencing. A release from the Seattle Hub for Synthetic Biology, which includes the Allen Institute, UW Medicine, and Biohub, says Jay Shendure’s DNA Typewriter was injected into one fertilized egg and the embryo developed for 13.5 days. The team read marks in 1.58 million nuclei and reconstructed how 1.28 million cells arose from the first division into tissues and organs, about 10 percent of the cells in a two-week-old mouse embryo. The two lineages from that first division contributed about 57 percent and 42 percent of cells, but the same proportion of every cell type. A Howard Hughes Medical Institute release the same day says Jonathan Weissman’s group at the Whitehead Institute used PEtracer to write marks at more than a hundred genomic sites, injected marked stem cells into an embryo, and after development in utero read both cell type and ancestry. Both releases frame the work as proof that a mammalian cell family tree can be recorded. The complete map John Sulston drew in 1983 was still of a nematode.

**Links:**

- [Nature — How to build a mouse: embryo development captured in stunning detail](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03193-x)
- [EurekAlert! — From a single cell to 1.2 million](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1146881)

**Commentary:** The public reconstruction covers about a tenth of the cells in one late embryo, plus a separate set of embryos from gastrulation into early organogenesis, not a Sulston map of every cell in every mouse.

### 6. Multiomics and records from more than 111,000 people with mild cognitive impairment nominate Alzheimer’s drug targets
**Summary:** Nature Neuroscience published an open-access paper on October 8 by Yuan Hou, Feixiong Cheng, and colleagues. Mendelian randomization across Alzheimer’s genome-wide association studies and functional multiomics nominated 19 druggable targets in people of European ancestry and 7 in people of African ancestry. One is a protective missense variant in epoxide hydrolase 2, EPHX2 p.Arg287Gln. In neurons and cerebral organoids made from patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells, the variant lowered EPHX2 and phosphorylated tau (pTau-181). Pharmacological inhibition of EPHX2 improved cognitive performance in 5xFAD mice. Using electronic health records from 111,680 patients with mild cognitive impairment, the authors found 12 drugs associated with lower Alzheimer’s incidence, including trazodone (ADRA1A) and baclofen (GABBR1). The paper says the screen covered 1,229 druggable targets with U.S.-approved drugs or molecules already under investigation.

**Links:**

- [Nature Neuroscience — Alzheimer’s disease target and drug discovery by leveraging multiomics and electronic health data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02472-0)

**Commentary:** The health-record associations and the mouse cognition result are target and repurposing leads, not a completed Alzheimer’s drug approval.

### 7. Australia clears a Phase 1/2 trial of EDIT-401 in heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia
**Summary:** Editas Medicine said on October 8 that the Phase 1/2 Strive trial of EDIT-401 has Human Research Ethics Committee approval in Australia and has completed Clinical Trial Notification with the Therapeutic Goods Administration, so dosing can start in people with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia who still need further LDL-cholesterol lowering. A New Zealand review has been submitted; if it is approved, patients in both countries can enroll. The company says EDIT-401 edits the LDLR gene in vivo to raise LDLR protein and lower LDL-C. In nonhuman primates, mean reductions in LDL-C, lipoprotein(a), and apolipoprotein B were about 90 percent or greater, and a separate preclinical data set covering about 10 months is scheduled as an oral presentation at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions on November 8. Part 1 is an open-label single-ascending-dose study; Part 2 is a single-dose randomized placebo-controlled expansion. Five sites were selected across Australia and New Zealand. The company expects dosing to begin in 2026, initial safety and tolerability data in the first quarter of 2027, and topline safety and efficacy after Part 1 enrollment later in 2027.

### 8. NVC-001 doses a first patient and receives FDA Fast Track designation
**Summary:** Nuevocor announced on October 8 that the first patient has been dosed in SUNBEAM-LMNA, a Phase 1/2 trial of the investigational gene therapy NVC-001 for LMNA dilated cardiomyopathy, and that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has granted Fast Track designation. The company says no approved disease-modifying therapy exists for this inherited cardiomyopathy. The trial, NCT07606274, is multicenter, open-label, and single-ascending-dose, testing one intravenous infusion at three dose levels. The primary objective is safety and tolerability. NVC-001 is a one-time AAV9 therapy encoding a dominant-negative form of SUN1, intended to reduce force transmitted to the cardiomyocyte nucleus and restore nuclear integrity. The company says preclinical studies showed improvements in survival and cardiac function. The principal investigator is Evan Kransdorf of the Smidt Heart Institute at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles.

## IV. Climate and Carbon

**Summary:** The U.S. Climate Prediction Center issued its El Niño/Southern Oscillation diagnostic discussion on October 8, with the alert status set to El Niño Advisory. The synopsis says El Niño strengthened further last month and that a strong-to-very-strong El Niño is likely through January–March 2027, with the chance remaining greater than 83 percent. Sea-surface temperature anomalies exceeded +4.0°C in parts of the eastern equatorial Pacific. Niño-3.4 was +2.1°C, Niño-3 was +3.0°C, and Niño-1+2 was +3.9°C. The equatorial subsurface temperature index was well above average, and anomalies at depth in the eastern Pacific remained above +10.0°C. The official outlook says El Niño will keep strengthening through the end of the year. The discussion gives the chance that a three-month Relative Oceanic Niño Index reaches +2.5°C or more, which would exceed the strength of events since 1950: 54 percent for September–November, 83 percent for October–December, and 70 percent for November–January. It says impacts consistent with El Niño are more likely at that magnitude, though not guaranteed. The next discussion is scheduled for November 12.

### 10. A Tianjin all-organic membrane loads single-crystal COF to 75.5 percent by volume
**Summary:** Nature Energy published online on October 7 a paper by Guangwei He, Zhongyi Jiang, and colleagues at Tianjin University, and the university reported it on October 8. The paper uses a weak-interaction inlaying strategy to embed single-crystal covalent organic frameworks in a continuous polymer at loadings up to 75.5 percent by volume while keeping the filler evenly dispersed. The membranes reach a CO2 permeability of 74,800 Barrer with a CO2/N2 selectivity of about 20. Polyimide-based membranes retain about 85 percent of their initial permeability after six months of aging at room temperature. The university release adds that, in simulated flue gas, permeability reached about 70 percent of the value a model predicts for a pure COF membrane, and that performance held with water, sulfur oxides, and nitrogen oxides. A preliminary techno-economic analysis under stated process and cost assumptions put capture at about 38 U.S. dollars per tonne of CO2.
