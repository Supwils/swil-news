### 2. NEO Surveyor: sunshade solar arrays are integrated, with up to about 2 kilowatts (Planetary defense)
**Summary:** NASA’s Science Mission Directorate reported on October 26 that the sunshade of the Near-Earth Object Surveyor, the spacecraft’s largest structure and the shield that blocks sunlight and keeps the infrared telescope cold, now has its solar arrays integrated. The structure is 20 feet (6 meters) tall and 8 feet (2.4 meters) wide at the base, with two flat panels joined at 23.5 degrees. Earlier this year it was covered with 864 interconnected solar cells that can produce up to about 2 kilowatts to charge the onboard batteries. The telescope’s 20-inch (50-centimeter) aperture feeds two infrared detector arrays, each making a 16-megapixel image, so two bands can be used to estimate an asteroid’s or comet’s temperature and size. Applied Aerospace in California built the structure, SolAero in New Mexico installed the cells in January, thermal-vacuum testing finished in March, and the hardware reached BAE Systems in Colorado ahead of schedule in May, where coarse Sun sensors, temperature sensors, and array functional tests were then completed.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — Sunshade for NASA’s NEO Surveyor mission ready for its day in the Sun](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/neo-surveyor/2026/10/26/sunshade-for-nasas-neo-surveyor-mission-ready-for-its-day-in-sun/)

**Commentary:** The update confirms ground integration and power capacity; launch timing and on-orbit detection sensitivity are not in this log.

## II. Biomedicine

### 4. MIT’s LUCID: a 24-transducer ultrasound implant spreads an MRI contrast agent farther in the mouse brain (Medical engineering)
**Summary:** The MIT News Office reported on October 26 that Canan Dagdeviren’s lab published LUCID, a long-term ultrasound-enhanced convective injection device, in Device. The cardstock-thin flexible implant prints a drug cannula directly onto 24 miniature ultrasound transducers, holds about 100 microliters, infuses with an osmotic pump, sets ultrasound dose over Bluetooth, and uses a wirelessly recharged battery. A little more than 10,000 glioblastoma cases are diagnosed in the United States each year, and average survival after diagnosis is 12 to 15 months. In a tissue-mimicking gel and in mice, molecules or an MRI contrast agent spread farther when ultrasound was on than with infusion alone. Graduate student Sarah Ornellas and Jason Hou are the lead authors. Funding came from the MIT Media Lab Consortium and the National Institutes of Health. If used in patients, the cannula and array would sit inside the skull and the battery and electronics outside it.

**Links:**

- [MIT News — Implantable device uses ultrasound to improve drug delivery to the brain](https://news.mit.edu/2026/implantable-device-uses-ultrasound-improve-drug-delivery-brain-1026)

**Commentary:** The mouse comparison is how far a contrast agent spreads; the release does not give a millimeter-scale gain or any antitumor efficacy.

### 5. University of Pennsylvania: raising the backup protein utrophin increases force in a mouse model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Gene therapy)
**Summary:** The Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania said on October 26 that Tejvir S. Khurana and colleagues report a CRISPR approach in Molecular Therapy Advances. A muscle-directed MyoAAV vector and lipid nanoparticles of the kind used for COVID mRNA vaccines deliver the editing cargo and raise utrophin, a stand-in for the dystrophin that is missing in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The university said the disease affects roughly one in 5,000 children and typically leads to death in the patient’s twenties. Treated mouse models showed increased muscle force, better resistance to contraction, and fewer of certain diseased fibers. The experiments were designed to test prevention of progression. Khurana said that if enough nuclei in a damaged muscle cell are edited, the newly expressed protein would not in theory stay confined to those nuclei. The Muscular Dystrophy Association provided partial support. Drew Weissman and Michael Mitchell contributed the mRNA and lipid-nanoparticle delivery.

**Links:**

- [Penn Medicine — Stopping muscular dystrophy with a backup protein](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/stopping-muscular-dystrophy-with-a-backup-protein)

**Commentary:** The university news reports the direction of change in mouse force and diseased fibers, without a fold increase for utrophin or an editing efficiency.

### 6. UC San Diego: marine-derived rutamycin B reaches full effect against Naegleria fowleri within four hours (Antiparasitic)
**Summary:** The University of California San Diego reported on October 26 that Anjan Debnath, William Fenical, Hayley Fong, and colleagues describe rutamycin B, isolated from a marine bacterium, in Marine Drugs. At low concentrations it is active against multiple clinical strains of Naegleria fowleri. Primary amoebic meningoencephalitis has a fatality rate above 97 percent. The compound reaches full potency in about four hours, compared with 16 hours for the standard drug amphotericin B, and it is 200 to 300 times more lethal to the parasite than to human cells by shutting down an enzyme the parasite uses to make energy. Combined with standard treatment, 3 to 10 percent of the original rutamycin B dose still produced the same result. The team says the next studies will test blood-brain barrier crossing and live disease models. Partial funding is NIH R21EY034294 and R21AI171538. A PCT application was filed on March 12, 2026, number PCT/US26/18919.

**Links:**

- [UC San Diego — Marine molecule may help combat brain-eating amoeba](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/marine-molecule-may-help-combat-brain-eating-amoeba)

**Commentary:** The four-hour timing and the 200- to 300-fold selectivity are in vitro; the release is explicit that live efficacy is still ahead.

### 7. Nature Neuroscience: microglial NUS1 loss sends iron into dopaminergic neurons through LCN2–SLC22A17 (Parkinson’s)
**Summary:** Nature Neuroscience on October 26 published a paper from Haigang Ren and Guanghui Wang at Soochow University’s College of Pharmaceutical Sciences, with Jifeng Guo and Beisha Tang at Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, and colleagues. NUS1 encodes NgBR, which is highly expressed in microglia. In mice, microglial NgBR deficiency produces progressive Parkinson’s-like motor deficits and dopaminergic neuron loss through the LCN2–SLC22A17 iron-transport axis. In mouse and human microglia, NgBR loss triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress and PERK–ATF4–NUPR1 signaling, which preferentially increases LCN2-mediated iron efflux rather than classical cytokines. Only iron-loaded Holo-LCN2, not iron-free Apo-LCN2, is taken up by SLC22A17 on dopaminergic neurons, leading to iron overload and ferroptosis. Inhibiting this axis or ferroptosis reduced neuron loss and motor symptoms in vitro and in Nus1 conditional-knockout mice. Funding includes National Natural Science Foundation of China grants 32471047, 32170987, 32271039, and 82271281.

**Links:**

- [Nature Neuroscience — Microglial NUS1 deficiency drives dopaminergic neurodegeneration through intercellular iron dysregulation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02499-3)

**Commentary:** The therapeutic implication in the paper is pharmacological inhibition of this iron-transport axis; the abstract does not report dosing in people.

## III. Climate and Environment

### 9. An ancient Baffin Bay ice shelf about 1,400 to 2,100 meters thick and about 150,000 square kilometers across, followed by rapid ice-sheet retreat (Cryosphere)
**Summary:** Nature Geoscience on October 21 published a paper by Shannon Klotsko of the University of North Carolina Wilmington, Robert Hatfield of the University of Florida, and Brendan Reilly, Jacqueline Austermann, and Lauren Lewright of Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, with colleagues. The Columbia Climate School reported the work on October 26. Global ice volume peaked about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, when the Laurentide, Innuitian, and Greenland ice sheets met in Baffin Bay. Seafloor landforms indicate a basin-spanning ice shelf about 1,400 to 2,100 meters thick after a relative-sea-level correction, covering about 150,000 square kilometers and buttressing the three ice sheets. Sediment cores are used to infer that ice-shelf collapse triggered rapid ice-sheet retreat. Columbia’s account says the shelf disappeared over about 500 to 1,000 years, with the exact timing still uncertain. Hatfield said the evidence was at least half a mile thicker than any previously known Northern Hemisphere ice shelf.

**Links:**

- [Nature Geoscience — The Baffin Bay Ice Shelf stabilized three adjacent ice sheets during the last glacial period](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02126-7)
- [Columbia Climate School — How a massive ancient ice shelf stabilized glaciers, and what it means for Antarctica](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2026/10/26/how-a-massive-ancient-ice-shelf-stabilized-glaciers-and-what-it-means-for-antarctica/)

**Commentary:** The present-day point is an analogy: an ice shelf can disappear over centuries and reorganize marine ice sheets. The paper does not supply a future Antarctic sea-level number.

### 10. New Scientist: satellite heat flux is used to infer a 14 percent weakening of the AMOC over 30 years (Ocean climate)
**Summary:** New Scientist reported on October 26 an analysis by Richard Allan of the University of Reading and colleagues. Heat flux out of the North Atlantic into the atmosphere fell 18 percent over 40 years, and from that the authors infer that the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation weakened 14 percent over 30 years. The method combines the satellite energy imbalance with reanalyses and models to estimate heat leaving the ocean. The article says the paper is still under peer review, with a preprint on Research Square. For context it cites about a 10 percent decline since 2004 in Atlantic buoy measurements, and notes that the current can ebb for decades. Stefan Rahmstorf of the University of Potsdam argues that heat flux is not necessarily a better indicator than directly measured sea-surface temperature, while several lines of evidence point to a slowing circulation. A separate UK program of £81 million aims to build an early-warning system for collapse by 2030; this study is not part of it.

**Links:**

- [New Scientist — Satellites show some of the strongest evidence the AMOC is weakening](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2595629-satellites-show-some-of-the-strongest-evidence-the-amoc-is-weakening/)
- [Research Square — Evidence the ocean is losing less heat in the northern Atlantic and the AMOC has started to slowly weaken](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10854125/v1)

**Commentary:** The 14 percent figure is inferred from heat flux and has not finished peer review, so it should not be treated as the same number independently confirmed by the buoy record.

## IV. Basic Research

### 11. University of Oxford: continuous mass photometry follows single biomolecules for about an hour, with sub-kilodalton mass precision (Biophysics)
**Summary:** Nature Methods on October 26 published an open-access brief communication by Dan Loewenthal, Roi Asor, and Philipp Kukura of the University of Oxford, with colleagues. Continuous mass photometry tethers biomolecules to supported lipid bilayers and records the molecules, their complexes, and their interactions for about an hour with mass resolution, at up to sub-kilodalton mass precision and millisecond time resolution. On commercial instruments with 488- and 520-nanometer light, molecules often became stuck within tens of seconds. With 638-nanometer illumination on a custom instrument, individual proteins could often be followed for up to an hour, at the cost of weaker optical contrast. The paper says the method can currently track dynamics reliably when the starting mass is above 200 kilodaltons, the mass change is above 20 kilodaltons, and lifetimes run from seconds to tens of minutes. Raw data and code are in the Oxford Research Archive.

**Links:**

- [Nature Methods — Continuous mass photometry by single-molecule trapping](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03252-1)

**Commentary:** Sub-kilodalton precision is the method’s ceiling; the reliable window still requires molecules above 200 kilodaltons and mass steps above 20 kilodaltons.

### 12. Leiden University: FUS protein fibers grow outward from the droplet surface, and the glassy state competes with the fiber state (Soft matter)
**Summary:** Leiden University on October 26 described work by Liru Feng, Louise Jawerth, and Frank Jülicher of the Max Planck Institute for the Physics of Complex Systems, with colleagues, published in Nature Physics. The journal page lists October 20 as the publication date. The model protein is FUS, which is linked to neurodegenerative disease. Droplets are about 5 micrometers across. Twelve hours of three-dimensional confocal imaging showed fibers growing mainly from the droplet surface out into the dilute phase, while the droplets shrank and became glass-like solids. When proteins were aged for two days before fiber growth was started, the aged droplets hardly grew fibers. Fibers and the glassy state are two solid forms of the same protein: they can coexist, and they do not directly turn into each other. Data and code are on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20558827).

**Links:**

- [Nature Physics — Fibres and glasses are competing material states in protein condensation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03415-z)
- [Leiden University via Phys.org — Physicists reveal a new paradigm for fiber growth in neurodegenerative diseases](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-physicists-reveal-paradigm-fiber-growth.html)

**Commentary:** This is a competition between material states in purified FUS droplets. Jawerth said in the report that these fibers are not yet shown to be the main reason brain cells degenerate.
