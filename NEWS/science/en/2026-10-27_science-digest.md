## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. ESA’s Hera resolves Didymos and Dimorphos as two separate asteroids for the first time (planetary defense)
**Summary:** ESA reported on Oct 27 that its planetary-defense spacecraft Hera has, for the first time, resolved its binary target as two distinct bodies. On the evening of Oct 18, Hera’s monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera imaged the pair from about 1,280 km while the system was some 197 million km from Earth; Dimorphos (~151 m) appears as roughly one pixel but is clearly separated from Didymos (~780 m). Earlier braking burns reduced relative speed to about 20 m/s. The bodies orbit ~1.15 km apart on average, with Dimorphos moving at ~18 cm/s. ESA plans sharper images on Nov 25, then close-up surveys and two CubeSats in the months ahead.

**Links:**

- [ESA — First light: Hera resolves two asteroids instead of one](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Hera/First_light_Hera_resolves_two_asteroids_instead_of_one)

**Commentary:** This is an optical confirmation milestone before arrival; crater morphology and mass still await close-range measurements.

### 2. NASA’s UVEX passes Key Decision Point C: launch by January 2031, $590 million lifecycle cost (space astronomy)
**Summary:** NASA Science said on Oct 27 that the UltraViolet EXplorer (UVEX) completed Key Decision Point C. With KDP-C approved, the agency commits to a launch by January 2031 and a total lifecycle cost of $590 million. Selected in 2024 as a Medium-Class Explorer, UVEX will survey the ultraviolet sky and, together with wide-field missions such as Nancy Grace Roman, help build a multiwavelength map of the universe; it can also rapidly point at ultraviolet counterparts of gravitational-wave events such as neutron-star mergers. Principal investigator Fiona Harrison is at Caltech; partners include UC Berkeley, JPL, Northrop Grumman, and Space Dynamics Laboratory.

**Links:**

- [NASA Science — NASA’s UVEX Space Telescope Successfully Passes Key Milestone](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/uvex/2026/10/27/nasas-uvex-space-telescope-successfully-passes-key-milestone/)

**Commentary:** KDP-C locks budget and schedule commitments; hardware delivery and on-orbit sensitivity still depend on the build phase.

## II. Biomedicine

### 3. AbbVie files FDA BLA for etentamig: Phase 3 ORR 74.0% vs 45.7%, 60% PFS risk reduction (hematologic oncology)
**Summary:** AbbVie announced on Oct 27 that it submitted a Biologics License Application to the U.S. FDA for intravenous etentamig, a second-generation BCMA×CD3 bispecific T-cell engager, for adults with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma. The filing rests on the global randomized Phase 3 CERVINO trial (NCT06158841): 393 triple-class-exposed patients with at least two prior lines were randomized 1:1 versus investigator’s choice of standard available therapies. Dual primary endpoints showed overall response rate 74.0% vs 45.7% and a 60% reduction in progression risk. With a single step-up dose, cytokine release syndrome occurred in 28.3% of patients, all grade 1–2; dosing is monthly from initiation. Common adverse events also included hypogammaglobulinemia, neutropenia, and anemia.

### 5. Yale: NELF and MLLT1/3 control somatic hypermutation; losing NELF cuts mutations ~10-fold (immunology)
**Summary:** Yale School of Medicine on Oct 27 highlighted work by Lizhen Wu, David G. Schatz and colleagues in Nature on how B cells drive somatic hypermutation at rates about a million-fold above typical genomic mutation. Using an inducible-degradation RASH-1C system, they show negative elongation factor (NELF) can hitchhike with RNA polymerase II into antibody-gene bodies and pause transcription, exposing single-stranded DNA for AID; without NELF, point-mutation accumulation at targets falls about tenfold even though AID is still recruited. MLLT1 and MLLT3 engage AID via YEATS domains and are required to enrich AID at the right sites; removing both nearly abolishes hypermutation. The paper was published online Sept 30, 2026.

**Links:**

- [Yale School of Medicine — How the Immune System Gets Better at Detecting Pathogens](https://medicine.yale.edu/news-article/how-the-immune-system-gets-better-at-detecting-pathogens/)
- [Nature — Transcription pause and elongation regulators mediate somatic hypermutation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11092-4)

**Commentary:** The mechanism centers on transcriptional pausing and AID substrate generation; the coverage does not report human interventions for autoimmunity or lymphoma.

### 6. Nature news: macaque brain activity flickers ~15 times per second between aware and unaware representations (neuroscience)
**Summary:** A Nature news piece dated Oct 27 covers a UC Berkeley bioRxiv preprint from Doris Tsao’s group. In a binocular-rivalry experiment in macaques, neural activity alone reconstructed with high fidelity both faces the animals were presumed to be aware of and faces present but outside awareness; prior unconscious signals had been weak. After the second face appeared, activity in inferotemporal and prefrontal face-related patterns switched about 15 times per second. Tsao’s Oct 17 social-media summary later exceeded two million views. Authors frame it as a small step toward understanding consciousness; the article notes the work is not peer-reviewed, and some neuroscientists say parts of the result were not wholly unexpected.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Mysterious flickering brain signals could help to unlock secrets of consciousness](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03384-6)

**Commentary:** The flicker rate is a striking descriptive finding; links to human subjective awareness still need peer review and independent replication.

## III. Climate & Environment

### 7. University of Tokyo: enzyme-embedded compostable plastics nearly vanish in five months at 757 m depth (marine plastics)
**Summary:** Phys.org reported on Oct 27 on a Nature study led by Tadahisa Iwata at the University of Tokyo (DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11192-1). Heat-stable, freeze-dried Candida antarctica lipase B and Humicola insolens cutinase were embedded in compostable polyesters (PBS, PBSA, PCL, PBAT) as films and microbeads and deployed from coastal waters to the deep sea for 1–18 months. At 757 m, three polymer films with 3% cutinase lost nearly all mass within five months while enzyme-free controls barely changed; PBAT lost just over 12% under the same conditions. Enzymes still promoted breakdown at 5,503 m, and 0.1% enzyme sped degradation in shallow water. Authors stress that better marine biodegradability alone does not guarantee overall environmental sustainability.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — Embedded enzymes help plastics break down faster in the deep sea](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-embedded-enzymes-plastics-faster-deep.html)
- [Nature — Enzyme-embedded polyesters for accelerated biodegradation in the deep sea](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11192-1)

**Commentary:** Deep-sea mass-loss numbers are strong, but lifecycle impacts, enzyme leakage, and real-waste scenarios are not settled in this coverage.

### 8. Stockholm University: marine organic matter binds persistent pollutants ~10× more efficiently than terrestrial matter (Arctic environment)
**Summary:** Stockholm University’s Department of Environmental Science on Oct 27 highlighted work by Xiaodi Shi, Anna Sobek and colleagues in Nature Communications. Pollutant concentrations in surface sediments from 111 Eurasian Arctic shelf sites were combined with organic-matter traits from the Circum-Arctic Sediment Carbon Database (CASCADE; >4,000 data points). Marine primary-production-derived organic matter bound persistent pollutants such as PCBs about ten times more efficiently than soil-derived matter, possibly due to higher lipid content. Coupled to an ocean–ice biogeochemical model, climate-driven primary production in the Eurasian Arctic shelves could rise by a factor of ~1.5 by 2100 relative to 2000, increasing pollutant removal from the water column to the seafloor by about 1.3×. The paper’s publication date is Oct 23.

**Links:**

- [Stockholm University — Climate-driven shifts in marine life are increasing pollutant removal from Arctic waters](https://www.su.se/english/divisions/department-of-environmental-science/news/articles/2026-10-27-climate-driven-shifts-in-marine-life-are-increasing-pollutant-removal-from-arctic-waters)
- [Nature Communications — Marine primary production enhances removal of hydrophobic organic contaminants from Eurasian Arctic shelf waters](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78857-3)

**Commentary:** “Removal” here means sequestration into sediments, not disappearance of toxicity from the Arctic ecosystem.

## IV. Basic Research & Materials

### 9. Jülich: superconducting single-qubit gates in 6.8 ns with >99.98% fidelity (quantum computing)
**Summary:** Forschungszentrum Jülich, via Phys.org on Oct 27, reported a Nature Communications result with Aachen and Cologne collaborators: two elementary single-qubit operations on an isolated superconducting transmon ran with 6.8-nanosecond microwave pulses at measured fidelities of 99.981% and 99.989%, with unwanted higher-state transitions below 0.002% per gate. Comparable gates often take tens of nanoseconds. The R2D pulse-shaping method builds on DRAG and cuts multiphoton-transition probability by more than 20× versus the prior standard. The work is part of QSOLID; the qubit was fabricated at the Helmholtz Nano Facility. Authors note the method has not yet been shown on multi-qubit processors or two-qubit gates.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — Quantum gates set speed record at 6.8 nanoseconds with over 99.98% accuracy](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-quantum-gates-nanoseconds-accuracy.html)

**Commentary:** Speed and fidelity set a new single-qubit bar; coupled multi-qubit networks remain the real stress test for fault tolerance.

### 10. Nature Chemistry: isolable crystalline oxoboranes with a terminal B≡O bond (inorganic chemistry)
**Summary:** Nature Chemistry on Oct 27 published work by Sai Zou, Zhaoziyuan Yang, Pengyu Gao, Wei Lu and colleagues. Isolating free oxoboranes (RB≡O) with a genuine B–O triple bond in the condensed phase has been a long-standing challenge. The team reports a carbon-monoxide-triggered heterocumulene transformation yielding a stable, isolable free oxoborane; single-crystal X-ray diffraction establishes a terminal B≡O unit, and electronic-structure analysis shows two orthogonal π bonds consistent with a highly polarized triple bond. Mechanistic work supports a coordination–insertion–isomerization pathway; the species is ambiphilic and opens access to neutral and cationic derivatives. Crystallographic data are deposited at the CCDC (e.g., 2542988).

**Links:**

- [Nature Chemistry — Crystalline neutral and cationic oxoboranes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-026-02273-9)

**Commentary:** The result moves B≡O chemistry from gas-phase/matrix isolation into a manipulable crystalline platform; synthetic applications still lie ahead.

### 11. Nature Synthesis: Pt/Fe-NC electrochemical ammonia cracking sustains H₂ production beyond 2,000 hours (energy chemistry)
**Summary:** Nature Synthesis on Oct 27 published work by Rui Jiang, Yiwen Yao, Wei Chen, Pengfei Ou and colleagues. Ammonia is an attractive hydrogen carrier, but electrochemical cracking is limited by platinum oxidation and nitrogen poisoning. Platinum nanoparticles on nitrogen-doped carbon with atomically dispersed iron (Pt/Fe-NC) reach an NH₃-oxidation mass activity of 733 A g_Pt⁻¹; electron transfer from the support suppresses Pt oxidation and accelerates rate-determining dehydrogenation, while Fe-NC enables water-assisted in situ regeneration that hydrogenates poisonous *N into reactive NH-containing intermediates. With pulsed electrolysis, intermittent acid washing, and NH₃ replenishment, the catalyst sustains H₂ production for over 2,000 hours at 10 mA cm⁻² and over 500 hours at 1.5 A cm⁻².

**Links:**

- [Nature Synthesis — Catalyst regeneration enables durable electrochemical ammonia cracking for H₂ production](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44160-026-01201-6)

**Commentary:** Durability figures are striking; scale-up still hinges on current density, energy cost, and regeneration logistics.

### 12. MIT: modular origami shells ~3.5× stiffer while using about two-thirds the material (structural engineering)
**Summary:** MIT News reported on Oct 27 that Alfonso Parra Rubio, Neil Gershenfeld and colleagues, writing in the International Journal of Solids and Structures, developed a modular origami construction method. Inspired by Ron Resch tessellations, individually folded units form zero-degree-of-freedom rigid blocks that assemble with hand tools into load-bearing shells without interior columns; an automated solver turns design targets into CNC-cut flat-sheet cells. Simulations indicate structures about 3.5 times stiffer than some prior structural-origami approaches while using roughly two-thirds as much material. A ~23-foot folded-aluminum “Lexicon” sculpture was hung at the Boston Public Library by a non-professional team in days. Proposed uses include disaster shelters, aerospace parts, and architectural panels.

### 13. Science Tokyo: H2A.W and H2A.Z act oppositely to silence transposons without shutting down genes (plant epigenetics)
**Summary:** Institute of Science Tokyo reported on Oct 27 that Associate Professor Taiko To, Researcher Shoko Oda and colleagues studied Arabidopsis thaliana to ask how plants silence transposons without also silencing needed genes. After DNA methylation and related silencing features were lost and then restored, histone variants played opposite roles: H2A.W promotes heterochromatin in transposon-rich regions, while H2A.Z suppresses heterochromatin and helps protect genes. Transposon-dense chromosomal regions rebuilt silencing robustly even after extensive methylation loss; gene-rich, transposon-poor regions recovered less readily. The work shows that rebuilding a silenced state depends on chromosomal location within the same genome.

**Links:**

- [Science Tokyo — How do plants know which parts of DNA to silence—and which to protect?](https://www.isct.ac.jp/en/news/h0bbhr6b4lif)

**Commentary:** The mechanism suggests handles for epigenome editing and breeding, but the release does not report deployed crop-trait edits.
