## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. 8-Inch Foundries Hit Full Capacity as AI Power Chips Drive Mature-Node Price Hikes (Chips)
**Summary:** Electronic Times reported on Nov 27 that major domestic and overseas 8-inch (200mm) foundry lines are already at or near about 100% utilization, as AI data-center demand for power-management ICs (PMICs), power devices and other mature-node chips saturates capacity; chip designers seeking outsourced wafers now face long lead times or must pay premiums. A Korean 8-inch foundry official said lines are effectively fully running and further price increases are under consideration if demand keeps rising. Global players include TSMC, UMC, Vanguard (VIS) and SMIC, plus Samsung, DB HiTek and SK keyfoundry in Korea; Samsung is scaling back its roughly 300,000-wafer-per-month 8-inch foundry to prioritize HBM. TrendForce said SMIC and VIS raised prices earlier this year and are negotiating further hikes for the second half and next year, while Korean makers push broad increases centered on BCD and MOSFET processes used for AI data-center power chips.

### 2. Silicon Wafer Blanks Run Short as Top Four Suppliers Warn of Shortfalls by 2027 (Critical Materials)
**Summary:** heise online reported that the world’s four largest makers of silicon wafer blanks—Shin-Etsu, Sumco, GlobalWafers and Siltronic—warn in their latest annual reports that they may be unable to fully meet chipmaker demand by 2027 at the latest. Contract chipmakers and memory producers are expanding rapidly, while wafer plants ramp only slowly: facilities completed by 2025 are still climbing, Shin-Etsu is focusing on expanding existing sites, and no supplier has announced another new plant. Both 300mm wafers (processors, AI accelerators, memory) and 200mm wafers (power semiconductors) are affected, with chiplet designs, large interposers and HBM stacks lifting silicon demand further. Former Sumco unit Formosa Sumco said 200mm and 300mm capacity is fully utilized and is already allocating 200mm wafers by quota because AI-server power-management chip demand exceeded expectations. Suppliers say long-term low-price contracts leave little profit for economically viable expansion, with larger price increases unlikely before late 2026.

**Links:**

- [heise online — Silicon wafers are running short](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Silicon-wafers-are-running-short-11541160.html)

**Commentary:** Wafer blanks are the floor of the semiconductor stack—if foundries are full and silicon is rationed, the bottleneck climbs one more layer upstream.

### 3. FC-BGA Substrate Equipment Lead Times Stretch to About 30 Months as Shortages Spread Beyond Fabs (Chips)
**Summary:** Electronic Times reported on Nov 26 that AI-driven demand for both chips and FC-BGA substrates has stretched lead times for certain substrate-processing tools from roughly six months to about 30 months—nearly five times the prior norm—while some semiconductor manufacturing tools already wait three years or more, pushing equipment shortages into the substrate industry. Industry sources said some major substrate makers are even prepaying to request faster delivery, yet only a limited number with purchasing power have locked supply commitments. Key FC-BGA players include Japan’s Ibiden and Shinko, Taiwan’s Unimicron, and Korea’s Samsung Electro-Mechanics, Daeduck Electronics and LG Innotek. Equipment makers themselves struggle to secure Japanese precision motors, servos, drivers and controllers, with waits of about six months; substrate makers are sending letters of intent before formal orders, and competition to secure tools is expected to stay intense until expansion investments largely finish.

**Links:**

- [Electronic Times — AI-Driven Equipment Shortages Spread From Chipmakers to Substrate Makers](https://en.etnews.com/20261126200009)

**Commentary:** When the tools to expand capacity become scarce, the AI hardware bottleneck upgrades from “not enough fabs” to “not enough capacity to build capacity.”

## II. Critical Materials & Upstream Manufacturing

### 4. BCG: German Factories Underestimate Critical-Metal Risk as Tungsten Intermediate Prices Surged About 500% (Critical Materials)
**Summary:** Mining Metal News reported on Nov 27, citing a Boston Consulting Group analysis reviewed by Handelsblatt, that German manufacturers underestimate how shortages of critical raw materials could halt production. After China restricted tungsten exports, the price of a key intermediate rose by about 500%, pressuring cemented-carbide cutting-tool makers; many firms gauge exposure only via direct suppliers and purchase costs, overlooking materials embedded in motors, sensors, semiconductors and the tools that machine them. BCG estimates the global gallium market is worth less than $350 million a year while dependent finished goods are worth an estimated $3.5 trillion; by 2040, demand for some critical materials could reach 120%–250% of projected supply. China accounts for about 2% of cobalt mining but Chinese owners control about 47% of processing; for nickel, about 31% of refining sits in China while Chinese owners control about 76% worldwide. Germany’s VDMA is also watching cobalt and synthetic diamonds, warning of further disruption if postponed Chinese export curbs on diamond powder and grinding wheels take effect in January 2027; BCG separately puts up to €200–350 billion of German gross production at risk.

## III. Capacity, Migration & Export Controls

### 5. Fast-Fashion Brands Divert About $254 Million in Orders from Bangladesh, Mostly to India (Migration)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 26–27 that global apparel retailers including Zara and H&M shifted nearly a tenth of their monthly Bangladesh orders to other Asian hubs in the first half of November; three factory owners said about $254 million of orders were diverted between Nov 1 and 18, most to neighboring India, with Vietnam, Cambodia and Indonesia also benefiting. The Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA) said protests have caused more than $400 million in production losses in an industry that accounts for more than 80% of export earnings and nearly 40% of manufacturing jobs. Rising wages, energy shortages and political uncertainty have eroded the country’s low-cost edge; factories around Dhaka lost an estimated 20–25 production days. A BGMEA director said India can offer about a week’s delivery advantage and Vietnam or Cambodia as much as about 15 days, with rival hubs typically completing production and delivery in 60–90 days versus about 120 days in Bangladesh; owners worry diverted orders could become permanent.

### 6. Nvidia Halts AI Server Shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia Under New U.S. Rules (Policy)
**Summary:** Reuters, the Financial Times and the New York Times reported on Nov 26–27 that Nvidia has told manufacturing partners including Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers containing its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. The move follows new U.S. Commerce Department curbs that took effect last week, requiring export licenses for advanced chips to those countries (and others such as Brazil) and restricting remote access via third-country data centers—aimed at closing routes Chinese firms have used to rent banned AI compute. Affected regional cloud providers said the rules impose a roughly 90-day window to secure licenses but offer little execution guidance, creating chaos in local supply chains; the four countries have been key destinations for Chinese AI developers seeking Nvidia clusters offshore. It remains unclear whether the halt is temporary while compliance is clarified.

### 7. U.S. Section 301 “Excess Capacity” Tariffs Target Auto Supply Chains and Blunt China+1 Savings (Policy)
**Summary:** Automotive Logistics reported that the USTR has proposed tariffs of up to about 40% on China, up to about 25% on Vietnam, about 20% on the EU, and additional duties covering Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, India and others, following March 2026 Section 301 investigations into “structural excess capacity” across 14 economies; measures are expected next year and specifically call out autos in China and the EU plus Chinese batteries, semiconductors, steel and aluminum. Under current proposals, imports from economies other than China would generally not stack the new tariffs atop existing Section 232 duties, yet U.S. OEMs building vehicles with imported components could still see higher domestic production costs. Malaysia is a key semiconductor hub, Thailand has a deep auto supplier base, and Indonesia is central to nickel and battery materials—so taxing Southeast Asia complicates diversification away from China and forces component-level reassessment of origin, classification and landed cost.

## IV. Manufacturing Momentum & Logistics

### 8. Reuters Poll: China’s Official Manufacturing PMI Seen at 49.4 in November, Second Month of Contraction (Manufacturing)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 27 that a poll of 13 economists puts China’s official manufacturing PMI at a median 49.4 for November, slightly above October’s 49.3 but still below the 50 expansion threshold for a second straight month; the National Bureau of Statistics is due to release the reading on Monday. Solid export demand for electronics and cars has not offset weak domestic consumption, while Middle East-related energy costs keep producer expenses elevated and hard to pass through. Industrial profit growth accelerated in October at the fastest pace in five months, but AI-related and upstream sectors outpaced traditional downstream industries. Heavy export reliance also heightens trade-friction risk after the U.S. this week proposed new tariffs on 14 partners, with the highest rates on Chinese goods. The private RatingDog manufacturing PMI, compiled by S&P Global, is forecast to ease to 51.5 from 52 in October and is due Tuesday.

### 9. Suez Comeback Congests European Ports: About 60% of Asia–Europe Capacity Returns, UK Cargo Diverted to Continent (Logistics)
**Summary:** World Ports Organization reported on Nov 27 that the gradual return of major carriers to the Suez Canal is concentrating arrivals at Northern European hubs and delaying Asia-to-UK shipments. The Suez Canal Authority recorded 1,194 transits in the first 24 days of November 2026, up 34.8% year on year, with revenues roughly doubled; about 60% of Asia–Europe transport capacity is again routing via Suez, with MSC, ONE, Evergreen, Maersk, Hapag-Lloyd and Ocean Alliance partners among those resumed or scheduled to return, and analysts expecting a broader shift in early 2027. UK forwarder Davies Turner said some UK-bound containers are being discharged in the Netherlands or Germany for onward transfer; Change of Destination fees are costly, empty returns are refused at some ports, and road capacity is squeezed as importers rush to avoid demurrage. Rotterdam and peers are already highly saturated, so shorter sailing times are temporarily absorbed by terminal and inland bottlenecks.
