## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. TSMC Reportedly Halts 16nm-and-Below Work for Chinese Designers as Controls Spill into Consumer and Auto Chips (Semiconductors)
**Summary:** Brief Asia reported on Nov 22, citing people familiar with the matter, that TSMC has told affected Chinese chip designers that orders at 16 nanometres and below are suspended unless they obtain U.S. export licences; those customers are generally limited to older 28nm and 40nm processes that avoid high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and advanced packaging. New U.S. Commerce Department rules that took effect on Nov 19 removed a verification path that had allowed some Chinese designs to clear controls without individual licences. Tom's Hardware, also on Nov 22 and citing Nikkei Asia, said the rules treat 16nm/14nm nodes as presumed advanced computing chips—even below 30 billion transistors and without HBM—and that TSMC has held some shipments to both Chinese and non-Chinese designers for compliance review, affecting smartphone, TV and automotive chips. Samsung has also reportedly paused some shipments to Chinese customers.

**Links:**

- [Brief Asia — TSMC Suspends Advanced Chip Manufacturing for Chinese Designers](https://www.briefasia.com/en/article/tsmc-suspends-advanced-chip-manufacturing-chinese-designers-us-export-rules)
- [Tom's Hardware — TSMC pauses shipments as BIS rules flag 16nm/14nm nodes](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/policy/tsmc-reportedly-pauses-shipments-to-chinese-designers-after-u-s-export-rules-flag-decade-old-16nm-and-14nm-nodes-new-bis-controls-threaten-smartphone-tv-and-automotive-chips)

**Commentary:** Export controls have moved from cutting-edge AI silicon into decade-old mature nodes, narrowing China's alternative compute path and putting consumer and automotive delivery on a “compliance first, then fab” cadence.

### 2. Nvidia Server Partners Halt Southeast Asia Shipments as Suppliers Worry About GPU Inventory (Semiconductors)
**Summary:** Lianhe Zaobao reported on Nov 22 that at least two of Nvidia's server partners have suspended shipments to Southeast Asian customers after the U.S. tightened advanced-chip export controls, with one also temporarily declining new regional orders—potentially delaying equipment for the region's fast-growing AI data centres. Nikkei Asia said one unnamed server maker halted all shipments from Nov 20, while another paused deliveries on legal advice pending compliance checks. The U.S. Commerce Department last week began requiring licences before shipping controlled advanced chips to destinations including Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia, aiming to stop restricted entities from remotely accessing U.S. compute via third-country facilities. One supplier executive said clients must provide more information before licence applications and feared expensive GPU boards piling up as inventory; the chain is also assessing planning uncertainty from the second half of 2028 after Jensen Huang's departure as CEO.

### 3. Corporate Japan Restarts Rare-Earth Supply Chains via Refining, Recycling and Overseas Mining Rights (Critical Materials)
**Summary:** Nikkei Asia reported on Nov 22 that China's rare-earth export controls are pushing Japanese firms to rebuild an independent supply chain, marking a comeback after the industry retreated in the 1990s under Chinese low-price competition. Proterial (formerly Hitachi Metals), Mitsubishi Materials and others are expanding domestic refining, recycling and overseas mining rights; the strategy spans extraction, refining, recycling and reuse, with refining still the tightest bottleneck. Related coverage says yttrium prices have risen roughly ninefold in a year under the curbs, spurring alternative sourcing and scrap recovery; Mitsubishi Materials has restarted rare-earth recycling, Toyota Tsusho aims to expand rare-metal recycling, trading houses such as Marubeni are moving upstream, and deep-sea mud near Minamitorishima remains a longer-term domestic option. Japanese firms are also studying feedstock and processing projects in Australia, Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

**Links:**

- [Nikkei Asia — Corporate Japan races to revive rare-earth supply chain amid China curbs](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/materials/corporate-japan-races-to-revive-rare-earth-supply-chain-amid-china-curbs)

### 4. Japan Considers Adding Organic Chemicals to “Specified Critical Products” Amid Hormuz and China-Dependence Risks (Policy)
**Summary:** The Japan Times reported on Nov 22, citing sources, that the government is considering adding organic chemical products to the list of “specified critical products” under the economic security promotion law to secure stable supply. The materials feed plastics and high-performance inputs essential for semiconductor manufacturing. Officials plan closed-door expert discussions aiming to amend the relevant Cabinet order by the end of fiscal 2026 (March next year). Ethylene and propylene are made from naphtha and related feedstocks; U.S.-Israeli conflict with Iran has effectively disrupted Hormuz shipping, while Japan still relies on Middle East oil for most naphtha feedstock, domestic chemical output has declined, and dependence on China has risen. Semiconductors, storage batteries and ventilators are already designated under the subsidy-backed system.

**Links:**

- [The Japan Times — Japan considers adding organic chemicals to ‘critical products’ list](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2026/11/22/japan/organic-chemicals-critical-items/)

**Commentary:** Placing basic organic chemicals on the security list shows geopolitical shocks have climbed from finished chips upstream into naphtha and chemical intermediates.

## II. Batteries & Manufacturing Capacity

### 5. Rosatom Commissions Moscow's Second Energy-Storage Gigafactory and Signs Electrolyte Pact (Batteries)
**Summary:** AK&M reported on Nov 22 that Rosatom commissioned a second lithium-ion energy-storage gigafactory in Moscow for EVs and stationary systems, rated at about 4 GWh a year; the first plant opened in Kaliningrad in 2025. Each gigafactory is designed for about 18 million cells and 50,000 traction batteries a year. The Moscow site has more than 1,400 pieces of equipment and over 450 robots, about 95% automation and roughly 2.5 km of process lines; trial runs produced about 20 Atom EV batteries, about 600 modules and more than 40,000 cells. The same day, Rosatom's fuel division and HimMed signed an agreement to produce lithium-ion battery electrolytes at an existing HimMed site, with potential capacity of up to about 10,000 tonnes a year—enough for roughly 15–20 GWh of batteries.

### 6. Bangladesh Garment Unrest: 317 Factories Report About $682 Million in Losses as Orders Relocate (Manufacturing)
**Summary:** The Business Standard reported on Nov 22 that a Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA) survey found 317 factories suffered about $682 million (Tk8,184 crore) in losses from labour-unrest disruptions and order relocation. Direct production losses were about $428 million, while confirmed orders worth about $254 million—about 59 million pieces—shifted to other countries. The assessment covers Nov 1–18 after workers began protests in October for a Tk30,000 monthly minimum wage and unrest widened early this month. More than 600 member factories were affected; the BGMEA president said 669 plants were hit, with stoppages lasting up to 21 days, and 25 factories remained closed on the day of the report. The association warned remaining orders face costly air freight, discounts or cancellations, damaging buyer confidence and long-term credibility.

## III. Logistics, Trade & Geopolitics

### 7. LA/Long Beach Watchmen Reach Tentative Deal, Averting Strike at U.S. Busiest Port Complex (Logistics)
**Summary:** gCaptain (Reuters) and ContainerSignal reported on Nov 21–22 that the Pacific Maritime Association and ILWU Local 26 reached a tentative contract covering about 250 watchmen at the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach, pulling immediate strike risk off the table. The deal still needs member ratification, and the current contract is extended until that vote. Though few in number, watchmen control terminal gate access, security patrols and safety reporting at terminals handling about 85% of Long Beach cargo and 33% of Los Angeles cargo; a walkout could have drawn other longshore workers into honouring picket lines. Talks broke off Wednesday and resumed Friday under federal mediation; PMA's prior offer included a one-time $3,200 bonus and wage terms lifting full-time average annual pay from more than $170,000 to about $198,000.
