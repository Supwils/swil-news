### 1. Memory Shortage Enters “Camp-at-the-Fab” Phase as Some Chips Nearly Decuple in a Year (Chips)
**Summary:** Seoul Economic Daily reported on Nov 26, citing the Wall Street Journal, that the AI-driven memory shortage has turned into a global scramble: some large AI firms have booked lodging near Samsung Electronics plants and stationed staff to track deliveries, while Karma Automotive’s CEO has visited Korea four times in six months and says suppliers are courteous but final allocations remain extremely hard to secure. Some memory prices have risen nearly tenfold over the past year, feeding price hikes for Apple MacBooks, iPhone 18 Pro models, Xbox, Echo Dot and Meta’s Quest 3.

### 2. Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix Lock Multiyear Deals as Samsung Capacity Books Out Through 2029 (Chips)
**Summary:** DIGITIMES reported on Nov 26 that the world’s three largest memory makers—Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix—are securing multiyear supply agreements as AI-driven shortages push customers to commit cash years before chips ship. SamMobile, citing Korean media the same day, said Samsung has already booked most of its memory production capacity through 2029, with long-term agreements signed with 10 major customers and talks underway with about 10 more, some extending to 2031; the company aims for such deals to cover about 60%–70% of memory capacity by 2030, with terms that may include price floors, advance payments and ceilings. Industry sources estimate makers are meeting only about 60%–70% of demand, with 2027 commodity DRAM wafer capacity virtually sold out, while priority for higher-margin HBM is squeezing conventional DRAM and enterprise NAND/SSD supply.

## II. Critical Materials & Upstream Manufacturing

### 4. Hunan Valin’s 65-Day Blast-Furnace Overhaul Plus Hebei Sintering Curbs Tighten China Rebar Into 1Q 2027 (Critical Materials)
**Summary:** ChemAnalyst reported on Nov 25 that Chinese steel rebar supply is tightening on two fronts: Hunan Valin Lianyuan Steel began a roughly 65-day overhaul of its 3,200-cubic-meter blast furnace on Nov 20, expected to cut about 400,000 tonnes of monthly output across HRC, CRC, rebar and industrial wire, while Jiuquan Steel, Yunnan Fangda and Qujing Hengte add further construction-steel and crude-steel losses. Hebei cities including Handan, Xingtai and Baoding remain under heavy-pollution emergency responses, with sintering curtailed about 20%–50% and hot-metal losses of roughly 9,000–10,000 tonnes per day from already-idled furnaces constraining downstream rolling. For the week ended Nov 20, rebar output fell a second week to 1.8264 million tonnes and mill inventories flipped to accumulation at 1.5015 million tonnes; apparent consumption dropped about 5.44% week on week as northern construction slows, while CISA’s push to strictly enforce export licensing leaves overseas buyers planning for constrained Chinese rebar availability through the first quarter of 2027.

## III. Capacity & Relocation

### 5. Trump’s Tariffs on 14 Economies Blunt the Appeal of China+1 Diversification (Relocation)
**Summary:** Bloomberg reported on Nov 26 that Washington’s latest tariff push against 14 Asian and European economies risks undercutting the China+1 manufacturing shift that followed the U.S.–China trade war, hitting winners such as Vietnam with rates that could approach or in some cases exceed those on Chinese goods—though exemptions often keep effective charges below headline levels. BusinessToday’s same-day digest said Vietnam faces proposed duties of up to about 35%, while India, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand could see up to about 25%; Southeast Asia drew a record roughly $246 billion of FDI in 2025, with manufacturing FDI up nearly 50% to about $40 billion, and Vietnam’s U.S. surplus hit about $191 billion in the first nine months of 2026 even as China/Hong Kong machinery exports to Vietnam rose from about $7 billion in 2014 to about $20 billion in 2025. Hinrich Foundation’s Deborah Elms said some firms may simply “go back to China” amid paperwork and tariff churn, while Eurasia Group’s Dan Wang argued companies are more likely to automate and consolidate than relocate again.

### 6. Vietnam Seeks a Fast U.S. Trade Deal to Defuse a Possible 35% Tariff Threat (Policy)
**Summary:** CNA reported on Nov 26 that Vietnam’s trade ministry, hours after USTR unveiled Section 301 “excess capacity” tariff proposals on 14 economies, said Hanoi is working with Washington to complete a trade deal as soon as possible and will cooperate with the U.S. investigation. Vietnam runs the largest trade surplus of any U.S. partner and faces potential rates of about 35%—the highest among Southeast Asian targets and second only to China; U.S. officials have said a reciprocal deal could leave Vietnam at about 20%, above its existing general duty of about 12.5% and above the roughly 18% deals struck by Bangladesh, Cambodia and Indonesia, but still well below China and enough to preserve nearshore appeal. Apparel, footwear and electronics assembly are most exposed; Washington also accuses Vietnam of serving as a transshipment point for Chinese goods, a particular risk for electronics that rely heavily on Chinese components, while Samsung and Tesla have urged against further IP-related tariff hikes. Vietnam the same day pledged to review imported-input dependence, build a domestic supplier database and create an early-warning system for trade surpluses.

### 7. HBR: Tariffs Will Not Reshore U.S. Auto Supply Chains as Domestic Casting Quotes Run 50%+ Above China (Relocation)
**Summary:** A Harvard Business Review analysis—featured as the lead item in Manufacturing Connected’s Nov 26 briefing—uses firm-level U.S. customs records covering more than 40 countries to argue that U.S. auto suppliers are not reshoring despite tariff pressure and cannot do so soon. One anonymous U.S. safety-component maker seeking a domestic source for a steel casting its Mexican plant had bought from China contacted more than 100 U.S. foundries; only six or seven quoted, all at least 50% above the Chinese $200 price, with one bid at $1,000. Researchers cite three structural barriers: scarce patient capital, a two-to-three-year ramp for new plants, and a largely vanished base of foundries, forges and machine-tool makers. Canada-assembled vehicle tariffs are also set to rise to 50% on Jan 1, 2027, compressing the USMCA window for the North American auto chain. The authors urge product redesign, “micro-shoring” movable steps, contractual tariff-risk sharing and customs arrangements rather than full-chain relocation fantasies.

**Links:**

- [Harvard Business Review — Why tariffs aren’t bringing factories back to the U.S.](https://hbr.org/2026/11/why-tariffs-arent-bringing-factories-back-to-the-u-s)
- [Manufacturing Connected — Why tariffs won't reshore auto supply chains](https://www.mfgconnected.com/briefings/20261126)

**Commentary:** Tariffs can rewrite landed costs but cannot recreate a missing foundry and machine-tool ecosystem—without that base, reshoring is just a more expensive idle hope.

### 9. DHL: Container Rates Still Up 138% YoY as Congestion Idles About 3.6 Million TEUs (Logistics)
**Summary:** FreightWaves reported on Nov 26 that DHL Global Forwarding’s November update puts container market rates 138% higher year over year and about 15% above August, with prices roughly stabilizing month on month as peak-season intensity fades; rates should stay well above last year’s baseline but are unlikely to match 2025 levels. Global demand is up about 4% year over year and Asian exports about 8%; Far East–Europe westbound demand is up about 9% year to date while the backhaul is flat. More than 60% of Asia–Europe services are again routing via Suez–Red Sea, yet fewer than one in three vessels arrive on time at key Asian ports, with backlog clearance expected into early summer 2027; VLSFO bunker prices are about 69% above February and diesel is up as much as 75% since December 2025, so inland surcharges should persist.

### 10. UNCTAD: Four Maritime Chokepoints Under Pressure; 2026 Seaborne Trade Seen Flat (Logistics)
**Summary:** UN News reported on Nov 26 that UN Trade and Development’s Review of Maritime Transport 2026 finds seaborne volumes reached 12.3 billion tonnes in 2025 but growth slowed to 1.9%, half the 2024 pace; with the Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea, Black Sea and Panama Canal under simultaneous pressure, UNCTAD projects no volume growth for 2026 and a roughly 3.2% rebound in 2027. Acting Secretary-General Pedro Manuel Moreno said Iran–U.S.–linked shipping attacks created a near-standstill in Hormuz oil and gas flows, with war-risk premiums for a $100 million tanker reportedly jumping within days from about $200,000 to $1 million per voyage, while container lines added emergency war-risk and fuel surcharges. Volumes are seen flat this year even as miles travelled rise about 1.5%; if the “fuel shock” lasts into 2027, shipping-cost-driven consumer-price increases in least-developed countries could approach twice the global average. South–South container routes grew about 12.1% last year and accounted for nearly one-third of containerized-trade growth, yet chokepoint fragility remains.
