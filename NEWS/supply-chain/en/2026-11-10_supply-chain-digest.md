## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Nexperia and ST warn: AI is crowding out mature-node capacity and stressing auto chips (Auto semiconductors)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 10 from the Electronica trade fair in Munich that Nexperia interim CEO Stefan Tilger said the Dutch chipmaker is “totally oversubscribed” as some suppliers divert manufacturing capacity toward AI data centers and away from the mature commodity chips used in cars and industrial equipment, creating bottlenecks in parts of the automotive supply chain; he said the auto industry is “suffering … quite heavily.” STMicroelectronics CEO Jean-Marc Chery had warned a day earlier that foundries and packaging firms are shifting resources away from automotive and industrial products, saying “we see many bottlenecks,” with some previously broad-serving plants “disengaging … from mainstream technology.” Tilger said AI and robotics demand is absorbing capacity while investment in diodes, transistors and other mature components has lagged for years.

### 2. Team Group warns memory shortage may last into 2028: 16Gb DDR5 contract prices up more than fivefold in a year (Memory)
**Summary:** Fortune reported on Nov 10 that Team Group CEO Gerry Chen, speaking on the Nov 2 earnings call, said the global memory shortage will worsen to the point that “even money can’t buy goods,” with the most severe stretch running from now through the end of the second quarter of 2027, no downward price trend next year, and supply-demand balance unlikely before the second half of 2028 at the earliest. He said Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron have shifted capacity toward HBM, with AI servers and enterprise apps taking most DRAM output; 16Gb DDR5 contract prices rose from about $8.70 in October 2025 to about $48 in September 2026. Even with contracts, the company cannot secure enough volume and must buy spot at a roughly 30%–50% premium; its New Taipei plant is slated to start in the second quarter of 2027.

**Links:**

- [Fortune — Memory chip CEO warns 2027 shortage will be so bad that 'even money can't buy goods'](https://fortune.com/2026/11/10/team-group-ceo-memory-chip-shortage-2027-even-money-cant-buy-goods/)

**Commentary:** Once HBM-first allocation becomes the default, consumer electronics and autos get whatever DRAM is left.

### 3. LME aluminum inventories down about 56% year to date: spot premiums rise as Middle East disruption limits deliverable metal (Base metals)
**Summary:** ScrapMonster reported on Nov 10, citing Westmetall and Bloomberg data, that LME aluminum warehouse stocks have fallen about 56% in 2026 to roughly 226,975 metric tons on Nov 9—the lowest reported level since about 1990. Cash metal traded around a $92/MT premium to three-month futures that day, with the official cash settlement at $3,147/MT versus $3,059.50/MT for three-month metal, a $87.50/MT backwardation. Middle East conflict has raised Persian Gulf shipping costs and delivery risk; Chinese capacity constraints and trade barriers toward the U.S. and Europe have also limited metal that can quickly enter LME warrants. Indonesian expansion is seen as longer-term relief, but new output is not yet registered as LME-deliverable; an ING strategist warned wider backwardation may be needed to pull metal into warehouses.

**Links:**

- [ScrapMonster — Aluminum Supply Squeeze Pushes Spot Premiums Higher](https://www.scrapmonster.com/news/aluminum/aluminum-supply-squeeze-pushes-spot-premiums-higher-2026-11-10/100439)

**Commentary:** More smelting capacity is not the same as “warrantable aluminum”—deliverable scarcity is rewriting manufacturers’ prompt buying costs.

## II. Batteries & Materials Localization

### 4. BMW adds Samsung SDI and LG Energy Solution for Gen6 cells: Europe- and U.S.-made supply for “local for local” (Batteries)
**Summary:** BMW Group said it has contracted Samsung SDI and LG Energy Solution as additional suppliers of sixth-generation (Gen6) high-voltage battery cells, with Samsung SDI supplying from Europe and LG Energy Solution from the United States beginning in the late 2020s. Europe’s cell mix will include CATL, EVE Energy and Samsung SDI, while LG Energy Solution will supply BMW in the U.S.; the company stressed a “local for local” approach that shortens distances among cell makers, battery assembly and vehicle plants. BMW has built Gen6 high-voltage battery assembly sites near vehicle plants in Irlbach-Straßkirchen (Germany), Debrecen (Hungary), Shenyang (China), Woodruff (U.S.) and San Luis Potosí (Mexico). Yonhap and The Korea Herald followed on Nov 10; Korean reporting says Samsung SDI plans 46 mm cylindrical cells at Göd, Hungary, and that LG Energy Solution’s Arizona line may supply around 10 GWh a year, though official contract value and total volumes were not disclosed.

### 5. LS MnM inaugurates Onsan battery-materials plant: about 24,000 tons of nickel sulfate a year from mine and recycled feed (Battery materials)
**Summary:** CHOSUNBIZ reported on Nov 10 that LS Group nonferrous smelter LS MnM held its 90th-anniversary ceremony and a completion event for a battery-materials plant at the Onsan Smelter in Ulju, Ulsan. The complex covers about 95,007 square meters and can process both mined nickel feedstock and material recovered from spent batteries, with plans to produce about 24,000 tons a year of nickel sulfate—enough for roughly 500,000 electric vehicles, the company said—plus cobalt sulfate and lithium hydroxide for battery-materials customers. Management framed the project as proving end-to-end capability from raw materials through materials production and recycling amid feedstock shortages and rising demand; Onsan ranks among the world’s largest single-site copper smelters, and the battery-materials push has been underway since 2023.

**Links:**

### 6. After China’s rare-earth control pause is extended, EU firms say financing and pricing still block alternative supply (Critical minerals)
**Summary:** The South China Morning Post reported on Nov 10 that as Beijing extended the pause on export controls covering selected rare earths and related technologies, as well as lithium batteries and synthetic graphite anode material, into January, European industry welcomed breathing room but stressed the need to diversify rare-earth supply. EIT RawMaterials CEO Bernd Schaefer said the extension underscores Europe’s need for predictable, continued access; executives also flagged financing hurdles, unreliable pricing signals and differing national rules as barriers to domestic substitution projects. S&P Global the same day noted the renewed pause covers five elements—holmium, erbium, thulium, europium and ytterbium—plus certain processing technologies and equipment, while April 2025 controls on seven other rare earths (samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium) remain in force.

## III. Capacity & Manufacturing

### 7. GE Vernova to invest about $34.5 million expanding Shreveport transformer plant: roughly 175 direct jobs (Grid equipment)
**Summary:** GE Vernova announced on Nov 10 a roughly $34.5 million expansion of its transformer manufacturing operations in Shreveport, Louisiana, to meet rising demand for grid equipment serving industry, energy systems and digital technologies. The project is expected to create about 175 direct jobs atop more than 630 existing roles, with Louisiana Economic Development estimating about 185 additional indirect jobs. Upgrades include new equipment, process improvements and supporting infrastructure focused on industrial and renewable-energy transformers, with full capacity targeted for 2028. The roughly 500,000-square-foot site has produced transformers since 1970; the company said the move is part of nearly $1.3 billion in U.S. manufacturing and R&D investment planned for 2025–2028.

### 8. EU Chamber China chief warns: European manufacturers could stop trying to compete with China by 2030 (Competition)
**Summary:** The Guardian reported on Nov 10 that Jens Eskelund, president of the European Union Chamber of Commerce in China, warned that if current trends continue, European manufacturers could face an existential question by the end of the decade—“what is the point of trading?”—amid fears of mass job losses. He cited a Goldman Sachs forecast that Chinese exports could cut EU GDP by about 0.5% and Germany’s by up to 0.9% by decade’s end, with the EU deficit running at a record about £1 billion (€1.18 billion) a day. Eskelund said many European firms are shifting from “Made in China for China” to “Made in China for the world,” and argued Europe may need policies that let domestic manufacturers join Chinese firms’ supply chains rather than treat them only as rivals; he said autos, chemicals and renewables are already feeling the pressure, and put China’s currency undervaluation at about 30%.

**Links:**

## IV. Logistics, Trade & Geopolitics

### 9. Bangladesh garment factories largely resume: wage deal unsettled, export and feeder-space risks remain (Apparel)
**Summary:** Logistics provider Metro said on Nov 10 that garment production across Bangladesh is recovering after widespread industrial unrest, with early signs that the hit to international shipments may be milder than first feared, though unresolved wage talks leave room for renewed disruption. Workers from early November demanded raising the monthly minimum wage from about Tk12,500 to about Tk30,000; protests spread across Dhaka, Ashulia, Savar, Gazipur and Narayanganj, forcing hundreds of factories to halt and disrupting roads. On Nov 4 the Minimum Wage Board agreed in principle to a revised structure from December without specifying the increase; on Nov 5 more than 100 factories in Ashulia suspended operations again, and by Nov 8 most plants in main industrial zones had resumed. Bangladesh is the world’s second-largest garment exporter, employing about four million workers and accounting for more than 80% of export earnings; if delayed cargo is released at once, feeder space from Chattogram to Singapore, Colombo and Port Klang—and urgent airfreight—could tighten.

**Links:**

- [Metro Global — Bangladesh garment production recovers as Metro monitors potential export disruption](https://www.metro.global/2026/11/10/bangladesh-garment-production-recovers-as-metro-monitors-potential-export-disruption/)

**Commentary:** Restarted sewing lines are not a reset supply chain—in the spring/summer fashion window, the wage settlement is still the next gate.

### 10. Hormuz disruption lifts Gulf congestion: Maersk sees rising Saudi Landbridge demand; Hormuz transit fee about $1,000 per box (Shipping)
**Summary:** Maersk’s November India–Middle East–Africa market update (published Nov 9 and widely followed around Nov 10) said disruption linked to the Strait of Hormuz and congestion at hubs such as Khor Fakkan pushed more shippers toward alternatives including the Saudi Landbridge, with interest expected to continue through November—though capacity, customs, border processing and final delivery must be confirmed shipment by shipment. DataPortuaria, citing Maersk’s Middle East operational update, said vessels transiting Hormuz may incur an additional about $1,000 per container (covering insurance and crew risk) and that booking and landside multimodal options for some Gulf destinations have been restricted.

### 11. Tariff shadows cost Canadian manufacturers U.S. orders: buyers walk even when duties do not yet apply (Trade)
**Summary:** The Financial Post reported on Nov 10 that U.S. customers are abandoning Canadian suppliers over the threat of tariffs—even when no duties currently apply—and that some lost business may never return. Control GMC Inc., a Boucherville, Quebec maker of specialized food-packaging machinery that traditionally generates about 70% of sales in the United States, said it sold no new machines south of the border for about six months beginning in 2025 because American buyers feared new duties could be imposed between order and delivery. The firm’s unusual response was to promise to protect such buyers against unexpected tariffs during that window. The reporting underscores that the mere prospect of tariffs can rewrite cross-border sourcing decisions.

**Links:**

- [Financial Post — How even the whiff of tariff uncertainty is costing Canadian manufacturers their U.S. business](https://financialpost.com/economy/tariff-uncertainty-costs-canadian-manufacturers/)

**Commentary:** Tariffs need not land to rewrite supply chains—uncertainty itself is a trade barrier.
