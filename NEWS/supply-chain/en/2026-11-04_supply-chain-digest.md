## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Siltronic: AI redirects wafer supply to data centers, squeezing phones and PCs (Chips)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 4 that Siltronic CEO Michael Heckmeier said the AI chip boom is redirecting semiconductor supply toward data centers and away from smartphones and PCs. The wafer maker raised its full-year sales forecast for the second time this year on stronger wafer demand. Heckmeier said AI is not merely offsetting weakness elsewhere—it is creating scarcity; when customers choose, chips go to hyperscalers who pay the most. He estimated AI server farms consume roughly three to four times more silicon area than traditional servers. Even if AI demand cooled, he argued, capacity could flow back to phones and PCs where underlying demand remains solid.

**Links:**

- [Investing.com / Reuters — AI boom draws chip supply from other end-markets, Siltronic CEO says](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/ai-boom-draws-chip-supply-from-other-endmarkets-siltronic-ceo-says-5000506)

**Commentary:** Allocation at the wafer layer now follows who pays most—consumer electronics must re-queue behind AI buildouts.

### 2. Chipmakers lock equipment orders out to 2028–2029 as lead times stretch one to two years (Equipment)
**Summary:** The Electronic Times (ETNews) reported on Nov 4, citing industry sources from Nov 3, that Applied Materials, ASML, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron, and KLA are in late-stage talks on 2028 equipment supply contracts, with some discussions already extending to 2029 delivery purchase orders. Traditionally, fab tool talks start 6–12 months before delivery, with arrival 3–6 months after a PO; amid AI-driven capex, some lead times have more than doubled, with certain tools taking two years or longer. ASML said last month it saw no lithography supply disruption through 2028 while still weighing further capacity expansion. Industry executives called three-years-ahead delivery talks unusual and likely to persist toward 2030.

**Links:**

- [ETNews — Semiconductor Equipment Orders Stretch Three Years Ahead Amid AI Boom](https://en.etnews.com/20261104200007)

**Commentary:** The advanced-capacity race now bottlenecks at the tool calendar—miss the slot, miss the next wafer share.

### 3. Citi cuts lithium outlook by about 40% as carbonate nears cost floor; near-term battery glut risk (Batteries)
**Summary:** Bloomberg reported on Nov 2 that Citigroup cut two-year lithium price targets: three-month carbonate to about $12,000/t from $20,000 (about −40%), hydroxide to about $12,000 from $17,000, and spodumene to about $1,200 from $2,000, with estimates lowered through end-2028. Analysts cited slower battery-demand growth, trade uncertainty, and macro headwinds—a “lithium reset”—expecting supply to improve in 2027 as buyers defer purchases and keep lean inventories. Guangzhou’s most-active carbonate contract traded near 91,160 yuan/t that morning, down more than about 40% since late August. SunSirs put battery-grade carbonate at about 93,000 RMB/t as of Nov 3, down about 22% from about 120,000 RMB/t a month earlier and approaching cost for some non-integrated producers.

**Links:**

## II. Capacity Layout & Migration

### 4. John Deere to close two Iowa plants, shifting lines to Des Moines and Louisiana (Ag equipment)
**Summary:** DTN reported on Nov 4 that John Deere will close plants in Ottumwa and Clarion (Hagie), Iowa, over the coming years. Windrower production moves to Des Moines by end-2027 and round balers by end-2028, after which Ottumwa closes; Hagie STS sprayer production shifts to Thibodaux, Louisiana, from 2028, with Clarion manufacturing ending by end-2029. At “normal” volumes, Deere told DTN about 300 positions would be affected, though current ag-economy output is far below normal. The company also plans to move sugar-cane harvester production from Louisiana to Catalão, Brazil, in 2029 (Louisiana Economic Development cited about 350–400 jobs), shift small planters from Moline, Illinois, to Paton, Iowa, in 2027, and is evaluating strategic sourcing in construction and forestry.

**Links:**

- [DTN — John Deere Announces Iowa Plant Closures, Production Moves Through 2029](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2026/11/04/john-deere-announces-iowa-plant-2029)

**Commentary:** Ag OEMs are aligning capacity to regional demand and high-utilization plants—U.S. manufacturing commitments and local job hits often arrive together.

### 5. Schaeffler to cut 3,460 European jobs and close five sites; ~9% of German core roles (Auto supply chain)
**Summary:** Schaeffler announced on Nov 3 (covered through Nov 4 by Reuters, CNBC, and manufacturing media) a further performance program cutting a net 3,460 European positions and closing five sites—four in Germany plus Herstal, Belgium—across 16 locations. About 2,660 German jobs will go, roughly 9% of a core workforce of 31,957 as of Sept 30, 2026; including prior programs and about 1,800 partial-retirement deals, the cumulative impact can approach about 18%. ICE-related plants such as Dortmund (~430 people) and Morbach (~160) are among closures. The company targets about €300 million in annual earnings improvement from 2030, with one-off costs of about €480 million, mostly booked in 2026. CEO Klaus Rosenfeld said German personnel costs are roughly 2.5× those in other European countries, citing weak vehicle demand, slower EV uptake, and competition.

### 6. Reshoring Initiative: 2026 reshoring and FDI job announcements may rise ~25% to ~309,000 (Reshoring)
**Summary:** MHL News reported on Nov 4 that the Reshoring Initiative’s 2025 annual report plus 2026 first-half data show about 247,000 U.S. manufacturing jobs announced via reshoring and FDI in 2025, roughly flat with 2024, and more than 2.3 million cumulative from 2010–2025. The 2026 survey found contract manufacturers quoting reshoring projects rising from about 16% to 33%; policy uncertainty remained the top concern (about 57%), while OEM use of total-cost-of-ownership analysis rose from about 30% to 40%.

## III. Policy, Logistics & Geopolitics

### 7. China proposes 70% domestic-content rule for industrial robots to win procurement preference (Policy)
**Summary:** MediaNama reported on Nov 4 that MIIT and the Ministry of Finance opened consultation on Oct 30 (comments due Nov 13) on five draft standards defining “domestic products” for government procurement. Industrial robots, gas analyzers, and water-treatment equipment would need at least about 70% of component cost from China-made parts; helicopters about 60% and field-emission SEMs about 50%. Qualifying domestic bids can receive a roughly 20% price deduction in evaluation. The robot draft covers fixed industrial robots with at least three controlled joints, lists 19 component groups, and requires three processes in China—including specified harmonic/RV reducer work, servo-motor winding insulation, and PCB soldering—with a proposed three-year transition.

**Links:**

- [MediaNama — China proposes 70% domestic content rule for industrial robots](https://world.medianama.com/china-proposes-70-domestic-content-rule-for-industrial-robots-to-qualify-for-government-procurement-benefits/)

**Commentary:** Localization is dropping from finished-goods labels into component cost sheets—foreign and JV BOM and process footprints must be reworked.

### 8. Anti-involution shifts to accounting, payment terms, and rebates; automakers pledge ~60-day supplier pay (Auto supply chain)
**Summary:** Trivium China analyzed on Nov 4 that after limited success fighting price wars directly, China’s anti-involution push is targeting accounting rules, supply-chain payment terms, and tax tools. New auto industry cost-accounting standards make below-cost sales easier to spot. Under regulatory pressure, large OEMs have pledged to cut supplier payment terms to about 60 days from an industry average near 170, with a roughly 30-day target for SME suppliers. The finance ministry plans to cut the NEV export VAT rebate from 13% to 9% from April 2027, with insiders expecting eventual phase-out. Similar levers are spreading to solar and batteries, aiming to raise the cost of loss-leading volume and restore margins and cash flow.

**Links:**

- [Trivium China — China’s anti-involution campaign gets under the hood](https://triviumchina.com/research/chinas-anti-involution-campaign-gets-under-the-hood/)

**Commentary:** Cutting OEM payment float returns cash to the parts chain—enforcement will decide whether the price war actually ends.

### 10. Bangladesh garment unrest enters day five; wage board agrees new structure from Dec 1 (Apparel supply chain)
**Summary:** SCMR’s Nov 4 briefing and local/international coverage said Bangladesh wage protests have forced roughly 500–700 garment factories offline at peaks, affecting nearly one million workers and disrupting cargo flows from Dhaka to Chittagong during peak export season. Workers seek a rise from about 12,500 taka to about 30,000 taka monthly (~140%). On Nov 4, the newly formed minimum-wage board’s first meeting agreed a revised structure would take effect Dec 1, with higher pay reflected in January for December wages, though the exact increase remains unset; a follow-up meeting was eyed around Nov 9. BGMEA/BKMEA urged a return to work. Buyers are questioning shipments, some cancelled factory visits, and orders may divert to India, Vietnam, Pakistan, Egypt, and Indonesia.

### 11. October LMI rises to 70.7 as inventory and aggregate logistics costs hit ~four-year highs (Logistics)
**Summary:** The October Logistics Managers’ Index printed 70.7, up 0.5 from September’s 70.2 and the second-highest reading in four years; SCMR on Nov 4 and DC Velocity flagged it as a peak-season logistics storyline. Inventory levels kept expanding, with downstream retail stocking slightly ahead of upstream. Inventory Costs jumped 8.9 points to 88.8—the highest since March 2022. Warehousing and transportation capacity stayed in contraction (43.5 and 38.0), while warehousing prices rose to 80.7. Combined Aggregate Logistics Costs reached 257.5 on a 0–300 scale, the fastest expansion since March 2022, pointing to continued supply-driven inflation risk.
