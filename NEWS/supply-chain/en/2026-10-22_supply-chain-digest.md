## I. Chips and Equipment

### 1. TSMC surveys suppliers on rare-earth-free motors; talks remain early (rare earths, equipment)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on October 22, citing three people familiar with the matter, that TSMC is exploring alternatives to rare-earth components in factory equipment so China's export controls are less likely to disrupt plants that make the most advanced chips. Three industry executives briefed by the company said TSMC has asked suppliers about rare-earth-free motors for factory automation, and one person said the driver was concern about supply disruptions from China. The same report recalls Economy Minister Kung Ming-hsin's February statement that an ITRI pilot line could, within three years, meet about 50 percent of Taiwan's domestic demand, while ITRI told Reuters only that it is planning a rare-earth project with the industry ministry.

### 4. Unimicron warns of double-booking in AI substrate capacity (substrates)
**Summary:** DIGITIMES reported on October 22 that Unimicron executive president Timothy Lan said AI demand is pushing the printed-circuit-board supply chain into equipment constraints, shorter development cycles, and overlapping capacity commitments. He said substrate makers have to strengthen supply-chain resilience and compress time to market at the same time, and that bottlenecks are shifting from materials toward equipment, especially precision imaging, inspection, and surface treatment. Building a PCB plant and bringing it into production takes at least 2.5 to 3 years. He warned that double-booking is hard to avoid when demand exceeds available capacity, and that competing AI customers, system designs, and advanced packaging such as CoWoS and EMIB can reserve the same capacity more than once, passing risk from end customers to board makers and then to material and equipment suppliers.

**Links:**

- [DIGITIMES — Unimicron warns of double-booking risks as AI PCB capacity race strains supply chains](https://www.digitimes.com/news/a20261022PD231/unimicron-pcb-demand-capacity.html)

**Commentary:** The 2.5-to-3-year span is the build cycle he cited. Double-booking is a risk warning, not a disclosed order cancellation or a capital-spending figure.

### 5. U.S. Commerce signs letters of intent for up to $389 million in power-semiconductor R&D (chips)
**Summary:** On October 22 the U.S. Department of Commerce said it had signed letters of intent with four companies for up to $389 million in CHIPS Act R&D awards for power semiconductors and materials used in the grid, data centers, and fusion. Final funding depends on the R&D being carried out, and the department will take a minority, non-controlling, non-voting equity stake in each company. Helion is lined up for up to $175 million for high-voltage power semiconductors and pulsed-power modules, and Commonwealth Fusion Systems for up to $85 million covering radio-frequency plasma heating, power electronics, diagnostic embedded electronics, and radiation-hardened electronics. Vertical Semiconductor is lined up for up to $73 million to advance vertical gallium nitride devices and demonstrate what it calls the first 2.5 MW super-intelligence data-center rack, and Sibelco for up to $56 million on ultra-high-purity quartz purification and finishing for crystal-growth crucibles and related hardware.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** All four figures are "up to" amounts in letters of intent, not checks already paid. The 2.5 MW rack is Vertical Semiconductor's demonstration target.

## II. Rare Earths and Lithium

### 6. Malaysia clears a first shipment of unprocessed rare earths; the applicant is unnamed (rare earths)
**Summary:** Mining Weekly reported on October 22, citing Bloomberg, that Malaysia approved a batch of unprocessed rare earths for export, the first permit under a framework meant to allow test shipments and draw investment into local capacity. The natural-resources ministry told Bloomberg that the Department of Mineral and Geoscience had cleared one research-and-development application and did not name the applicant or the volume. Guidelines issued earlier this month ease a raw-export pause in place since 2024, so unprocessed material can move for research or processing when conditions are met, including by foreign applicants; the ministry said it will weigh investment quality, technology transfer, and long-term value rather than the number of approvals. The report also notes that Australia's Lynas already operates in Malaysia and that France's Carester plans a separation plant in Perak with Malaco.

**Links:**

- [Mining Weekly (Bloomberg) — Malaysia allows first export of rare rare earths under new rules](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/malaysia-allows-first-export-of-rare-rare-earths-under-new-rules-2026-10-22)

**Commentary:** What can be checked that day is one unnamed research-export permit. It is not a commercial opening of raw-ore exports, and no tonnage was given.

### 8. Benchmark: storage lithium demand may slow to about 5 percent a year; 2050 equals 134 new mines (lithium)
**Summary:** Energy Storage News reported on October 22 that Benchmark Mineral Intelligence expects lithium demand from stationary storage, after rising more than 60 percent a year for two years, to slow to about 5 percent a year through 2030, with electric vehicles rising from 55 percent of global lithium demand today to 76 percent by 2050 and batteries already about 92 percent of demand in 2026. In the base case, lithium-carbonate-equivalent demand rises from 2.11 million tonnes in 2026 to about 7 million tonnes in 2050, equal to 134 new mines at 36,000 tonnes a year, including 44 mines for 2040-2050 growth, and more than $100 billion of investment before recycling. An upside case is near 9 million tonnes and more than 180 mines, and recycling could approach half of supply by 2050. Australia's mined share is forecast to fall from 29 percent in 2026 to 20 percent in 2050, China's to stay near 25 percent, North America's to rise from 1.7 percent to 13 percent, and Europe's from under 1 percent to 5.5 percent.

**Links:**

- [Energy Storage News — Lithium demand from storage expected to slow to 5% annual growth](https://www.ess-news.com/2026/10/22/lithium-demand-from-storage-expected-to-slow-to-5-annual-growth/)

**Commentary:** The 5 percent pace, the 134 mines, the $100 billion, and the regional shares are Benchmark forecasts, not a list of mines already approved.

### 12. U.S. factory job gains are concentrated in advanced manufacturing (manufacturing)
**Summary:** Axios reported on October 22 that U.S. factory output rose 0.3 percent in September while auto output fell 3 percent, and that the ISM manufacturing index expanded for a ninth month. ## IV. Trade and Logistics

### 13. Interview: China's trade beat early forecasts, and a shift to Southeast Asia can raise trade (logistics)
**Summary:** Air Cargo News on October 22 published an interview with Steven Altman, who leads the DHL Initiative on Globalization at NYU Stern. He said China's 2025 trade volume grew about twice as fast as the forecast made at the start of that year, that the 2026 forecast has been revised to growth faster than 2025, and that imports have strengthened while exports have shifted toward electric and hybrid vehicles, technology products, intermediate goods, and capital goods. He called Southeast Asia a leading trade-growth hotspot for the next decade, with Vietnam and Indonesia particularly strong, and said the next DHL Trade Atlas is due in March 2027. His view is that moving production from China to Vietnam, Indonesia, Thailand, or Malaysia often increases trade, because those economies lack China's supplier base and must import more inputs.

**Links:**

- [Air Cargo News — China remains strong driver of trade and air cargo](https://www.aircargonews.net/supply-chains/2026/10/china-remains-strong-driver-of-trade-and-air-cargo/)

**Commentary:** "About twice as fast" and the upgraded 2026 forecast are Altman's reading of existing trade forecasts. The interview did not publish a new customs growth rate.
