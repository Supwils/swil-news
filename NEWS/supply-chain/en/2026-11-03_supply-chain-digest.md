## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Memory prices roughly double as makers prioritize data centers over PCs and consumer electronics (Memory)
**Summary:** Semafor reported on Nov 3, citing Nikkei and others, that memory chip prices have roughly doubled over the past year, and that Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix are prioritizing data-center customers at the expense of household-appliance and PC makers. Korean suppliers are locking in roughly five-year deals with dozens of clients; Samsung aims to put as much as about 70% of capacity under such agreements. ABN AMRO told clients that overcapacity is “disappearing like snow in the sun.” Combined with AI infrastructure demand, consumer-side buyers face longer lead times and higher procurement costs.

**Commentary:** AI orders are rewriting allocation tables—PCs and consumer electronics are no longer the default primary customer.

### 2. Samsung reportedly to exit 8-inch foundry by end-2030 as TSMC also cuts mature capacity (Mature nodes)
**Summary:** TrendForce on Nov 3, citing The Elec, said Samsung has notified customers of plans to exit the 8-inch foundry business entirely by end-2030, shifting labor and equipment to advanced 12-inch processes. Samsung stopped taking new 8-inch product orders from early 2026, covering roughly 65nm–180nm nodes used for PMICs, DDIs, MCUs, and image sensors. TrendForce expects capacity cuts by Samsung and TSMC, plus AI-related power-chip demand, to push average 8-inch utilization among the top 10 foundries to nearly 90% in 2026, up from about 80% in 2025. Separate reports say TSMC plans to transfer about 80% of its roughly 5 million wafers/year of 8-inch capacity in phases to affiliate Vanguard International Semiconductor (VIS).

**Links:**

- [TrendForce — Samsung Reportedly to Exit 8-Inch Foundry by 2030 as TSMC Also Cuts Capacity](https://www.trendforce.com/news/2026/11/03/news-samsung-reportedly-to-exit-8-inch-foundry-by-2030-as-tsmc-also-cuts-capacity/)

**Commentary:** Mature nodes are becoming scarce goods—power and analog customers will face earlier fab switches, redesigns, and requalification costs.

The same day, Korean media said Samsung plans HBM base-die production at its second Taylor fab for AMD and Nvidia, though timing, volumes, and commercial agreements remain unconfirmed.

### 4. REalloys and U.S. Army execute non-binding term sheet for heavy rare-earth plant at Tooele (Rare earths)
**Summary:** REalloys announced on Nov 3 that a subsidiary and the U.S. Army executed a non-binding term sheet for a potential Enhanced Use Lease on about 200 acres at Tooele Army Depot in Utah to develop a heavy rare-earth processing facility for dysprosium and terbium used in defense magnets. The framework contemplates an initial 50-year lease with one 25-year renewal option (up to about 75 years); the Army would have a right of first offer to buy product but no purchase obligation. A definitive lease still depends on rent valuation, environmental permitting, FOCI review, financing, board approval, and Congressional notifications; either party may terminate if conditions are not met within 18 months.

**Links:**

- [REalloys — Term sheet for heavy rare earth processing at Tooele Army Depot](https://realloys.com/realloys-press-release/realloys-and-u-s-army-execute-term-sheet-for-heavy-rare-earth-processing-facility-at-tooele-army-depot/)

**Commentary:** Defense rare-earth onshoring has reached site-and-lease talks, but a non-binding term sheet is a reminder that permits and capital—not press releases—decide delivery.

### 5. Japan’s Sojitz plans yttrium imports from Malaysia from 2027 amid China’s export squeeze (Rare earths)
**Summary:** RAWMATERIALS reporting on Nov 3, drawing on Nikkei Asia, said Japanese trading house Sojitz plans to begin importing yttrium from Malaysia as early as 2027, supplied by Lynas Rare Earths’ expanding Malaysian processing facilities; samarium, gadolinium, and lutetium are also in future sourcing plans. China’s April 2025 export controls on yttrium and related products are the backdrop: USGS estimates Chinese yttrium-related exports nearly halved between 2024 and 2025, while Chinese yttrium-oxide shipments to Japan fell from about 51 metric tons in September 2025 to about 4 tons in September 2026—roughly a 92% drop. Lynas’ new Malaysian unit is designed for up to about 5,000 metric tons of heavy rare-earth feedstock a year and about 1,100 tons of nominal yttrium processing capacity, with investment estimates near $205 million; under the partnership, up to about 75% of Lynas’ medium- and heavy-rare-earth output may be available to Japan.

**Links:**

- [RAWMATERIALS.net — Yttrium Supply Crunch: Japan Turns to Malaysia](https://rawmaterials.net/yttrium-supply-crunch-japan-turns-to-malaysia/)

**Commentary:** The yttrium squeeze is translating export-license risk into alternative-origin investment—Japan’s buying calendar is already rebased to a 2027 supply window.

## II. Capacity Footprints & Reshoring

### 6. Lonza to acquire five U.S. Merck KGaA CDMO sites, expanding from four to nine U.S. plants (Biomanufacturing)
**Summary:** Lonza said on Nov 3 it signed a definitive agreement to buy five U.S. CDMO manufacturing sites and about 1,100 employees from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany, at an enterprise valuation in the low- to mid-triple-digit millions of Swiss francs; the CEO indicated a midpoint of a roughly CHF 100–600 million range. Sites include St. Louis (bioconjugates), Madison and Verona, Wisconsin (high-potency payload-linkers), Carlsbad, California (viral vectors), and Indianapolis (lipid nanoparticles and aseptic fill-finish), lifting Lonza’s U.S. CDMO footprint from four to nine sites and U.S. headcount above 6,000. Acquired sites are expected to contribute about CHF 250 million in sales in 2028; closing is targeted for mid-2027.

### 7. 3M has closed 16 plants and DCs this fiscal year, with changes planned at 10 more sites (Network optimization)
**Summary:** Supply Chain Dive reported on Nov 3 that CEO William Brown said on the Oct 20 earnings call that 3M has closed 16 manufacturing sites and distribution centers since the start of the fiscal year and will make changes at 10 more facilities over the next 12 months, aiming for about $1 billion in annual productivity gains by end-2027. The company also wants to cut days of inventory on hand from the high 90s to the mid-70s (about $35 million per day) and is investing more than $250 million over three years in standardized automation. Brown has previously warned that factory-closure paybacks can take three to five years.

## III. Battery Feedstock, Logistics & Policy Calendars

### 11. Severe Indonesia drought slows nickel refining, pressuring China’s EV battery materials chain (Battery feedstock)
**Summary:** South China Morning Post reported on Nov 3 that a severe drought in Indonesia is slowing output of nickel used in lithium-ion battery components, creating risks across China’s vast electric-vehicle supply chain. Indonesia is the world’s largest nickel production hub; CRU Group said the cutbacks could “add uncertainty … across the battery materials value chain.” Drought constrains hydropower and process water for refining, a classic weather shock transmitting into battery cost and delivery risk.

**Links:**

### 13. Pentagon’s China rare-earth magnet ban clocks toward Jan 1, 2027; industry says new supply can take a decade (Policy)
**Summary:** The Oregon Group on Nov 3 noted that in under about 60 days—on Jan 1, 2027—the Pentagon is set to stop buying rare-earth magnets, tungsten, and tantalum mined, refined, or processed in China. Aerospace Industries Association messaging cited in the piece says a new non-Chinese mine or refinery can take up to about 10 years to identify, build, and certify, with significant U.S. aerospace-grade processing and magnet capacity not expected until 2028 or later, plus another roughly 12–24 months of qualification. Some defense suppliers report source-transition costs above about $10 million, and one has stockpiled at more than about 30 times historic prices; industry is seeking roughly two-year waivers for magnets, tungsten, and germanium. By comparison, U.S. demand for a common rare-earth magnet type was roughly 48,000 metric tons versus domestic supply near 300 tons in a recent benchmark year.

**Links:**

- [The Oregon Group — Pentagon’s China rare earth ban starts on Jan 1, but new supply can take 10 years](https://theoregongroup.com/latest-news/pentagon-china-minerals-ban-supply-10-years/)

**Commentary:** The policy calendar is running ahead of the industrial calendar—before the ban hits, buyers are stuck choosing among waivers, stockpiles, and alternative qualification.
