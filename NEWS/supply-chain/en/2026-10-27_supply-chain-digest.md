## I. Chip Packaging and Manufacturing Demand

### 1. TSMC reportedly weighs CoWoS price hikes of up to about 20% as tool lead times stretch to six to eight months (chips)
**Summary:** TrendForce reported on October 27, citing Commercial Times supply-chain sources, that TSMC is considering raising CoWoS quotes by as much as about 20% as larger AI accelerators keep advanced packaging tight; the same report said foundries and OSAT providers are preparing another 10% to 20% CoWoS price round. Seoul Economic Daily, citing Nomura Securities, said final testing for Nvidia Blackwell takes roughly four times as long as Hopper and Rubin could take about seven times as long, further squeezing packaging and test capacity. Equipment lead times have lengthened to about six to eight months from three to six months, and Commercial Times said TSMC reportedly placed tool orders at existing prices this year without seeking discounts or free upgrades. Analysts estimate TSMC's monthly CoWoS capacity will exceed 130,000 wafers in 2026 and target about 200,000 in 2027; TSMC has not formally confirmed a price increase.

### 2. U.S. core capital-goods orders rose another 0.5% in September as computer-related orders jumped about 27% year over year (manufacturing demand)
**Summary:** The Commerce Department's Census Bureau said on October 27 that new orders for core capital goods excluding defense and aircraft rose 0.5% in September, extending a five-month streak, while core capital-goods shipments rose 1.2%, feeding GDP estimates of equipment investment. Axios the same day said orders for computers and related products such as servers, storage, and peripherals rose about 27% from a year earlier, including a nearly 4% increase in September alone, a yearly pace not matched since January 1997. Overall durable-goods orders rose 1.8%, led by commercial aircraft, and orders excluding transportation rose 0.5%. William Blair's Richard de Chazal said investment-led demand has run ahead of what factories can deliver since spring, with unfilled orders rising every month for more than a year.

## II. Critical Materials, Battery Recycling, and Capacity Rollout

### 3. Clarios plans to acquire Ecobat's three U.S. critical-mineral recovery plants that process more than 40 million batteries a year (critical materials)
**Summary:** Ecobat said on October 27 that Clarios plans to acquire its U.S. critical-mineral recovery and processing business, including plants in City of Industry, California; Indianapolis, Indiana; and Middletown, New York, plus the Dallas office. The three sites together process more than 40 million batteries a year, recovering lead, tin, antimony, and other materials; closing is expected by early calendar 2027, subject to customary conditions and regulatory approvals. Clarios framed the deal within its about $6 billion U.S. energy manufacturing strategy, including a $1.9 billion commitment to domestic critical-mineral recycling and processing; Ecobat said the sale completes a portfolio shift begun in 2025 that included ten divestitures spanning seventeen sites in six countries over roughly sixteen months.

**Links:**

- [Ecobat — Clarios Strengthens U.S. Critical Mineral Supply Chain with Acquisition of Ecobat](https://ecobat.com/2026/10/clarios-strengthens-u-s-critical-mineral-supply-chain-with-acquisition-of-ecobat/)

**Commentary:** The deal buys operating recovery capacity, underscoring a push to loop lead-acid and critical minerals back into U.S. manufacturing.

### 5. South Carolina Ports moves Isuzu's first production-equipment container to Inland Port Greer (capacity migration)
**Summary:** Railway Age reported on October 27 that South Carolina Ports has moved the first container of production equipment for Isuzu North America Corp.'s new U.S. plant from the Port of Charleston by rail to Inland Port Greer. Isuzu chose Greenville County in 2025 partly for the Charleston–Greer link; the plant is expected to start in 2027, building N-Series gas and electric trucks and F-Series diesel trucks. ### 6. China's industrial profits rose 14.4% year over year in September, with AI-linked manufacturing far ahead of consumer sectors (manufacturing cycle)
**Summary:** CNBC reported on October 27 that National Bureau of Statistics data showed profits at large industrial firms rose 14.4% year over year in September, the strongest pace since June, and climbed 15.5% for January–September, slightly below the prior 15.7%. An NBS statistician tied most of the gain to electronics and high-tech manufacturing: profits more than tripled for electronic specialty materials makers and rose 51.7% for semiconductor device equipment makers; optical-fiber makers saw profits more than sixfold higher and cable makers more than doubled, while copper and aluminum raw-material profits rose nearly 45%. Retail sales grew only 1.1% in September, and consumer-related industries such as clothing, autos, and furniture lagged; analysts said exports and the AI cycle cannot fully offset weak domestic demand under the property drag.

**Links:**

### 8. Mexican truckers reach a deal for talks with the Interior Ministry after protests hit at least 19 states (logistics)
**Summary:** FreightWaves reported on October 27 that Mexico's National Chamber of Freight Transport (CANACAR) and other groups agreed with the Interior Ministry to open four high-level working sessions on highway security, infrastructure, tax incentives, regulations, and operating costs, seeking to resolve nationwide demonstrations that began Monday over cargo theft, violent highway robberies, extortion, and rising costs. ### 10. TiL says sudden tariff announcements, more than tariff levels, scramble box terminals as cargo shifts from Canadian to U.S. ports (logistics)
**Summary:** The Loadstar reported on October 27 from TOC Americas in Cartagena that Tom van Eynde, TiL Group's director for terminal investments in North America, said tariffs are relocating container trade rather than destroying it, with some flows that once entered the U.S. via Canadian ports now landing at TiL's U.S. terminals. He expected little volume impact once tariffs settle around 10% to 15%; the sharper hit, he said, comes from sudden announcements that trigger pre-deadline cargo rushes carriers can meet with extra ships, while terminals need years to plan and permit capacity that may arrive after demand has moved again. Extra gangs and equipment can absorb peaks but raise yard and rail congestion and cost per box, eating into margins.

**Links:**

- [The Loadstar — Tariff announcements cause cargo chaos for box terminals, says TiL](https://theloadstar.com/tariff-anouncements-cause-cargo-chaos-for-box-terminals-says-til/)

**Commentary:** For terminal operators, the cadence of policy shocks is harder to absorb than the level of the duty itself.
