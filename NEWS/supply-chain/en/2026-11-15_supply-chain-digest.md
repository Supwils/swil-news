## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. AI data-center demand spills over: industrial and power chips tighten; TI Korea stresses in-house capacity (Industrial chips)
**Summary:** CHOSUNBIZ reported on Nov 15 that Texas Instruments Korea head Park Jung-seo said AI data-center demand has moved beyond memory and advanced nodes into power-management, cooling-control and embedded chips, with industrial equipment and auto recovery adding pressure on mature-node supply. He noted that general-purpose and real-time MCUs, plus communications and radar chips, mostly run on 28–130 nm nodes, while industry capex is concentrating on memory and 2–3 nm advanced fabs—so more investment does not automatically lift industrial-chip supply. PCB, MLCC and related semiconductors are also tightening. TI leans on about 15 fabrication, assembly and test sites across eight countries and regions, including the United States, Germany and Japan, and is ramping 300 mm wafer production for supply and cost competitiveness.

**Commentary:** AI is not only a GPU story—the mature nodes that feed power, cooling and MCUs are becoming the next invisible bottleneck.

## II. Rare Earths, Critical Minerals & Great-Power Leverage

### 3. Reuters: Trump’s minerals push has yet to loosen China’s grip ahead of Xi talks; U.S. manufacturers still highly dependent (Critical minerals)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 15 from Shenzhen/Houston that Trump meets Xi this week with a core trade goal unfinished: building a domestic critical-minerals supply to counter Beijing. Despite billions of dollars in federal support, many U.S. mining, refining and processing projects remain years from production, leaving manufacturers dependent on Chinese metals, magnets and compounds for semiconductors, robotics, weapons and AI. IEA figures put U.S. neodymium-iron-boron magnet capacity at roughly 4,500 metric tons a year in 2025 versus about 210,000 in China. Examples include a Korea Zinc–backed ~$7.4 billion Tennessee refinery not due until 2030, Thacker Pass lithium around 2028, and Alaska’s Trilogy copper project around 2031. The leaders may discuss extending a trade truce due in January that includes export-control waivers for certain critical minerals.

### 4. Yttrium oxide ~125× more expensive in Europe than China: heavy-REE licensing still on; offshore premiums signal pre-summit strain (Rare earths)
**Summary:** MINING.COM, OilPrice and others, citing Argus and related assessments, report yttrium oxide near about $1.15 million a ton in Europe versus about $9,144 in China—roughly a 125-fold gap—with dysprosium and terbium also carrying multi-fold offshore premiums. China’s Nov 9 extension of some export-control pauses through Jan 10, 2027 does not lift April 2025 licensing on seven medium and heavy rare earths (samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium). Customs data show sharp drops in yttrium oxide shipments to Japan in the first nine months of 2026; yttrium is used in semiconductor processes and turbine-blade coatings, while alternative capacity (e.g., Lynas) is largely not scaled until around 2028. U.S. officials have already complained that rare-earth shipment pace lags commitments.

**Summary:** The Business Standard and others reported on Nov 15 that BGMEA said about 121 garment factories in Savar, Ashulia and Gazipur could not run normally that day; indefinite closures under Section 13(1) of the Bangladesh Labour Act rose to about 65 (from about 55 the prior day), with others on shutdown or “general holiday” status under “no work, no pay.” Workers are demanding a monthly minimum near Tk 32,000 versus a current minimum of about Tk 12,500; the wage board’s third meeting is set for Nov 16, with employers’ proposal reported around Tk 15,000. BBC and others said nearly 150 sites across the textile hubs were affected; the industry generates nearly $40 billion in annual exports. Brands cannot translate plant counts into lost output without plant-level order and restart data.

### 9. Durban congestion: container delays near 20 days; Metair pegs extra costs at 20–25% of prior-year EBITDA, may invoke force majeure (Ports / auto parts)
**Summary:** Splash247 on Nov 15 and Metair’s Johannesburg Stock Exchange update say Durban Gateway Terminal’s new terminal operating system, equipment failures and limited crane availability have pushed container delays toward 20 days; the terminal is run by ICTSI with Transnet. Meaningful improvement may not arrive before end-Q1 2027; prolonged disruption could force force-majeure notices. Shares had already tumbled on the warning.
