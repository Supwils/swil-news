## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. SMIC: Capacity may stay tight through 2027 into mid-to-late 2028; tool lead times stretch to 16–24 months (Foundry)
**Summary:** TrendForce, Reuters and the Global Times reported on Nov 13 that SMIC’s third-quarter net profit attributable to shareholders rose about 123.4% year on year to roughly $428.4 million, with revenue of about $3.19 billion (up about 33.9% YoY). Utilization climbed to 97.3% from 93.7% in the prior quarter, and gross margin reached about 29.9%; China accounted for about 90.8% of quarterly sales. Co-CEO Zhao Haijun said the industry broadly expects a severe capacity crunch in 2027 that may last into mid-to-late 2028; AI-related memory could roughly balance after mid-2028, while computing and supporting chips may remain short. He also warned that tight equipment supply and panic ordering have stretched new-tool quotes to about 16–24 months, which could disrupt full-year capital spending even as 2026 expansion stays on track. SMIC said it would adjust pricing with the market and prioritize the tightest segments; full-year revenue is expected above $12 billion (nearly +30% YoY), with Q4 revenue guidance of about 4–6% sequential growth and gross margin easing to roughly 26–28%.

### 3. Infineon processes functional 300mm SiC power devices at Villach using existing silicon tools; ~2.3x more chips per wafer (SiC)
**Summary:** Infineon announced on Nov 9—and the milestone was widely cited in Nov 13 industry weeklies—that it has fabricated functional power devices from 300-millimeter silicon-carbide wafers at its Villach, Austria fab in a full-scale semiconductor environment, using existing silicon manufacturing equipment. The company calls it an industry-first milestone of this kind. Versus 200mm wafers, the larger diameter can yield about 2.3 times as many chips, potentially lowering unit manufacturing costs for automotive, industrial and AI data-center power devices. The progress lands amid elevated SiC demand and mixed expansion plans across the industry, and is a signal for longer-run supply elasticity in power semiconductors.

**Links:**

## II. Rare Earths & Critical Minerals

### 4. Benchmark: More ex-China rare-earth milestones, but separation remains the bottleneck—China ~97% of 2026 Dy/Tb oxide separation (Rare earths)
**Summary:** Benchmark Mineral Intelligence analyzed on Nov 13 that three ex-China rare-earth developers have announced production milestones in recent weeks, spanning sintered magnets in Europe and pilot oxide, metal and magnet output in the United States—adding capability at several supply-chain stages. Benchmark data show the constraint remains separation rather than magnet making, with China accounting for about 97% of separated dysprosium and terbium oxide supply in 2026. Downstream magnet pilots therefore do not automatically equal independent midstream heavy-rare-earth capability; separation capacity and qualification remain the hardest non-Chinese gap to close.

**Links:**

- [Benchmark Minerals — Will recent rare earth developer milestones boost ex-China supply](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/will-recent-european-and-us-rare-earth-developer-milestones-move-the-dial-on-ex-china-supply-)

**Commentary:** Magnet plants can go up first; separation plants decide whether you can truly diversify—midstream chemistry is still rare earths’ geopolitical choke point.

### 5. REalloys signs U.S. Army term sheet at Tooele: targets 2028 heavy-rare-earth metallization and ~3,000 tpy U.S. magnets by 2030 (Rare earths)
**Summary:** REalloys reported on Nov 12–13 that it has executed a non-binding term sheet with the U.S. Army to negotiate an Enhanced Use Lease for a heavy rare-earth processing facility on about 200 acres at Tooele Army Depot in Utah; quarter-end cash was about $116.2 million. Its Heavy Rare Earth Metallization Facility is targeted for commissioning in Q1 2028 with about 50 tonnes a year of combined dysprosium and terbium oxide feedstock. The company expects North American NdPr metallization access via the Saskatchewan Research Council beginning in Q1 2027 and outlined a mine-to-magnet path toward about 3,000 tonnes a year of sintered NdFeB magnets in the United States no later than 2030. It stressed these are design targets, not commitments, with final siting and financing still undecided.

## III. Capacity Moves & Auto Supply Chains

### 6. Toyota rewires its China EV supply chain: beyond keiretsu, local content rising toward ~40–50% (Auto)
**Summary:** Nikkei Asia reported on Nov 13 that Toyota Motor is reshaping its China EV supply chain, looking beyond its traditional keiretsu network of affiliated Japanese suppliers toward more local companies to cut costs and keep pace with Chinese rivals. Japanese coverage of the strategy says Executive Vice President Yoichi Miyazaki has stressed that suppliers will be chosen on quality, capability and cost regardless of nationality or group affiliation; local-parts content that was about 10% on early EV models is rising to roughly 40–50% on next-generation programs under development, with Chinese rivals’ quotes used as cost benchmarks. The backdrop includes an about 25% drop in Toyota’s China sales in April–September to about 684,900 vehicles, while strong hybrid profits buy time for a China EV counteroffensive.

## IV. Logistics, Ports & Trade Resilience

### 10. Port of Durban system switch hits Metair: shares plunge ~34%; extra costs seen at 20–25% of EBITDA (Ports)
**Summary:** Business Day reported on Nov 13 that South African auto-components group Metair said rollout of a new terminal operating system at the Port of Durban has severely disrupted container handling; with equipment failures and limited crane availability, delays have at times approached 20 days, causing production interruptions, stock shortages and a temporary stoppage on one customer line. Airfreight used as a substitute since August has been “exorbitant.” Metair estimates additional costs for the financial year to December at about 20–25% of last year’s reported EBITDA before exceptional items (about R1.69 billion), costs it says are not recoverable from customers; shares fell about 34% in early trade. Durban is South Africa’s main container gateway; Metair said meaningful improvement may not come before the end of Q1 2027 and it may need to consider force-majeure provisions.

### 11. McKinsey: About a quarter of global imports face overlapping vulnerabilities; ~80% of seaborne trade passes chokepoints (Resilience)
**Summary:** Axios and the McKinsey Global Institute reported on Nov 12–13 that roughly one-quarter of about $19 trillion in global imports (Axios cites the share rising to about 23% in 2024 from about 15% in 2000) faces at least two of three vulnerabilities—product criticality, supplier concentration and geopolitical distance—described as trade’s “Achilles’ heel”; about 4% face all three, concentrated in goods such as semiconductors, permanent magnets and consumer electronics. About 80% of maritime trade passes through roughly two dozen major chokepoints listed in the report, several of them—including the Strait of Hormuz—with no viable seaborne alternative. Authors caution that shifting final assembly to a geopolitically closer country without fixing upstream concentration still leaves firms exposed.

**Links:**

- [Axios — Lurking trade vulnerabilities leave companies "exposed," per McKinsey](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/13/global-trade-supply-hormuz-mckinsey)
- [McKinsey — The Achilles’ heel of global trade](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/the-achilles-heel-of-global-trade-building-resilience-where-it-matters-most)

**Commentary:** Nearshoring is not the same as resilience—if critical inputs still sit behind one upstream vendor and one strait, moving the factory only relocates the risk.
