## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Mitsui Chemicals to acquire JSR for about ¥1.84 trillion: Japan’s photoresist leader folds into a materials group (Semiconductor materials)
**Summary:** Mitsui Chemicals announced on Nov 9 that it has signed a share purchase agreement with funds managed by JIC Capital to acquire all common shares of semiconductor-materials firm JSR jointly with the Development Bank of Japan (DBJ), at a deal value of about ¥1.84 trillion (roughly $11.6–12 billion)—among the largest Japanese chemicals M&A deals in recent years. DBJ plans to invest about ¥200 billion in cash for a stake convertible into non-voting preferred shares, while Mitsui Chemicals will hold 100% of voting rights and consolidate JSR. JSR is a leading global supplier of advanced photoresists, including EUV grades, with customers including TSMC, Intel and Samsung; closing still requires competition and foreign-investment clearances in Japan and abroad, with Mitsui targeting the second half of the fiscal year ending March 2028. Mitsui is also advancing a basic-chemicals carve-out and a stronger ICT materials portfolio.

### 2. China’s tungsten export curbs send AI PCB micro-drill prices soaring: China holds ~80% of global tungsten output (Critical metals)
**Summary:** TrendForce reported on Nov 9, citing Nikkei, that China’s export restrictions on tungsten and related critical metals have disrupted refining and powder supplies in Japan, Taiwan, the U.S. and Europe, sending prices for tungsten-carbide micro drill bits—used to bore vias in PCBs and chip substrates—up several-fold. AI server boards with more layers and harder materials are raising bit consumption; Taiwan’s Topoint Technology said each drilling set now needs about four to five bits, up from about three. USGS data cited in the reporting put China’s share of global tungsten production at about 79% in 2025; Shanghai Metals Market data show ammonium paratungstate (APT) prices rising from about RMB 212,750 per metric ton in February 2025 to more than RMB 530,000 in the week of the report. Chinese drill-bit makers are expanding on feedstock access, with China Tungsten High-Tech unit Shenzhen Jinzhou launching six capacity projects since 2025 aimed at adding nearly 800 million micro drill bits of capacity.

### 3. Microchip lead times stretch to as long as 32 weeks as AI buyers crowd OSAT capacity (Packaging & test)
**Summary:** Technology outlets following Microchip’s earnings call reported on Nov 9 that lead times for many standard products—historically four to eight weeks—have stretched to about six to 32 weeks. CEO Steve Sanghi traced the bottleneck to outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) houses, where large AI chipmakers have taken capacity, so even wafers from Microchip’s own fabs still queue for external packaging and test. The company is bringing some work in-house and qualifying new contractors in other countries, but qualifying a new supplier takes about six months and some added capacity will not arrive until the March and June 2027 quarters; management also expects major foundry and packaging suppliers to raise prices again in January 2027. September-quarter sales rose about 42% year over year, yet gross-margin guidance came under pressure and the shares fell sharply around Nov 6.

## II. Critical Materials, Batteries & Refining

### 4. China extends suspension of rare-earth, lithium-battery and graphite-anode export controls to Jan 10, 2027 (Export controls)
**Summary:** China’s Ministry of Commerce and the General Administration of Customs issued Announcement No. 58 of 2026 on Nov 9, keeping six October 2025 export-control measures—covering superhard materials, rare-earth equipment and inputs, selected medium and heavy rare earths, lithium batteries and artificial graphite anodes, rare-earth items outside China, and rare-earth technologies—suspended from Nov 11 through Jan 10, 2027, bridging the prior pause that was due to expire Nov 10. Reuters reported the same day that the step implements the extended grace period in the U.S.–China trade arrangement; a MOFCOM spokesperson also said measures against certain U.S. entities on the export-control and unreliable-entity lists, plus countermeasures against selected U.S. subsidiaries of Hanwha Ocean, stay suspended for about two more months. Analysts note that April 2025 controls on seven medium and heavy rare earths are not in this suspension set, so buyers still need to check HS codes and licensing.

### 5. India clears about Rs 2,814 crore in lithium and nickel refining support, targeting the battery midstream (Refining)
**Summary:** India Today reported on Nov 9 that the Union cabinet on Nov 4 approved a roughly Rs 2,814 crore Capex Support Scheme for critical-mineral processing to build four large lithium and nickel refineries, including the country’s first commercial-scale lithium plants: two lithium units of at least about 30,000 tons a year each and two nickel units of at least about 50,000 tons each, with about 30% of eligible capital costs subsidized (capped near Rs 500 crore per lithium plant and Rs 900 crore per nickel plant). If executed, the plan targets about 60,000 tons of lithium and 100,000 tons of nickel capacity by about 2030 and roughly Rs 10,000 crore of investment. The cabinet also approved about Rs 14,500 crore in viability-gap funding for roughly 92 GWh of grid-scale storage with domestic-content requirements. The cabinet note itself cites capital intensity, technology access and raw-material constraints; China still controls about 65% of global battery-grade lithium-chemical refining capacity.

### 9. Bangkok Suvarnabhumi cargo backlog spreads to exports as airlines restrict inbound freight (Air cargo)
**Summary:** The Loadstar reported on Nov 9 that a backlog at Thai Airways’ cargo warehouse at Bangkok Suvarnabhumi is disrupting imports and exports, prompting airlines to restrict Bangkok-bound freight. Airline accounts on Thursday cited more than about 1,000 outstanding cargo lots and roughly 700–800 pallets awaiting breakdown; Thai Airways acknowledged rising inbound volumes left many ULDs awaiting breakdown and storage, delaying deliveries, and said it was prioritizing shipments that arrived before Oct 23. The Thai Chamber of Commerce warned that delays are raising costs and disrupting production for manufacturers that rely on imported materials, with congestion now hitting exports; Cathay Cargo has restricted Bangkok-bound freight at least through Nov 15, and Singapore Airlines Cargo has also flagged temporary limits. Forwarders say exposed goods include data-center and electronics equipment, semiconductor parts, medical devices and pharmaceuticals—shipments that normally clear in 24 hours now taking days or, in some cases, weeks.

### 10. LA–Long Beach watchmen contract extended to Nov 20, deferring a strike at the West Coast hub (Ports)
**Summary:** Reuters and port-industry outlets reported on Nov 9 that the Pacific Maritime Association and ILWU Local 26 extended the contract covering about 250 terminal watchmen through Nov 20 and will keep bargaining, with federal mediators assisting. Watchmen are only about 2% of the ILWU workforce at the two ports, but they work terminals that handle roughly 85% of Long Beach cargo and 33% of Los Angeles cargo; a strike could disrupt vessel, yard and gate operations because other ILWU crafts are not expected to cross picket lines. The prior agreement expired in July 2025 and has been kept alive through repeated short extensions; the U.S. Meat Export Federation has warned that a West Coast stoppage would hit channels carrying about two-thirds of waterborne U.S. beef exports and about half of waterborne pork exports.
