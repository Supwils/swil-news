## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Auto Tier 1s warn: AI is crowding out mature nodes; Aumovio says chip costs will “explode” (Auto semiconductors)
**Summary:** Automotive World reported on Nov 11 that the AI investment boom is creating a second automotive chip squeeze as foundries and packaging houses divert capacity from the mature-node commodity devices cars need toward higher-margin data-center products—a price-driven crunch, unlike the pandemic shortage, that automakers are poorly placed to win against AI buyers. Nexperia interim CEO Stefan Tilger and STMicroelectronics CEO Jean-Marc Chery had already flagged oversubscribed books and foundries “disengaging” from mainstream technology. Aumovio, which supplies sensors and in-vehicle electronics to customers including BYD, BMW and Ford, warned through interim finance head Claus Bauer that chip costs will “explode” next year, with “no price sensitivity whatsoever” in that segment and suppliers able to “sell twice their capacity to this very lucrative, non-price-sensitive area.” Industry analysis says the squeeze is likelier to show up as higher component costs, de-contented vehicles and delayed driver-assistance features than as 2021-style empty lots.

**Commentary:** For carmakers the issue is less whether chips exist than whether they still have any bidding power against AI premiums.

### 2. China’s copper fabricators squeezed: buy cathode only after orders; import and spot premiums at records (Base metals)
**Summary:** Briefs reported on Nov 11 that a surge in local copper prices is crimping cash flow and margins at Chinese plants that turn metal into wire, rod and tube. Ningbo Shimao Copper Industry sales engineer Zhang Benben said plants are largely doing “back-to-back” business and “only buy copper cathode to produce after we receive sales orders.” Import and spot premiums have hit records in recent weeks; LME copper was up about 0.8% at $13,788.50 a ton in morning Singapore trade. Refined copper shipments into China have weakened this year and domestic smelters have cut output as higher ore costs meet thin margins. Solar wire supplier Zhejiang Baichuan prepays for cathode while customers buy on credit, tightening cash cycles; copper-clad aluminum is being tested as a cheaper input, but adoption has not scaled without a national standard.

**Links:**

- [Briefs — China's copper squeeze is crimping fabricators and margins](https://www.briefs.co/news/china-s-copper-squeeze-is-crimping-fabricators-and-margins/)

**Commentary:** Copper tightness hits fabricators’ working-capital clocks first—orders before cathode means elasticity is locked by cash.

## II. Batteries & Materials

### 3. India bans battery “black mass” exports to keep recycling feedstock at home (Battery recycling)
**Summary:** Hindustan Times reported on Nov 11 that India’s Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) on Nov 10 prohibited exports of lithium-ion battery “black mass,” the powder from shredded end-of-life cells and manufacturing scrap that holds recoverable lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese. The ban covers unsorted waste and scrap of primary cells and accumulators that do not contain lead, cadmium or mercury. A commerce ministry spokesperson said the aim is to assure feedstock for domestic recyclers, cut import dependence and strengthen battery and clean-energy supply chains after Indian black mass was exported while local recyclers sometimes imported feedstock and underused capacity. The move aligns with the National Critical Mineral Mission and a Cabinet-approved incentive scheme of about 150 billion rupees from September 2025 for recycling capacity.

**Links:**

- [Hindustan Times — India bans export of battery ‘black mass’ to cut import dependence for EV, storage sector](https://www.hindustantimes.com/business/india-bans-export-of-battery-black-mass-to-cut-import-dependence-for-ev-storage-sector-101794397732288.html)

**Commentary:** Resource nationalism now reaches secondary minerals—whoever keeps the black mass is closer to upstream cell autonomy.

### 4. Ganfeng Lithium: battery-metal prices harder to predict as storage becomes a second demand pillar (Lithium)
**Summary:** Reuters via Investing.com reported on Nov 11 that Ganfeng Lithium president’s assistant Roy Zhang told a Shanghai conference that swings have grown more extreme and cycles shorter, with “an entire price cycle” compressed since the start of the year and no clear view of prices over the next 6–12 months. He said sentiment often dominates fundamentals on the Guangzhou Futures Exchange, while large batteries for energy storage have become a meaningful consumption pillar alongside EVs. Chinese lithium carbonate spot prices are about 23% lower year to date at roughly 91,500 yuan ($13,700) a ton after a May rally above 200,000 yuan. Zhang said demand is growing about 40% this year as supply catches up quickly, leaving a delicate balance where “even a small disruption” or one major mine failure could tip the market into shortage.

## III. Capacity, Feedstock & Relocation

### 5. Thailand’s SCG aims to restart Hormuz-hit Long Son plant in mid-2027 on U.S. ethane (Petrochemicals)
**Summary:** Nikkei Asia reported on Nov 11 that Siam Cement Group CEO Thammasak Sethaudom said the group’s core Long Son plastics/petrochemical complex in Vietnam, suspended after the Strait of Hormuz closure disrupted naphtha and related feedstocks, is targeted to reopen around mid-2027 as SCG uses the downtime to accelerate a switch from naphtha to imported U.S. ethane. Before the conflict, the Middle East supplied about half of naphtha and propane inputs; SCG aims to cut that share below 25%. Ethane is described as roughly 40% cheaper and as reducing supply-security and emissions risk. Vietnamese coverage the same day cited Long Son Petrochemicals saying operations paused from late May 2026, with a roughly $500 million retrofit underway and a Q2 2027 restart / end-Q3 ramp target. Thammasak said “no one can trust the Strait of Hormuz anymore.”

**Summary:** Business Standard reported around Nov 11 that Indian groups including Essar (via Mesabi Metallics’ roughly $18 billion integrated Iowa steel project), Reliance, Sun Pharma, JSW, Sterlite and Abhyuday have announced more than $40 billion of U.S. investments this year in steel, pharma, tech, refining and infrastructure—excluding separately disclosed projects such as Reliance’s Texas refinery venture—creating more than 11,000 planned U.S. jobs among firms that disclosed hiring. ### 7. Canadian textile mill plans U.S. production as tariff damage hits customer trust (Relocation)
**Summary:** The Canadian Press / BNN Bloomberg reported on Nov 11 that Toronto’s Fine Cotton Factory, with roughly half of sales tied to U.S. customers, saw orders delayed and inventories drawn down after tariffs of up to about 50% on some products. The firm is already shipping unfinished fabrics to U.S. contractors for further processing to cut finished-goods tariff exposure and plans to open a U.S. warehouse before sourcing equipment and starting production early next year. Sales EVP Skip Kann said even if tariffs are removed or a new trade deal is struck, the company will put “some form of our business in the U.S. as a long-term plan” so the shock “can never happen … again,” because customers may no longer “fully trust the supply chain out of Canada.” McMaster economist Pau Pujolás said fear of disruption alone can drive suboptimal investment choices; HEC Montréal’s Ari Van Assche said aggregate FDI has not yet surged south, but more firms feel they “cannot hold off for too long anymore.”

## IV. Trade, Logistics & Supply-Chain Finance

### 8. Indian garment makers cut U.S. dependence: Orient Craft’s U.S. share falls from about 84% to under 10% (Trade)
**Summary:** Reuters reported on Nov 11 from Ranchi that after U.S. tariffs rose as high as about 50% last year, Indian apparel exporters are reorienting toward Britain, Europe and domestic buyers. Orient Craft founder Sudhir Dhingra said even with lower duties and a possible trade pact he will not again depend so heavily on one market; U.S. sales now account for less than 10%, down from about 84% before the tariffs, on annual exports of roughly 1.5 billion rupees ($15.82 million). Gokaldas Exports, with more than 53,000 employees, aims to lift Europe’s revenue share to 25%–30% over three to five years from 15%–16%, cutting the U.S. share to 35%–40% from 50%–55%. Global Trade Research Institute data show April–August textile, apparel and home-furnishings exports to the U.S. fell 9.8% to $4.0 billion while shipments to other markets rose 3.7%.

### 9. GEP Volatility Index: North American safety stockpiling more than doubles month on month; oil and electronics still tight (Inventories)
**Summary:** GEP released its Global Supply Chain Volatility Index on Nov 11 (based on a monthly survey of about 27,000 businesses) showing manufacturers accelerated precautionary stockpiling in October against future inflation and supply disruptions. North America’s safety-stockpiling indicator more than doubled from September to a five-month high, while European inventory building rebounded sharply. Global transportation costs rose for a second straight month; material shortages stayed above their long-run average for an eighth month—though the least severe since March after the Middle East conflict— with oil, electronic and electrical components and metals widely cited. Asia’s index rose to 1.67 from 1.49, the region’s strongest pressure in four months; North America was 1.03 and Europe 0.78. GEP consulting VP John Piatek warned that buffer buying supports activity now but, once stocks are built, slower purchasing could drag manufacturing and the wider economy in 2027.

### 10. DP World: more than 1 million containers rerouted overland since Hormuz closed; 3,000-truck fleet built (Logistics)
**Summary:** AGBI reported on Nov 11 that DP World Middle East & Africa CEO Ahmad Yousef Al-Hassan said the ports group has moved more than 1 million containers across the UAE on alternative road and rail routes since the Strait of Hormuz closed, building a roughly 3,000-truck fleet from scratch. Bonded corridors link Fujairah and Khor Fakkan on the east coast with Jebel Ali, with similar links to Sohar and Duqm in Oman. Jebel Ali volumes fell 90%–100% at peak periods; the port was fully closed for only about six hours during the conflict. In July DP World announced an AED2.5 billion ($681 million) plan for two Fujairah terminals, with Al Rugaylat sized for up to 2.5 million TEU a year; first-half profit fell about 39% to $585 million.

**Links:**

### 11. China’s manufacturing price wars stretch payment terms and squeeze supplier finance (Supply-chain finance)
**Summary:** The Diplomat analyzed on Nov 11 how cut-throat Chinese manufacturing competition can shift financing costs onto smaller suppliers through delayed payments. An Economic Observer review of 14 automakers found average accounts-payable and notes-payable turnover lengthened from about 164.4 days at end-2025 to 187.5 days in the first half of 2026; CAAM’s invoice-to-payment measure showed an average of about 54 days (different methodology). The PBOC has acknowledged large firms delaying payments and forcing smaller suppliers to borrow; outstanding electronic payment certificates peaked near 3 trillion yuan and were still about 2.4 trillion yuan by July 2026 with average periods around 92 days. Large industrial firms held about 29.77 trillion yuan in receivables at end-September 2026, up about 10% year on year, with average collection near 72 days. Beijing has issued auto payment rules and broader State Council guidance, but enforcement remains hard in hyper-competitive sectors.

**Links:**

- [The Diplomat — Who Pays for China’s Manufacturing Price Wars?](https://thediplomat.com/2026/11/who-pays-for-chinas-manufacturing-price-wars/)

**Commentary:** The hidden losers of price wars are suppliers’ balance sheets—capacity without cash stalls both expansion and innovation.
