## I. Chips & Critical Materials

### 1. Advanced packaging substrates become the new bottleneck: Intel locks ~$5.3–6.1B; Samsung Electro-Mechanics expansion still short (Substrates)
**Summary:** Korea JoongAng Daily reported on Nov 7 that advanced packaging substrates have become a new AI-chip supply-chain bottleneck. In its third-quarter SEC filing, Intel said packaging-substrate shortages have kept it from fully meeting data-center CPU demand, and it expects constraints on substrates and memory to persist into next year. After the third quarter, Intel signed two substrate supply agreements with estimated financial commitments of about $5.3–6.1 billion, of which roughly $3.3 billion is to be paid by the end of 2027. The squeeze reflects larger, more multi-layer substrates needed as AI server CPUs/GPUs grow more powerful—capacity that is hard and slow to add. Samsung Electro-Mechanics said in September it would invest a total of about 6.78 trillion won (~$5 billion) in FC-BGA capacity—about 4.3 trillion won in Sejong and 2.5 trillion won in Vietnam—targeting mass production in September 2028 and June 2030, respectively; on its earnings call it still said planned expansion would not meet all customer demand. LG Innotek is also pushing oversized AI-accelerator substrates, aiming for mass production for training/inference around 2027 and server substrates by 2028.

**Commentary:** After packaging, the next choke point is substrates—customer-funded capacity locks are spreading from CoWoS to FC-BGA.

### 2. TSMC reportedly weighs 3D DRAM at Nvidia’s request, adding a foundry variable to memory supply (Memory)
**Summary:** Seoul Economic Daily’s English site reported on Nov 7, citing The Information, that TSMC has begun reviewing plans to produce next-generation 3D DRAM at the request of its largest customer, Nvidia, including options to build a new memory plant or acquire and convert an existing facility; no decisions have been made on investment, scale, or timing. The backdrop is an intensifying shortage of high-performance memory for AI compute, with Nvidia seeking to diversify memory sources. 3D DRAM stacks memory cells vertically to raise density and differs structurally from HBM, which stacks DRAM chips to boost bandwidth. If TSMC enters memory at scale, it could add another controlled link beyond GPU foundry and advanced packaging, posing a medium- to long-term challenge to Samsung and SK hynix’s memory dominance—though heat, process integration, and yields remain open issues that could take years, and Korean peers plus Micron are also developing the technology.

**Links:**

- [Seoul Economic Daily — TSMC Weighs 3D DRAM Push at Nvidia's Request](https://en.sedaily.com/finance/2026/11/07/tsmc-weighs-3d-dram-push-at-nvidias-request)

**Commentary:** Customers are pushing foundries toward vertical integration—the memory bottleneck is pulling TSMC onto a track it long avoided.

### 3. Micron effectively halts Taoyuan HBM/advanced DRAM plant work amid bonus dispute; ~$3–4.5B investment stalled (Capacity)
**Summary:** Seoul Economic Daily’s English site reported on Nov 7 that Micron has effectively halted construction of Fab 11E in Taoyuan, Taiwan—ground broken in July—after a dispute with the local union over wages and performance bonuses, and has informed Taiwanese authorities and partners. The company had planned about $3–4.5 billion of investment in HBM and advanced DRAM capacity aimed at supplying Nvidia and others from around 2028. A wage deal was reached with the Taichung union, but the Taoyuan union—citing Samsung and SK hynix—is demanding that about 15% of operating profit be shared with employees worldwide and that bonuses be paid mainly in cash, stalling talks. Taiwan accounts for roughly 60% of Micron’s DRAM output, raising discussion of whether the company may rethink its Taiwan-centered production strategy.

**Links:**

- [Seoul Economic Daily — Micron Halts Taiwan HBM Plant Work Amid Bonus Dispute](https://en.sedaily.com/finance/2026/11/07/micron-halts-taiwan-hbm-plant-work-amid-bonus-dispute)

**Commentary:** HBM expansion is not only about tools and substrates—labor contracts can push 2028 supply timelines back on their own.

### 4. Trafigura steps up Asian copper buying as China spot premiums hit records; SHFE stocks under a day’s use (Copper)
**Summary:** Bloomberg reported on Nov 7 that Trafigura has spent the past two weeks approaching Asian producers and traders for large November–December refined-copper volumes to feed Asian sales, adding to a scramble that has pushed regional spot premiums to records. Shanghai Metals Market data show Shanghai spot premiums roughly tripling in two weeks to a record about 2,615 yuan/mt (~$392/mt); Fastmarkets said import premiums rose to about $195–210/mt, the highest in data going back to about 2017. SHFE deliverable inventories stood near 33,848 tons—close to a 2022 multiyear low and less than a day of China’s copper use. Chinese smelters face tight concentrate and scrap inputs that have slowed refined output, while flows in recent years have preferred the U.S., drawing down Chinese stocks. A company spokesperson declined to comment.

### 5. Rare earths: U.S. says China has met ~two-thirds of pledges; MOFCOM pause still set to Nov 10 without a published extension (Rare earths)
**Summary:** East Asia Brief reported on Nov 7 that U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pressed Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng in a Nov 6 video call to accelerate critical-mineral shipments, saying Beijing had fulfilled only about two-thirds of bilateral rare-earth export commitments, with spot tightness in heavy elements including yttrium, dysprosium, and terbium. China’s partial export-control suspension under Ministry of Commerce Announcement No. 70 remains legally set through Nov 10, 2026; although broader U.S.–China trade messaging points to a truce toward about Jan 10, 2027, MOFCOM has not published a formal notice amending the Nov 10 expiry. Separately, NDRC and other agencies recently released a resource-conservation action plan targeting about a 10% cut in energy intensity per unit of GDP by 2030 versus 2025, embedding mineral quotas deeper in domestic regulation. Domestic magnet makers such as JL MAG continue to expand, while overseas plants remain more exposed to licensing windows.

**Links:**

- [East Asia Brief — US presses China on rare earth shipments ahead of Nov. 10 deadline](https://eastasiabrief.com/energy-minerals/us-presses-china-rare-earth-shipments-ahead-nov-10-1209)

## II. Capacity, Manufacturing & Migration

### 6. SoftBank and ABB explore China carve-out to salvage ~$5.4B robotics deal (Robotics)
**Summary:** Bloomberg reported on Nov 7 (relayed by Reuters and others) that SoftBank and ABB are exploring carving the China portion of ABB’s robotics business into a separately owned entity after China’s Office of the Working Mechanism for Foreign Investment Security Review halted SoftBank’s roughly $5.4 billion acquisition last Thursday. ABB’s Nov 5 official update confirmed SoftBank informed it that the China robotics acquisition was prohibited and that both sides are working constructively. China is the division’s largest single market—about 30% of robotics—and hosts roughly 30 local companies with about 15,000 employees across nearly 130 cities. Talks remain early and may not succeed; ABB is also weighing other buyers (potentially valuing the business around $2–3 billion) or reviving a spin-off/IPO. The case is another frontier-technology deal blocked by China this year after Meta/Manus.

### 7. Malaysia eases raw REE export moratorium on conditions, trading feedstock for three-year processing-plant pledges (Rare earths / Policy)
**Summary:** The Star reported on Nov 7 that Investment, Trade and Industry Minister Johari Abdul Ghani said Malaysia will provide limited flexibility under its raw rare-earth export moratorium to attract investors and build domestic processing. The easing is limited to R&D uses, and approved exporters must commit to developing processing facilities in Malaysia within three years of approval. Johari said potential investors have been reluctant without a stable raw-material supply; policy priority is midstream and downstream—especially permanent magnets, advanced materials, electronic components, and batteries—via partnerships and technology transfer across the chain. The same reply addressed a roughly $96 million Lynas–U.S. rare-earth oxide supply arrangement as a commercial matter, with oxides intended for U.S. permanent-magnet manufacturing R&D exploration.

## III. Logistics, Trade & Policy

### 8. India DGFT consults exporters on inland freight and container-crunch relief as West Asia shipping stretches (Logistics)
**Summary:** The Hindu BusinessLine reported on Nov 7 that India’s government is consulting exporters on support measures for rising inland transport costs and shipping-container shortages. The Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) has sought industry input on eligibility and implementation. Drivers include West Asia conflict disruptions that have pushed some vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, delaying container returns to Indian ports, plus transshipment congestion and schedule disruption. Stronger exports add to equipment demand: September 2026 merchandise exports rose about 23% year on year to about $44.40 billion, and April–September cumulative exports rose about 18.51% to about $259.89 billion, led by electronics, engineering goods, and petroleum products. Exporters say freight hikes, erratic container availability, and depot charges are eroding margins, while longer transit times and blank sailings raise inventory costs at factory gates.
